# Jobs theory, as we use it: notes from Competing Against Luck

Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan, 2016. Read in full on 29 Sep 2026. These are our working notes in our own words, not a summary of the book. They set the rules for the second jobs list (`BRIEF-v2.md`).

## What a job is

- A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. Not a need, not a wish, not a product. A need ("I need to sell more") is always true and points nowhere. A job names the moment: "It is Monday, ad spend is up 20 percent and revenue is flat, and I have to decide by noon whether to cut or hold."
- The circumstance is the unit of work. Where they are, what just happened, who is with them, what they must do next, what pressure they are under. Change the circumstance and the job changes, even for the same person.
- Every job has three dimensions. Functional (get the number right). Emotional (stop worrying, feel in control, avoid looking foolish). Social (look competent to a partner, a client, an investor, the team). The emotional and social parts often decide the hire.
- Jobs are stable. Solutions change. The owner's job of "know whether ads are making money" is the same whether it is done with a spreadsheet, an agency or a screen. We discover jobs, we do not invent them.
- A job is written in verbs and nouns. "Convenience", "clarity" and "confidence" are experiences a solution gives, not jobs.
- Right level of abstraction test: the things a person could hire to do the job must come from different categories. If the only things that could do it are other screens like ours, it is a feature spec, not a job. If they could hire an agency, a freelancer, a spreadsheet, their own gut, a phone call to a friend, or do nothing, it is a job.

## Hire and fire

- People hire a solution when a job arises, and fire it when it does a poor job. The question that matters: what job did you hire it to do?
- Big hire: the moment they choose the product. Little hire: every moment they use it again. Most data tracks the big hire. Only the little hires prove the job is being done. For us: the founder opening a screen on a Monday morning is the little hire. If they stop opening it, the screen is fired, whatever the contract says.
- Something always has to be fired. Before a screen is hired, the founder fires an agency report, a spreadsheet, a weekly call, a gut feel, or the habit of not looking. Innovators forget this. We must name what gets fired.
- Nonconsumption is the biggest competitor. Many owners do nothing about a job because every solution is too expensive, too slow or too embarrassing. A screen that turns nonconsumption into consumption competes with nothing.

## The four forces at the moment of choice

- Push of the situation: the frustration is big enough to act on.
- Pull of the new solution: it promises real progress.
- Habit of the present: "I am used to doing it this way."
- Anxiety of the new: "What if it is wrong? What if I look stupid? What if it costs more?"
- The pull must beat habit plus anxiety. Design the experiences that reduce anxiety: a practice run, a no-risk trial, a person to call, a visible track record. Most products only work on pull.

## How to see a job

- Make a mini documentary of one person in the moment of struggle. Sample size of one, in depth. Stories, not statistics.
- Five questions: what progress do they want; what are the circumstances; what obstacles block them; what workarounds or compensating behaviours are they using; what would "good" mean and what trade-offs would they accept.
- Where jobs hide: your own life; nonconsumption; workarounds and cobbled-together fixes; things people do not want to do (negative jobs); unusual uses of what exists.
- Customers cannot tell you what they want. They can tell you their struggle. Watch what they do, not what they say.

## From job to solution

- Job spec: the functional, emotional and social progress; the trade-offs accepted; the full set of competing solutions to beat, including doing nothing; the obstacles and anxieties to remove. The solution then includes the experiences of buying and using it, not just the thing.
- Products that nail a job become services in the customer's life: they take complexity off the customer's desk and move it into the provider's process. That is what an agency is hired for. That is what a screen must do.
- Competitive advantage comes from processes that deliver the experiences every time, not from features. Features get copied; integrated processes do not.
- Purpose brand: when a name becomes the answer to a job ("when I need to furnish a place today, IKEA"). Each screen should be able to finish the sentence "When [circumstance], I open [screen] to [progress]."
- Say who should not hire it. A product hired for the wrong job gets fired and reviewed badly.

## Measure the job, not the machine

- Measure what matters to the customer's progress, not what is easy to pull from the server. Southern New Hampshire University measures minutes to call back a prospect, not brochures mailed. Amazon measures when the parcel arrived, not when it shipped.
- Three data fallacies once a product is live: active data (sales, funnels, product stats) drowns out passive data (the context of the struggle); surface growth (sell more things to the same customers) pulls focus off the core job; conforming data (numbers that agree with what we already believe). Someone has to be the voice of the job.
- Efficiency is only valuable inside a process that does the customer's job. Getting better at the wrong thing feels like progress.

## What this changes in our lists

- A job is written from inside a moment, with the emotional and social weight visible, not as a capability the screen has.
- Every job names what the owner hires today and what must be fired.
- Every job names the anxiety a screen must remove before the owner will trust it.
- The metric to watch is the owner's progress, the way they would check it, before it is the platform's number.
- Jobs where the owner currently does nothing (nonconsumption) rank higher than the score alone would put them.
- Every screen gets a purpose line and a "not for" line.
