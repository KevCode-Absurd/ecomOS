## What wave 1 missed most

Wave 1 is strong on flows, offers and CX design but was written from the inside (what a lifecycle program should contain) rather than from the tool market. The clearest gaps: (1) identity resolution / unified customer profile as its own build step, assumed rather than specified; (2) governing an actual AI support agent's autonomous actions (Agent Operating Procedures, escalation, replay-testing before go-live) as distinct from writing reply templates; (3) reviews and reputation off the brand's own site : Google, marketplaces, complaint boards, and syndicating reviews back into ads and feeds; (4) tax and privacy compliance seams landing on the same team as marketing, neither of which is a marketing task: US sales-tax handling on refunds and CCPA/state-privacy erasure requests by default; India only, GST credit notes on refunds and DPDP Act erasure requests instead; (5) voice/IVR as a channel, absent from the SMS/WhatsApp/push scope; (6) a customer community as a retention surface, not covered at all; (7) marketplace seller-support (Amazon/Walmart Marketplace, or Flipkart in India) running on its own clock, separate from the brand's own helpdesk.

## What is possible but not sold yet

Four records marked `possible`: a per-customer, evidence-based win-back draft instead of one shared template (the data exists in support/review/order history, no vendor personalises to this depth); true customer profitability (support cost, return cost, discount cost against revenue) feeding loyalty tiering, instead of revenue-only RFM; watching named competitors' returns/loyalty/subscription policy pages for material changes over time; and browser-driven rescheduling of failed deliveries (a carrier return-to-sender for US brands, a COD NDR for India) through a carrier's own portal when no API integration exists, which is exactly the gap between what API-integrated carriers cover (ShipStation/EasyPost for the US, Shipway/ClickPost for India) and what a smaller brand does by hand today.
