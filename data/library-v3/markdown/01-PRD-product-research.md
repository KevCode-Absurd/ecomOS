## Baseline product-research checklist before any tool
Use when: Screening a candidate product or deciding whether a product found via ad spy, Instagram or TikTok is worth further analysis.
- Require a gross margin of at least $30–$40 above supplier cost (the source uses this as the margin floor, not a percentage).
- Confirm the product solves a clear problem or relieves an insecurity: pain relief, home fitness, hygiene, convenience. Products that only ride a viral trend with no real utility are the ones the source says beginners lose money on.
- Check that user-generated content already exists for the product (search TikTok) so you have raw footage for ads and site assets; you can download and re-edit clips, and the source advises against posting someone else's video untouched.
- Avoid heavy or oversized items, which create shipping and logistics problems the source calls a nightmare.
- Sanity-check that the product is not purely 'junk' from a trend: the source's contrast case is a music projector and a gym-bag product versus a $25 hand massager sold at $80 and a $40 Pilates set sold at $120.
- Score each candidate against all four criteria in a Google Sheet alongside buy price, intended sale price and competitor notes before spending on ads.
Caveat: The $30–$40 margin figure is the source's rule of thumb for dropshipping, not a universal benchmark; lower-ticket or higher-repeat purchase models may differ.
Caveat: The source's largest example (a Pilates set bought at $40, sold at $120 with a free guide) is a personal result, not a guarantee.

## Three fastest ways to fail at product selection
Use when: Stress-testing a product idea before committing budget, or diagnosing why previous tests failed.
- Do not sell what you personally like — the source says if that worked he'd be selling golf gear and fish pictures. Personal taste is not demand evidence.
- Do not take the 'copy a competitor and make it slightly better' route as a beginner; the source calls it the oldest trick in the book and says it does not work for newcomers.
- Reject products with no existing online content. Content availability is a hard requirement because you need raw video to build ads and product pages.
- Look instead for something untapped: un-niched utility products for beginners, or a saturated product with an unclaimed buyer segment.
- Treat the presence of many competitors as a demand signal, not an automatic veto — but only if you have a concrete plan to reach a different audience (see Blue Ocean angle pivoting).
Caveat: The source's claim that he now succeeds with roughly one of every two products tested is a personal anecdote, not an expected hit rate.

## Random, un-niched products as the beginner entry path
Use when: Choosing a first product or first test when you have little experience and no brand equity.
- Pick products that belong to no obvious niche — the source's examples are a portable bubble bath (cost ~$40, sell ~$90) and a shoe cleaner/sterilizer that dries shoes in about 30 seconds.
- Deliberately skip crowded verticals: no generic dog products, no mass-market beauty aimed at women unless the product itself is new and unique.
- Expect to be crushed by competition or pay far more per click in those crowded niches as a beginner.
- Verify the candidate still clears the baseline checklist (margin, problem, content, shipping weight) — 'random' means un-niched, not low-quality.
- Treat this as the easiest on-ramp, not a permanent rule: the source explicitly says it is 'by no means a must-do' and later shows how to compete in saturated categories.
Caveat: The source frames this as a beginner convenience strategy; experienced operators can compete directly and the source moves on to Blue Ocean pivoting for them.

## Meta Ad Library method: find dropshippers via generic keywords
Use when: Starting product research with no paid tools, or validating whether other stores are currently advertising a product.
- Open the Meta Ad Library and search generic dropshipping terms such as 'free shipping' or '50% off'; these attract dropshipping stores rather than brand advertisers.
- Apply the Active ads filter, and optionally restrict to ads launched in the last one to two weeks to bias toward new tests.
- Scroll the results and screen each product against the baseline checklist (margin, problem, content, weight); the source rejects low-margin jewelry but flags an eye-bag/under-eye product bought around $30 and sold at €69 (~$75) as a real candidate.
- Take the product name and search it again in the ad library to count distinct stores currently running it.
- Proceed if fewer than five stores are running ads on it in the last seven days; fewer than three is the source's even-better signal.
- Repeat with vertical keywords like 'pain relief' to surface problem-solving products rather than novelty items.
Caveat: Fewer than five active advertisers in the last week is the source's threshold, not a statistical guarantee of success.
Caveat: Ad Library counts can be noisy; verify the stores are actually distinct and currently active rather than abandoned pages.

## Instagram Reels feed-training method for product discovery
Use when: Daily passive product hunting, especially when you want product ideas to come to you rather than searching manually.
- Create a brand-new Instagram account so your existing feed data does not pollute the recommendations.
- Go to search and look up #winningproduct, then like anywhere from five to ten videos that clearly show physical products, including obvious dropshipping ads.
- Return to the Reels tab (the source says this works even better on the mobile app) so the algorithm starts serving dropshipping product ads.

- For each promising product, check AliExpress for the sourcing price (example given: a period-cramp belt bought at $17 and sold at $59), then log it in your research sheet.
- Repeat over multiple sessions; the source claims three to five serious candidates within about 30 minutes once the feed is trained.

## Daily research log and 3-to-1-to-1 selection funnel
Use when: Setting up a repeatable daily product-research routine and deciding what to actually launch.
- Create a Google Sheet for researched products and record: product name, buy price, intended sell price, competitor pages to revisit, and free-text notes (e.g. 'seen little on Facebook but heavy on TikTok — untapped').

## Blue Ocean logic: saturated means demand, not dead
Use when: When your best-margin candidates all look crowded and you are tempted to abandon them or compete head-on.
- Reframe saturation as the presence of proven demand — people already want the product, which removes the risk of teaching the market from scratch.
- Instead of competing for the same buyer, hunt for an untargeted sub-demographic or an untargeted geographic market.
- Do not try to reinvent the wheel: the source's rule is that you only need to do something nobody else is doing with an otherwise proven product.
- Accept a smaller market if you can own it — the source's fishing analogy contrasts three boats fighting over a $20M market with one boat alone on a $2M market.
- Treat the second half of product research as thinking about audience, not tooling: the source attributes roughly 50% of the game to finding products and 50% to how you think about marketing them.
Caveat: The '100% success rate' claim for geo-arbitrage into untapped European markets is the presenter's assertion, not measured evidence — smaller or unproven markets carry their own risk.
Caveat: Blue Ocean repositioning only works when the product genuinely suits the new audience; forcing an ill-fitting avatar wastes budget.

## Geo-arbitrage: move proven US/UK products into untapped markets
Use when: You have a proven product with strong English-language ad evidence and want lower competition than the source market.
- Identify products with heavy existing US/UK ad volume as proof of demand, not as a place to compete.
- Choose a market where the same product has not been advertised (the source cites untapped European countries as his example).
- Assume consumer preferences transfer reasonably across similar markets — the source argues people 'like the same products' but simply have not been shown them.
- Localise creative and copy to the target market's language and context before launching.
- Verify local shipping costs, delivery times and import duties, since these can erase the margin advantage of lower competition.
Caveat: The source's confidence in cross-market preference transfer is an opinion; the 'pretty much 100% success rate' phrasing is a claim, not a benchmark.
Caveat: Logistics, VAT/import rules and payment availability vary by country and must be checked before committing.

## Angle pivoting: rebuild a saturated product around a new avatar
Use when: When a product is validated but competing ads all speak to one generic audience, and you need a differentiated campaign.
- Search the product on TikTok and catalogue the audiences current advertisers target — the source's shoe-dryer example shows everyone selling it generically, with one advertiser targeting football parents.
- List alternative avatars the product genuinely serves; for the shoe dryer: parents of kids who get shoes dirty, campers and outdoor enthusiasts with wet gear, and fitness players with smelly shoes.
- For a home-fitness product, note that almost all advertisers target young women doing short workouts; the source's untapped angles are older people with stiff joints who won't commit to a gym, and slightly overweight buyers trying to regain control of their life.
- Write the ad hook and landing copy around the new avatar's specific pain and desired outcome rather than the product's generic function.
- Check that the chosen avatar is a small share of existing advertiser spend (the source cites about 10% for the weight-loss angle) — low share plus real need is the signal.
- Keep the product and offer otherwise unchanged so the pivot is a positioning change, not a new sourcing project.
Caveat: Visual examples in the source are specific to shoe dryers and home-fitness equipment; the technique generalises but the avatars do not.
Caveat: Older or lower-tech audiences can require different creative formats and checkout assumptions than the platforms' default dropshipping audience.

## Verify blue oceans by auditing what competitors are not doing
Use when: Systematically generating differentiated angles for any saturated product you are considering.
- Step one: confirm the product solves a real problem and clears the baseline margin, content and shipping criteria.
- Step two: research the product's competitors — collect their ads, landing pages and targeting signals, then write down explicitly what they are not doing: which customer type, life circumstance or outcome they never mention.
- Look for angles that are visibly rare in the ad set rather than completely absent, since a small amount of validation reduces the risk of an angle nobody wants.
- Position your whole campaign on the gap — avatar, hook, imagery, proof — rather than adding it as a secondary message.
- Repeat the audit whenever entering a new category; the source frames this as thinking rather than automating, and argues most dropshippers do not do it.
Caveat: 'Do something nobody's doing' is the source's maxim and a direction of travel, not evidence that any untaken angle will convert.

## Target Massive Stagnant Categories Instead of Educating Consumers
Use when: Choosing which product category to enter or reinvent, especially when deciding between a novel category and an established commodity.
- Scan for categories that are already massive and well-understood so you never have to teach the customer what the product is or why they need it.
- Within those categories, hunt for a 'sea of sameness' — every incumbent uses near-identical packaging, color, form and voice, and the category takes itself too seriously.
- Eric Ryan's examples: dish soap where everything is green and framed as toxic, and vitamins that are all round and pseudo-scientific.
- Treat sameness as the opportunity: incumbents have trained the consumer on function but left the emotional and aesthetic space empty.
- Tie the entry decision to your ability to visibly break that pattern rather than to incremental function claims.
Caveat: Choosing a huge category means competing against entrenched brands and shelf space; the framework says nothing about distribution or capital requirements, which sit outside product-research scope.
Caveat: A 'sea of sameness' observation is qualitative; validate consumer boredom or dissatisfaction before committing.

## Force-Apply Outside Cultural Shifts to Create Creative Tension
Use when: Developing the core concept or brand world for a commodity product that needs to feel culturally current rather than functionally new.
- Map macro shifts happening outside your category — behaviors, values and rituals consumers already care about. Ryan's example is boutique fitness being treated as a spiritual pursuit.
- Take one of those shifts and force-apply it to the dormant category — e.g., bring that fitness-as-spirituality energy into daily vitamins.
- Aim for the 'intersection of familiar and novel': the product's function stays instantly recognizable while the cultural framing is new.
- Score the concept by how much tension it creates between what the category expects and what you present.
- Use the shift to write the brand's belief and language, not just the packaging — the tension should be legible across the whole experience.
Caveat: The source presents this as a qualitative creative device, not a tested growth lever; no performance benchmark is claimed.
Caveat: Tension that is too strong drifts into novelty for its own sake — pairing this with the one-iteration rule keeps adoption viable.

## The One Iteration Rule: Change Exactly One Product Variable
Use when: Deciding how far to deviate from a familiar product format, scent, form, function or price point during concept design.
- List the core expectations the category has trained consumers on — form factor, function, color, scent, ritual, packaging format.
- Choose exactly ONE of those to break, and keep everything else within expectation.
- Ryan's rule: if you change two or three variables simultaneously, the product becomes 'too novel' and breaks consumer adoption.
- Treat additional changes as a roadmap for later iterations rather than launch features.
- When testing the concept, check whether the single change reads as surprising-but-legible; if consumers cannot quickly place it, reduce novelty further.
Caveat: This is a cognitive-adoption heuristic attributed to Eric Ryan, not an experimentally derived threshold; some categories and audiences tolerate more change.
Caveat: What counts as 'one' variable is subjective — form and packaging can be perceived as one aesthetic change or two.

## Cross-Industry Aesthetic Theft to Force Differentiation
Use when: Generating packaging and brand-visual direction for a category where all incumbents share the same visual language.
- Define the visual clichés of your category so you know exactly what to avoid.
- Deliberately source aesthetic references from industries with no adjacency — Ryan's example is stealing high-end houseware vase shapes and applying them to dish soap packaging.

- Apply the stolen architecture to structure, silhouette and materials, not just surface graphics, so the difference survives at shelf and in thumbnails.
- Check that the borrowed language still communicates the category function at a glance; theft should shift perception, not obscure what the product is.
Caveat: Distant references must be translated, not copied, to avoid looking mismatched or derivative of the borrowed industry.

## Iterate One Variable on a Proven Category
Use when: Choosing which product or category to build in, or deciding how bold a repositioning should be
- Target well-established, easy-to-understand categories rather than inventing a category from scratch: you avoid driving consumer education for something wildly new.
- Run the 'one iteration' rule: change exactly ONE major variable from the core product expectation (e.g., package shape OR benefit framing OR format).
- Avoid changing two or three things at once — that pushes the product into 'too novel,' breaking consumer adoption.
- Diagnose failed launches by counting changes: serial founder Eric Ryan says he fails more often from being too novel than too familiar.
- For an underperforming pitch or launch, strip it to the single most convincing differentiator instead of stacking four; audiences drop multiple simultaneous changes the way they drop multiple thrown eggs.
Caveat: Ryan applies this beyond consumer goods (even B2B), but notes he knows consumer better; treat cross-domain claims as his opinion.
Caveat: One-variable discipline is a probability argument, not a guarantee.

## Cross-Industry Theft to Create Creative Tension
Use when: Generating distinct product, packaging or brand directions in a saturated category
- Steal from as far away as possible rather than copying competitors — from personal care into home care, or housewares into dish soap.
- Ask the core question 'what if this, but for that?' to bridge two disparate domains.

## Design for the Familiar-Novel Intersection
Use when: Evaluating whether a concept is differentiated enough to matter yet easy enough to adopt
- Test the concept on two axes: if it's too familiar it lacks differentiation; if it's too novel it feels foreign and is harder to get someone to try.

- Use familiarity anchors consumers already trust — e.g., fiber as a benefit people already believe is good for them — and add novelty at the brand/product level.
- Accept that finding the intersection is 'easy to say, hard to find' — it is the art of the process, so generate several candidate bridges rather than assuming the first works.

## Simplify Instead of Demonstrating Sophistication

## Translate Benefits on Packaging, Not Features

## Time to Fun (TTF): the onboarding metric that predicts retention
Use when: Designing onboarding, first session, registration flow, or first-purchase experience for a product or store.
- Define the earliest moment a new customer genuinely experiences the product's core pleasure. In mobile games this is called TTF (time to fun) — the gap between tapping to open the app and the first moment of delight.
- Audit your current flow and count every step between arrival and that moment: registration, email capture, phone number, push-notification opt-in, tutorials. The source's example: a game that demanded sign-up and permissions before the player had tried anything had effectively no time to fun at all.
- Compress the path so the core pleasure arrives first and the data collection or account creation comes after. Mario's first 15 seconds teach all the controls, let you defeat the easiest enemy, earn a coin, and grow bigger — no tutorial.
- Map the concept to your own channel: for ecommerce the equivalent moment might be seeing a hero product in use, receiving a sample, or landing on a page that immediately communicates the product's payoff.
- Set a target TTF and treat every element that doesn't shorten it as a candidate for removal or deferral.
- Recognize that reducing TTF changes perceived wait time as well as actual wait time — a small early payoff makes a long experience feel shorter.
Caveat: The source is a podcast conversation, not a tested ecommerce benchmark; it offers no numeric target for TTF in commerce.
Caveat: Removing registration friction must be balanced against whatever data the business genuinely needs — the claim is that fun should come first, not that accounts never matter.

## Inverse international expansion sequence to dodge US ad auction saturation

- Treat the US last because its Meta ad auctions are the most competitive and expensive; a brand entering cold faces established incumbents bidding up the same audiences.
- Use the earlier markets as proving grounds: each launch should produce conversion data, creative learnings and social proof that makes the eventual US entry stronger.
- Confirm the strategy is viable for your situation before committing: this sequence assumes sufficient capital to run several launches and a product that travels across English-speaking markets with minimal localisation.
- Validate demand independently in each new market rather than assuming spillover from the previous one; auction dynamics, competitors and buyer behaviour differ by country.
Caveat: This is one operator's stated sequence from a podcast interview, not a universal rule; the source qualifies it as conditional on brand state and available capital.

## The Two Golden Margin Rules: 3x COGS and $30 Gross Margin
Use when: Deciding whether a sourced product can support paid acquisition before committing to a test.
- Rule one: price at minimum 3x your landed cost of goods sold (sourced plus shipped). If it costs $15 landed, sell at $45 or more.
- Rule two: keep at least $30 of gross profit per unit. The presenter says he used to use $20 and raised it because Facebook ad costs keep increasing.
- Check both rules at once. Example: source for $15, sell for $50 gives a $35 gross margin — this satisfies both the 3x multiplier and the $30 floor.
- Account for the fact that established competitors have negotiated better supplier rates than a new dropshipping brand, so do not assume you can match their cost base.
Caveat: These are the presenter's rules of thumb for a specific ad-cost environment, not universal economics.
Caveat: 3x COGS with a $30 floor can conflict at low price points — a $12 product gives 3.6x but only ~$36 margin, while a $10 product at 3x yields only $20 margin and fails rule two.
Caveat: Margins must also cover shipping, payment fees and refunds; the rules as stated only address ad costs.

## Ad Failure KPIs: CPC, CTR and CPM Thresholds
Use when: A US product test is unprofitable and you need to determine whether the ads, not the offer or product, are the constraint.

## Funnel Failure KPIs: ATC Ratio, Checkout Completion and AOV
Use when: Ad metrics look acceptable but the product still isn't converting, or you need to locate friction between click and purchase.
- Add-to-cart ratio: of everyone who lands on the first page (advertorial, pre-sell page, or PDP), at least 10% should add to cart. This is somewhat price-dependent — for more expensive products 8–9% can be acceptable. Below that, the funnel or the traffic quality is failing.
- ATC-to-purchase ratio: aim for 30% completion, so 3 of every 10 add-to-carts purchase. Below 30% points to friction between cart and purchase — check payment-processor approval rates and simplify the checkout. The presenter favors removing steps over adding conversion bumpers.
- AOV: on a straight Shopify setup, target AOV 20–40% above the base selling price (he suggests pushing even 50% above for a one-product store). A low AOV usually means missing upsells or downsells on the first purchase.
- LTV: for subscription or retention-heavy models, LTV should be substantially higher than AOV. A low LTV means you acquire customers reasonably well but don't earn enough from them over time.
Caveat: These are the presenter's working benchmarks, not validated universal thresholds; they vary with price point and model.
Caveat: ATC and checkout figures can be distorted by bot traffic or by traffic quality from the ad itself, so read the ad and funnel metrics together.
