## Run the 3x3 loop: Commit, Check-in, Close for ecommerce teams
Use when: Standing up or fixing the team structure behind a store's merchandising, content, ads or fulfillment operations.
- Step 1 — Commit: hold an explicit conversation about three foundations: goals (what we want to achieve), roles (who does what), and norms (how we share information and make decisions). The source stresses these must be explicit and openly discussed, not assumed.
- Step 2 — Check-in: on a recurring basis, revisit those commitments: are we still behind these goals, and does our working style still hold? Mario Musa calls the natural tendency 'drift' — teams get pulled by multiple projects and responsibilities, so goals/roles/norms decay without revisits.
- Step 3 — Close: identify the 'saying-doing gap' between stated intentions and actual behavior, then close it in small, specific steps rather than grand overhauls.
- Pay attention to the environment around each change and adjust it so the small steps are supported, and be a 'realistic optimist' — think ahead about what will get in the way instead of assuming plans will just work.
- Repeat the loop continuously; the source frames it as iterative, not a one-time onboarding event. Ecommerce example: after a catalogue-wide launch has drifted, re-convene, restate the goal (say, conversion on the top 20 SKUs), re-confirm who owns PDP content vs. ad creative vs. inventory, and agree one small test per week.
Caveat: No fixed ratio of check-in time to working time emerged — Mario explicitly says 'it depends' on the team's appetite for process; the important point is that check-ins happen at all.
Caveat: Framework is from observation of executive simulation teams, not from measured ecommerce experiments.

## Write goals you can actually check against
Use when: Setting quarterly goals for a store team or agreeing who owns which channel before a busy trading period.
- Treat goal-setting as a conversation, not a document drop: the source says teams need to be explicit and have a 'really good conversation' about goals, roles and norms together.
- Ensure norms are covered alongside goals — how the team shares information and makes decisions is a first-class part of the foundation, not an afterthought.
- Because drift is 'natural' and 'almost inevitable' as people juggle multiple teams and projects, keep the agreed goals few and small enough to re-read and check against later; Mario advises focusing on a few things rather than a lot, since teams get overwhelmed by goals that are too big.
- Reuse the same written goals at check-ins: the check-in step asks 'what do we want to do, how do we work together, are we still committed?' — this only works if the original aims were specific enough to compare against.
- Ecommerce application: for a peak-season push, agree the goal (e.g. which product lines or margin targets matter), the roles (who owns pricing, who owns creative, who owns stock cover), and the norms (how decisions on discounts get made), then revisit weekly.
Caveat: The source gives no numeric target-setting method or KPI formula; it is a conversation framework, not a measurement system.
Caveat: Overly detailed goal documents risk contradicting the 'keep it simple and manageable' advice.

## Close the saying-doing gap with small, environment-aware steps
Use when: When a store team keeps agreeing on plans in meetings but day-to-day execution on pricing, listings or fulfilment keeps slipping.
- Diagnose the gap honestly: compare what the team said it would do (goals, roles, norms) with what is actually happening each week.
- Choose small steps targeted at specific changes rather than one big reform — the source says the most effective way to close the gap is 'in small steps... targeted at really specific changes.'
- Engineer the environment to support each step: pay attention to the conditions around the team and try to create an environment that makes the new behaviour easy.
- Adopt 'realistic optimism' over pure optimism: think ahead about what could block the intended action and plan for it, rather than assuming it will happen.
- Loop the change back into the check-in: after taking the small step, revisit whether the gap has narrowed, then pick the next small step. Ecommerce application: instead of a store-wide 'improve conversion' push, change one thing — e.g. whose job it is to fix out-of-stock PDPs each morning — and remove the blocker that stopped them.
Caveat: Small steps are a deliberate trade-off; the source does not promise that incremental change fixes structural problems.
Caveat: Realistic optimism requires time to think ahead about obstacles, which can conflict with firefighting during a live sale.

## Three guidelines for better check-in conversations
Use when: Running team rituals such as weekly ops standups, launch retros or trading reviews where conversations get stuck or unproductive.
- Address style differences explicitly: people vary on extroversion/introversion and on comfort with conflict; have a conversation about those styles so the team knows how to talk to each other.
- Use one-on-ones as a primary trust-building channel: group dialogue alone becomes complex because of group dynamics, so pair dialogue with group time to build relationships.
- Keep the agenda narrow: focus on a few things rather than a lot, because teams 'typically get overwhelmed' when trying to do too much or when goals are too big. Simplicity makes the conversation manageable.
- Combine all three: the source argues that if you attend to how you talk to each other and simplify what you talk about, you tend to get a better conversation than if you do neither.
- Ecommerce application: in a weekly trading meeting, restrict the agenda to two or three metrics/decisions rather than every channel, and hold separate 1:1s with merchandising, paid media and ops leads before the group review.
Caveat: No formula is given for how much time to spend in 1:1s versus group check-ins; use judgement based on the team.
Caveat: The source presents these as guidelines, not mandatory rules.

## Match the work to teams vs. individuals
Use when: Deciding whether to assign a store task — e.g. writing a product page, setting pricing, or solving a merchandising problem — to an individual or a group.
- Identify tasks that should probably stay individual: the source lists work requiring specialised expertise, highly creative work, and situations where time is really short, because individuals tend to be more efficient than groups when speed matters.
- Note the source's example: writing a report is likened to creative individual work; in an ecommerce context, drafting a product description or a campaign concept can be treated the same way, then reviewed by the team.
- Reserve the team for problems requiring multiple perspectives and multiple sources of expertise — that is the condition the source identifies as appropriate for team work.
- When you do convene a team, apply the 3x3 steps (commit to goals, roles and norms, check in, close gaps) rather than assuming the group will organise itself, since groups often fail to maximise potential because they don't organise well.
- Ecommerce application: let one specialist own the copy or the bid strategy, but convene merchandising, marketing and supply chain when deciding which categories to back next season.
Caveat: The source gives directional guidance rather than a decision matrix; boundaries between 'expert' and 'multi-perspective' work require judgement.
Caveat: It notes there is lots of evidence groups generally don't maximise potential — this is a general claim, not an ecommerce-specific benchmark.

## Common team failure modes: hero reliance, plan-worship, role neglect
Use when: Diagnosing why a store launch or seasonal plan stalls after a good planning meeting.
- Avoid the 'great person' trap: the source calls relying too much on one strong-vision, dominant person the 'great person theory' and says it is 'almost always a big mistake' to focus too much on one individual. Spread critical knowledge so the store isn't dependent on one merchandiser or one ads buyer.
- Don't stop at the plan: teams commonly focus too much on simply making a plan and not on how to execute it and organise people around it. Add an execution/organisation step after planning.
- Do not leave roles implicit: a common mistake is having a good goals discussion, then saying 'let's go do it' without agreeing who does what, which means efforts aren't maximised. Explicitly assign ownership.
- Build the fix into the routine: the source ties execution and roles back to the commit and check-in steps, so revisit role assignments when circumstances change.
- Ecommerce application: after a promotional calendar is set, list each campaign and name the single owner for creative, pricing and stock, then review ownership at the next check-in.
Caveat: These are observed common errors from a simulation setting, not quantified failure rates.
Caveat: Spreading ownership must be balanced against accountability; the source does not prescribe how many owners per task.

## Build psychological safety so people actually speak up
Use when: When store meetings produce silence or polite agreement but the real problems (stockouts, bad creative, weak margins) surface only later.
- Assume the default is withholding: the source says most of the time people do not speak their minds, and cites research that well over half the time people are not sharing what they are thinking and feeling.
- Treat more information as better: if people share more, decisions and collaboration improve, so create an environment where they feel safe to speak.
- Build safety through concrete behaviours identified in the source: building trust, having good one-on-one conversations, listening, and showing empathy and that you care.
- Prioritise it: if focusing on one thing, the source says it would be creating an environment of psychological safety; it calls psychological safety an apparently soft factor that plays a big role in delivering results ('the soft stuff is the hard stuff').
- Ecommerce application: in post-mortems on failed launches or promotions, explicitly ask what people were worried about but didn't raise, and protect the person who names the uncomfortable problem.
Caveat: Psychological safety is presented as a strong opinion by the author, with general research cited but no specific study figures beyond 'well over half'.
Caveat: It takes sustained one-on-one effort; it is not achieved by declaring a meeting 'safe'.

## Know when to go fast and when to go slow
Use when: Deciding how much process to apply to a same-day flash sale versus a long-term category or assortment decision.
- Borrow the fast/slow distinction: Mario references Kahneman's Thinking Fast and Slow and applies it to teams — 'when you need to go fast maybe don't pay so much attention to team process.'
- Go slow when team process is genuinely important: for example when you need to pull in multiple perspectives, that is when to be deliberate about how the team forms and how it has its conversations.
- Set the right expectation for check-ins: there is no fixed ratio of check-in to working time; some teams have a large appetite and need for process and will check in more, while 'get it done' teams will check in less. What matters is that check-ins happen, with judgement on how much time to spend.
- Distinguish task type when choosing: pair this with the source's split between individual-suitable work (expert, creative, time-short) and team-suitable work (multi-perspective problems).
- Ecommerce application: let one person push a time-critical price change; convene the cross-functional team to deliberate on which categories to invest in or how to restructure the subscription offer.
Caveat: 'It depends' is the source's own answer on check-in frequency — avoid turning this into a fixed cadence rule.
Caveat: The fast/slow framing is an analogy to Kahneman's work, not a validated ecommerce decision rule.

## Build landed unit cost and contribution per ecommerce order
Use when: Pricing a product, deciding whether a SKU is viable, or setting up daily profit reporting in Shopify/Triple Whale.
- Start with net sales excluding pass-through taxes and recorded discounts/refunds.
- Allocate inbound freight to units using a documented driver. A $11,000 container carrying 1,000 identical units adds $11 per unit, not $1.
- Include manufacturing, applicable non-recoverable duties, inbound freight, pick/pack, outbound delivery, storage allocation and payment fees without double-counting.
- Subtract advertising acquisition cost to calculate post-ad contribution; it is not net profit until remaining overhead and other expenses are considered.
- Record landed product costs in the store/reporting system and reconcile fulfillment and advertising separately; verify the current integration.
- Test the resulting contribution at plausible prices and acquisition costs before ordering; no single landed-cost-to-retail ratio guarantees viability.
Caveat: Use current lane- and product-specific duty inputs from the responsible specialist, not blanket country assumptions.
Caveat: This is a management contribution model; keep accounting classifications documented.

## Write a tech pack to control supplier quality and prove non-conformance
Use when: Developing or re-sourcing any physical product, especially apparel, before placing a large purchase order.
- Include: (1) complete drawing with print placement, shape and colours; (2) fabric details — GSM/thickness, softness, colour references (strike-offs), and treatment specs like acid wash; (3) precise measurements for grading across S/M/L/XL; (4) graphics; (5) hardware (zips, pockets, closures); (6) labels and swing tags with company name and correct positioning — wrong or missing labels can trigger regional fines; and (7) packaging specs.
- Keep physical reference samples of fabrics and strike-offs. When quality complaints arrive, cut a standard-size square of fabric and weigh it against the spec GSM to prove the supplier used thinner, wrong fabric — this is how Oodie resolved 'too thin' complaints and got replacements.
- Use the tech pack to brief a second supplier for competitive quotes and to switch factories without redeveloping the product.
- If needed, commission a tech-pack creator using a defined deliverable and current quote; the source's under-$200 price is anecdotal.
- Retain the agreed tech pack, purchase order and supplier messages for quality disputes; platform protection is subject to current terms and does not guarantee a refund.
- Any product has a tech pack; it just becomes more complex for injection-moulded or hardware-heavy goods.
Caveat: The source's quality-improvement estimate is not a guarantee.
Caveat: Document ownership, usage and exclusivity terms separately; neither the supplier nor the buyer automatically owns all IP merely because of this workflow.

## Negotiate MOQ, price and lead time as one trade-off set
Use when: Selecting a supplier or placing a first or repeat purchase order.
- Obtain the supplier's actual minimum order quantity, unit price and manufacturing lead time; small custom runs may be possible at different economics.
- Choose deliberately between higher price/lower MOQ (fits a small budget) and higher price/lower lead time (fits brands that want faster cash-flow turns). The source notes Oodie sometimes paid more for lower lead time.
- Run the landed cost against your intended retail before committing. If landed cost forces a retail price that kills demand, do not launch that variant.
- Write refund/tolerance terms into the supplier relationship before transferring large sums: what happens if product arrives different, tolerance for wrong quantities, and how you verify claimed quantities.
- Resolve important specifications, ownership and remedies in agreed terms before committing funds; relationship-building does not establish these rights.
- Compare multiple factories and consider de-speccing the product to hit a viable cost — the source estimates ~20% price improvement is achievable through comparison and negotiation.
Caveat: MOQs, contract terms and savings depend on supplier and product; the source's 20% saving is an example, not a benchmark.

## Order against the outputs: operations as an input→process→output system
Use when: Deciding which operational initiative to fund or fix (warehouse change, automation, forecasting hire) and how to justify it against marketing spend.
- Define the output as the customer experience, not an internal metric. A process improvement that doesn't change what the customer receives is not the goal.
- Reduce inputs (cost) and process load (labour/steps) through automation and negotiation to get more output for less — this is the lens for every freight, 3PL and supplier decision.
- Use the two-brand cost comparison to justify the work: if brand A lands product at $21 and brand B at $28 due to worse freight, courier, product and warehousing terms, brand A has ~$7 more per unit to spend on marketing — improving CAC headroom and profit.
- Test this by comparing parallel supplier/3PL/freight quotes on freight (e.g. $5 vs $6), product ($10 vs $12), courier ($5 vs $8), storage ($1 vs $2).
- Treat operations as weeding a garden: without consistent cadence, the sales/marketing 'plants' stop producing as much.
- Assign ownership to a COO-type role once you can afford it; below roughly $1M revenue, the founder often holds all these roles.
Caveat: The $21/$28 and 20% figures are illustrative example numbers from the source, not a standard.
Caveat: Needs dedicated ops attention to realise — the source attributes missed growth to lacking an ops role during a demand spike.

## Build a critical path to hit launch and seasonal deadlines
Use when: Planning any product launch or seasonal buying cycle where missing a ship date means stranded inventory (e.g. Christmas).
- List every step: strike-offs/prints, first sample, revised sample, production sample, QC, bulk production, freight, and inbound to warehouse.
- Sequence them in order and assign a duration to each (e.g. DHL sample from China ~5 days).
- Set dates against each step from the target launch/ship date and use a project tool (Monday.com, Asana, Notion, Excel) with reminders for overdue tasks; on slip, push downstream dates or identify slack.
- Assign an owner to each step and monitor daily. Don't let suppliers edit the critical path themselves — they can manipulate dates and you lose visibility.
- Book a QC agent for the first, and ideally second/third, production run: they visit the factory, weigh and measure product against the tech pack and check Pantone colour. Alibaba has an inbuilt QC function; SGS also does compliance.
- Work backwards from the Christmas shipping cut-off — failing to receive samples on time cascades to missing peak trade and leaves you holding stock through January/February.
Caveat: Tooling changes; the specific apps named may differ in current interface.
Caveat: QC via Alibaba or SGS incurs cost and lead time of its own.

## Forecast demand on an in-stock window, not a raw calendar period
Use when: Replenishment planning for any SKU before placing a purchase order.
- Never forecast from a period containing stockouts. If you were out of stock 15 days of a 30-day month, use the 15 in-stock days and extrapolate — the source notes forecast misses of 50% common when this is ignored.
- If under ~1 year of history or growing fast, use month-on-month: e.g. 100→120→145→175 suggests ~20% growth and a linear-to-slightly-exponential projection for the next month.
- Where seasonality dominates (most ecommerce due to Black Friday and holiday gifting), build a month-by-month curve: e.g. Jul 200, Aug 210, Sep 205, Oct 2,000, Nov 4,000, then a sharp drop after the Christmas shipping cut-off.
- Find product-driven seasonality via Google Trends (e.g. 'beach tent' spikes in summer) and account for northern vs southern hemisphere opposites.
- Apply a growth-rate assumption (e.g. 20–40% monthly) in your tool or Excel, then output two answers: sell-out date and replenishment quantity.
- Review on a weekly, fortnightly or monthly cadence; picking the wrong comparison window is the top forecasting error.
Caveat: Growth-rate figures are source examples, not targets.
Caveat: Too much inventory ties up all profit and cash (existential risk); too little yields zero revenue against fixed costs.

## Set lead time, days-of-stock and safety stock inputs in the forecast tool
Use when: Configuring replenishment thresholds and deciding how much working capital to commit to inventory.
- Enter supplier manufacturing lead time (e.g. 100 days) — longer lead times push the tool to trigger orders earlier.
- Set target days of stock based on volatility and available capital: typical ranges are 4, 8 or 12 weeks.
- Add 5–10 days safety stock as a buffer only after padding lead time with your average (rather than best-case) figure — the source says safety stock shouldn't really be needed if lead time is properly padded.
- Use the tool output to get sell-out date and replenishment amount, then raise the purchase order.
- Simple calculators (e.g. Inventory Planner, which the source recommends and uses; verify current features) or a custom Excel sheet both work — the goal is visibility on prior results to make future assumptions.
- Consider hiring an ops manager (not necessarily a dedicated demand planner) to run this weekly once you're profitable and paying yourself.

## Protect against 3PL failure with SLAs and multi-warehouse RFPs

## Run forecasting continuously to avoid the two existential inventory failures
Use when: Deciding SKU expansion and seasonal buy quantities under cash constraints.
- Recognise both failure modes: under-ordering yields $0 revenue against fixed costs; over-ordering ties up all profits in inventory so you can't acquire customers or pay taxes.
- Expand SKUs cautiously — adding colour/size variants multiplies the forecast risk and capital requirement.

## Purchase order visibility across departments
Use when: Setting up or auditing how purchase orders are recorded and shared before a replenishment order is placed or while stock is in transit.
- Recognise that a PO affects finance (paying supplier invoices and deposits), operations (stock arrival timing), marketing (when to push a product) and customer service (refund/discontinuation decisions on inbound stock).
- Keep the authoritative PO in your accounting software (e.g. Xero, MYOB) so it matches supplier deposits and final payments.
- Create a second shared sheet (Google Drive/Sheets or Airtable) that the whole team can view, containing SKU-level product list, quantities and PO number.
- Add freight details as they arrive: estimated departure date, ETA, vessel number, bill of lading; for air freight a tracking number plus estimated dates.
- Update ETAs whenever the vessel is pushed back and notify the affected teams via Slack or comments so no one acts on stale assumptions.

- Before discontinuing a product or mass-refunding, check the sheet for a PO about to land to avoid paying for stock you cannot sell.

## Auditing 3PL invoices against actual order volume
Use when: Monthly reconciliation of a 3PL bill, or comparing 3PL quotes when switching providers.
- Break 3PL pricing into three components: pick-and-pack (per-item fulfilment cost), inbound (unloading container/air freight into the warehouse), and outbound (carrier shipping cost).
- Decide per business whether inbound is material enough to include in unit economics; source notes it is sometimes small enough to ignore.
- Record outbound separately because each 3PL holds its own carrier account (e.g. UPS, Australia Post, Royal Mail) and rates vary hugely — a high-volume 3PL may pass on excellent rates, a brand-new one may not.
- Build a spreadsheet estimating each cost category from your Shopify sales for the period and compare against the invoice.
- Watch for blended pricing that hides where costs sit; source reports roughly 30–40% of clients were overcharged in some cost areas by accepting courier or 3PL figures without checking.
- Track storage separately — usually immaterial unless products are large, but audit it anyway.
Caveat: The 30–40% overcharge figure is one operator's client sample, not a benchmark.
Caveat: Cheapest 3PL is not automatically best; source argues for a mutually profitable relationship, not squeezing the provider on every order.

## Running an RFP to select a 3PL
Use when: Choosing or replacing a third-party logistics provider.
- Assemble a master document with product dimensions, previous results, and — most importantly — forecasts; a growing business should present growth to get providers competing for the account.
- Send the same document to multiple 3PLs so you can compare quotes on identical assumptions.
- Visit the facility or at least see interior footage; refusal to show facilities is a red flag (source cites a prospective 3PL using stolen supermarket trolleys to move product).
- Look for a barcode-scanning operation on both inbound and outbound — without scanning, missing stock becomes unresolvable finger-pointing.
- Assess whether you are a valuable customer: being the smallest or largest account on their book affects the service you get, and sales-team behaviour is an indicator.
- Confirm barcodes, cycle-count frequency and pick-time guarantees are written into the service level agreement.
- Give forecasts you realistically intend to hit — wild forecasts cause over-staffing and erode the relationship.
Caveat: Source view is that the cheapest option is not always best and a zero-sum relationship with the 3PL tends to backfire.

## Writing an SLA with pick-time penalties for peak periods
Use when: Negotiating or renewing a 3PL contract, especially before Black Friday or Christmas.
- Require an SLA stating promised pick time (e.g. within 24–48h) and ship time, with explicit consequences if missed — source suggests a pick discount or a free pick.
- Recognise peak risk: if a 3PL under-forecasts its own staff, your pick time can blow out to 7 days and carrier backlogs compound the delay.

Caveat: SLA terms and pick-time windows vary by provider and market; verify the current contract language.

## Designing the customer service tier split
Use when: Deciding how to staff and structure the customer support function, particularly when scaling past a few agents.

## Run Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning as a planning lens
Use when: Onboarding a new store team, integrating an acquisition, or reading conflict in an existing squad as a stage rather than a crisis.
- Forming: members are anxious about what they and the team should do; relationships are guarded. Front-load orientation — clear goals, procedures, and enough information delivered well, since the source says information availability and presentation are crucial at this stage.
- Storming: expect resistance, impatience with progress, dominant members forcing agendas, challenges to the leader, or withdrawal. The source's key warning: suppressed conflict breeds resentment, bitterness, apathy or abandonment, so let conflict surface.

- Norming: cooperation, open communication and acceptance of mutual influence emerge; widely shared rules of behavior develop. Reinforce belonging and commitment here.
- Performing: trust and acceptance are established, diverse views are encouraged, wild ideas can be shared without fear, leadership shifts to whoever is best for the problem at hand.
- Adjourning: set a specific, recognizable end for task teams rather than letting the team dissolve abruptly; the source notes adjournment is often unplanned.
Caveat: The source states no fixed timeline for progressing between stages, so do not schedule stage deadlines.

## Work through the five-step commitment ladder before asking buy-in
Use when: Rolling out a new process (new WMS, new ad platform, new PDP workflow) where the team nods but does not adopt.
- Vision: project a goal bigger than a paycheck that offers challenge, appeals to personal pride, and creates a sense of making a difference; then picture success out loud — 'what will it look like when we get there, how will others know?'
- Commitment: expect a pre-commitment phase of doubts; the source warns it does not mean people do not care — it often means they care and are stuck weighing pros, cons, obstacles and worries.
- Leaders: avoid seeking accountability without providing support; if you do not help people process doubts, they may 'pretend to commit' — say yes and mean maybe at best.
- Trust: build confidence in leadership and vision by having leadership commit first and visibly. The source's three-step self-process: list the unknowns, assess worst-case scenarios and the probability of overcoming them, research the unknowns.
- Accept irreducible risk: some unknowns resolve only after commitment, so commit with partial ignorance and state that openly.
- Inclusion: get others to genuine commitment in small groups allowing thorough give-and-take; communicate the vision, verify understanding, share leadership's commitment including how risk and reward are shared, and actively elicit doubts.
- Use the three communication skills: non-assumptive questions ('What do you think?'), listening that separates taking in information from judging it, and directed response — answering on the spot where possible, giving a timeline where research is needed, and truthfully saying 'I don't know' on irreducible risk.
- Help exchange: let teammates propose the tasks and efforts to reach the vision; the leader's job here is catalyzing consensus, not issuing orders.
Caveat: Source framework is written for organizational leadership generally; it is not validated against ecommerce rollouts.
Caveat: The 'pretend commitment' concept is the source's interpretation, not an empirical finding.

## Map decisions with Responsibility Charting (R, A-V, S, I)
Use when: When launches stall because nobody knows who can approve, veto, fund or needs notifying — pricing changes, promotions, product selection, site changes.
- Step 1: Construct a grid. Down the left side, list the types of decisions and classes of actions in the area under discussion. Across the top, list the actors who might play a part.
- Step 2: Assign one of four behaviors to each actor against each issue.
- R — Responsibility: initiates action and ensures the decision is carried out (the source's example: a department head initiating the departmental budget).
- A-V — Approval required: must review the item and can approve or veto it.
- S — Support: provides logistical support and resources for the item.
- I — Inform: must be informed, and by inference cannot influence the decision.
- Use the grid when a simple task assignment is actually multi-actor, since work, approval and contribution are typically split among different people.
Caveat: The source calls it simple, relevant and effective for improving team functioning, but offers no comparative evidence.
Caveat: Grids go stale — reassign when people or scope change.

## Use Force Field Analysis to unblock a stalled situation
Use when: When a store initiative (site speed migration, returns process fix, listing quality standard) keeps stalling despite agreement that it matters.
- Step 1: Name the problematic situation, describe the current condition carefully, and state why change is needed.
- Step 2: Describe the desired condition completely and carefully — define what the desired state is.
- Step 3: List driving forces toward it and restraining forces against it.
- Step 4: Examine the forces and divide them into those under your control/influence versus those not under control.
- Step 5: Choose strategies that increase driving forces and reduce restraining factors to shift the equilibrium from current to desired state.
- Step 6: Once the desired condition is reached, implement action plans to stabilize and maintain that state — the source treats stabilization as its own step.
Caveat: Requires honest listing of restraining forces; understating resistance undermines the analysis.
Caveat: No scoring or weighting method is given — treat it as structured discussion, not a quantified model.

## Run a role negotiation contract when resistance is power-based
Use when: When a functional owner (e.g., a category lead or channel owner) blocks a workflow change that would reduce their control.
- Choose this technique when the cause of ineffectiveness is unwillingness to change based on loss of power or influence; the source says it directly intervenes in relationships of power, authority and influence and deliberately avoids probing personal likes and dislikes.
- Contract setting: run the session on work behaviors, not feelings, specifying for each person what needs to be started, stopped and continued. The source says to aim for a written contract of behavioral changes.
- Issue diagnosis: each participant thinks about how their own effectiveness would improve if others changed; each fills out an issue diagnosis form for every other group member stating what they want the other to do more, less, or unchanged.
- Influence trade: pairs discuss the most important behavior changes they want from the other and what they will change themselves; negotiation ends when all parties believe they get a reasonable return for what they give up.
- Schedule a follow-up meeting to check whether contracts were honored and to assess effects.
Caveat: The source recommends a full day for the technique, preferably a two-day session with a follow-up a month later — this is a substantial time commitment.
Caveat: Designed for work-relationship issues; it is not a tool for personal conflict or performance management.

## Choose the team type that matches the work
Use when: Setting up a squad for a defined need: a site redesign, a marketplace launch, a continuous improvement routine, or ongoing category management.
- Functional teams: members perform the same function and speak the same functional language (e.g., a whole paid-media or fulfillment function).
- Project teams: people with different expertise each tackle a different part of the task.
- Cross-functional and interorganizational teams: membership drawn from different functions or organizations to address common problems; can include members across all levels or representatives of concerned interest groups.
- Leader-led vs. leader-less: leader-led teams have a nominated leader with a dominant coordination role; leader-less teams appoint a coordinator who coordinates rather than leads.
- Problem-solving teams: typically 5–12 employees from the same department meeting a few hours each week to discuss and solve work-related problems (quality, safety, productivity, morale); these may have indefinite life or be temporary task forces.
- Self-managing (autonomous) work groups: given responsibility for a task area without day-to-day supervision and with authority over group membership and behavior.
- Cross-functional teams: members from about the same hierarchical level but different work areas, coming together to accomplish a task; task forces and project teams fall here.
- Quality circles: meet regularly, usually an hour or so weekly or fortnightly, to identify, analyze and propose solutions — but normally with no authority to implement; proposals go to management.
- Task forces: formed for a specific highly important task, meeting intensively over weeks or months then disbanding; members keep their normal jobs and backgrounds are typically diverse. Managers often use them for strategic orientation, gathering external environment data, and designing implementation approaches.
Caveat: Team-type labels come from management literature and overlap in practice; classify by how you will actually run and dissolve the team.
Caveat: Self-managing teams still need defined boundaries and decision rights to be effective.

## Separate task roles from process roles and staff both
Use when: Fixing a team that hits tasks but has low morale, or one that is pleasant but never ships.
- Task behaviors that move work forward: starting the group working, suggesting directions, offering information and opinions; proposing goals and tasks and developing plans for approaching the task and sharing responsibilities; offering facts, opinions, ideas and relevant information.
- Process behaviors that keep the group functional: listening actively; encouraging compromises and harmonizing; welcoming others' ideas while expressing your own; giving verbal and non-verbal feedback; warmly encouraging participation and visibly valuing contributions; reconciling disagreements when opinions differ.

## Use Belbin team roles to balance a team, not to label people
Use when: Composing a pod or diagnosing why a capable group underdelivers — for example a team with strong execution but no external exploration.
- Treat a team role as a tendency to behave, contribute and interrelate in a particular way — a behavior cluster, not a personality type.
- Know the nine: Coordinator (mature, confident, clarifies goals, promotes decision-making, delegates; can seem manipulative, offloads personal work), Shaper (challenging, dynamic, thrives on pressure, provides drive and courage; prone to provocation, can offend), Plant (creative, imaginative, unorthodox, solves difficult problems; ignores incidentals, too preoccupied to communicate), Monitor Evaluator (sober, strategic, discerning, judges accurately; lacks drive and ability to inspire), Implementer (disciplined, reliable, conservative, efficient, turns ideas into actions; somewhat inflexible, slow to respond to new possibilities), Completer Finisher (painstaking, conscientious, anxious, searches out errors and omissions, delivers on time; inclined to worry unduly, reluctant to delegate), Resource Investigator (extrovert, enthusiastic, communicative, explores opportunities, develops contacts; over-optimistic, loses interest after initial enthusiasm), Teamworker (co-operative, mild, perceptive, diplomatic, listens, builds, averts friction; indecisive in crunch situations), Specialist (single-minded, self-starting, dedicated, provides rare knowledge and skills; contributes only on a narrow front, dwells on technicalities).
- Use the framework for self-knowledge and adjustment to external demands, not as a permanent label.
- Match composition to the situation: a pressurised turnaround may need Shaper drive, a creative relaunch may need Plant input plus Implementer follow-through.

## Play the Coordinator role without competing with the leader
Use when: When a strong operator is placed in a junior or peer position on a project (e.g., a senior buyer on a cross-functional launch squad).
- Identify people's skills and abilities and use them; make colleagues feel their contributions matter and their skills are recognized.
- Encourage members to put personal objectives second, and give positive feedback on performance.
- Smooth over disagreements and unproductive competition using tact, insight, detachment and firm control.
- Involve colleagues in decision making while retaining task focus and control over getting things done.

## Team Role Strengths and the Behaviours to Avoid
Use when: Running a team retrospective or 1:1 with a merchandiser, marketer or ops lead whose strength has started causing friction.

- Completer: strength is standards, checking and urgency; risk is detail obsession over direction, destructive criticism and morale-lowering worry.

## Communications Norms by Work Type: Routine/Emerging × Autonomous/Interdependent

## Shift the Leader Role by Quadrant: Defender, Manager, Lookout, Facilitator
Use when: Setting a team lead's priorities for the week across routine operations and emerging channel challenges; diagnosing constant firefighting.

## Choose a Conflict Mode Deliberately
Use when: Handling a dispute over promotion timing, budget between channels, or an interdepartmental turf issue in an ecommerce operation.
- Know the five modes: Withdrawal (avoiding), Smoothing (accommodating), Compromising (give-and-take), Forcing (competing), Confrontation/Collaborating (direct problem-solving).
- Confront (collaborate) when: both sides can get at least what they wanted and maybe more; to reduce cost; to create a common power base; to attack a common foe; when skills are complementary; when there is enough time; when there is trust; when you have confidence in the other person's ability.
- Smooth (accommodate) when: reaching an overarching goal; creating obligation for a later trade-off; stakes are low; liability is limited; maintaining harmony; any adequate solution will do; creating goodwill; you will lose anyway; to gain time.
- Force (compete) when: you are right; a do-or-die situation exists; stakes are high; important principles are at stake; you are stronger; to gain status or power; the relationship is unimportant; a quick decision must be made.
- Withdraw (avoid) when: you can't win; stakes are low; to gain time; to unnerve your opponent; to preserve neutrality or reputation; you think the problem will go away; you win by delay.
- Ecommerce application: confront on cross-channel attribution disputes where both functions gain from a shared measurement fix; smooth on low-stakes copy-style disagreements; force on a deadline-driven launch call; withdraw from status-only arguments that consume planning time.
Caveat: The mode list and conditions are the source's presentation, combining several conflict models; apply judgement, not a lookup table.
Caveat: Modes can be combined or sequentially applied; the source treats them as situational alternatives rather than one universal rule.

## Tell Constructive Conflict from Destructive Conflict
Use when: Deciding whether to intervene in an ongoing conflict between merchandising and marketing, or between ops and customer service.
- Destructive signs: diverts energy from important activities; destroys morale or reinforces poor self-concepts; polarizes groups (increasing sub-group cohesion but reducing intergroup cooperation); deepens value differences; produces irresponsible or regrettable behaviour such as name-calling; increases hostility; creates a negative climate.
- Constructive signs: opens important issues and clarifies them; resolves problems; increases involvement; produces authentic communication; releases pent-up emotion and anxiety; helps individuals grow and apply learning later.
- Adopt the source's premises: conflict is inevitable; not all conflict is bad; without it there can be no learning; it prevents stagnation, stimulates curiosity, fosters creative thinking and cooperative problem-solving, energizes people, motivates change and acts as a safety valve for problems.
- Act on the flip side: badly resolved conflicts create future conflicts, so unresolved disputes must be closed properly rather than suppressed.
- Ecommerce application: a pricing or promo debate between channels may be constructive if it clarifies margins and cannibalization; intervene when the same argument resurfaces and people begin avoiding the meetings or blaming individuals.
Caveat: These are diagnostic indicators from the source, not measured thresholds; leaders must judge context.
Caveat: Constructive conflict still needs containment — unmanaged release can damage trust, which the source says can be lost in a second.

## Resolve Conflicts by Process Consultation, Not Verdict

## Pick a Leadership Style from Information and Time
Use when: Deciding how to run a pricing decision, an incident response, or a creative brief in an ecommerce team.

## Measure Team Effectiveness Instead of Only Output

## Logic of addition vs. logic of multiplication: how to answer a growth target when headcount is frozen

Caveat: Multiplication is not 'do more with less' - Wiseman frames it as getting more by using more of people's intelligence, so it still requires investment of time and attention.

## Extract or suppress: measure how much of your team's intelligence you are actually accessing

## Run decision debates that surface real evidence before committing

## Choose one overarching goal and translate it into a measurable target

## Run a Five Dysfunctions Team Diagnostic

- Re-run the diagnostic periodically, because the source stresses that even strong teams drift back toward dysfunction without constant work.

## Use 'Real-Time Permission' to Keep Difficult Debates Going
