## A job is progress in a circumstance, not a customer type
Use when: Starting any avatar or customer-research effort and deciding what unit of analysis to research and record.
- State the job as progress-in-a-circumstance: 'when [situation], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome]'.
- Include all three dimensions: what the person is trying to accomplish functionally, how they want to be seen (social), and how they want to feel (emotional).
- Reject substitutes that are demographics-only: age, gender, income or a stock-photo persona with no causal mechanism does not define who buys or why.
- Treat the circumstance as the driver of the decision; the same person can hire different solutions in different situations.
- Test the definition by asking whether it explains why a real customer chose this and not an alternative in that moment.
Caveat: Demographics can still be used for media buying or reach planning, but they do not explain the choice and should not be the avatar's core.
Caveat: A job statement without a circumstance usually collapses back into a generic persona.

## Mine five sources for undiscovered jobs
Use when: When you need new job candidates rather than validation of existing assumptions or feature wishlists.
- Personal life: look outside the product category at routines and struggles in everyday life that your product could serve.
- Nonconsumption: study people who currently buy nothing in the category and find out what they do instead.
- Workarounds and compensating behaviors: watch for duct-tape solutions, spreadsheets, manual steps and homemade fixes that reveal an unmet job.
- Negative jobs: identify tasks people hate doing or dread and would gladly hand off.
- Unusual uses: examine off-label and edge-case uses of the product; they often expose a job the product was not designed for.
- For each candidate, record quoted evidence from the person and the trigger circumstance, not just the inferred need.
Caveat: Feature-request surveys and focus groups asking what to add produce wishlists, not jobs; they miss the context that causes purchase.
Caveat: Off-label use signals a possible job, but confirm it recurs before treating it as a segment.

## Prioritize passive struggle data over active operational data
Use when: When choosing what evidence to gather and when a data-driven team wants to substitute reports for direct research.
- Recognize the fallacy of active data: sales figures, CRM fields and customer characteristics are readily available and tend to crowd out the harder-to-get data about why people acted.
- Treat sales and attribute data as outcome evidence: useful for sizing and segmentation, but not for explaining the causal mechanism of choice.
- Collect passive data of struggle: what the customer tried, what frustrated them, what they rigged up, and what finally triggered the switch.
- Record the struggle in the customer's own words so the avatar rests on quoted evidence.
- Use quantitative data afterward to check whether the discovered job is common enough to matter.
Caveat: This is not a rejection of analytics; it is a warning about using convenient operational data to answer causal questions.
Caveat: Small qualitative samples can reveal a job but cannot establish its prevalence without follow-up measurement.

## Map the forces: push and pull versus habits and anxieties
Use when: When diagnosing why prospects stall or abandon, or when building objection-handling into an avatar record.
- Identify the push: the frustration or triggering event that makes the current situation untenable.
- Identify the pull: the attractiveness of the new solution and the progress it promises.
- Identify the habits of the present: routines, sunk costs and defaults that keep the customer using the old approach.
- Identify the anxieties of the new: fear of the unknown, risk of looking foolish, switching cost, data-migration worry, uncertainty that it will work.
- Judge relative magnitude: if pull is weak or anxiety and habit are strong, the purchase does not happen even when the customer wants the outcome.
- Record opposing forces as objections to address later in page or offer design; the avatar's job is to name them, not resolve them here.
Caveat: Addressing objections on the page belongs to landing-page and offer work; this section only discovers and documents them.
Caveat: Strong anxiety is not the same as low desire; a customer can want the progress and still not switch.

## Interview by reconstructing the purchase timeline
Use when: Running customer interviews to uncover the job and the trigger moment behind a purchase.
- Ask the customer to tell the story of how they came to buy, starting before they knew your product existed.
- Track the timeline: the first thought something had to change, the events in between, and the moment of decision.
- Probe for the trigger event: what specifically happened that made the status quo unacceptable that week or that day.
- Ask what they tried before, what they almost bought, and what they turned down, to surface competing solutions.
- Avoid attribute questions such as 'which feature did you like' or hypotheticals such as 'would you buy this'; they generate rationalizations rather than stories.
- Capture verbatim quotes at each stage so the avatar record carries evidence rather than summary.
Caveat: Retrospective accounts are imperfect; ask for concrete events and sequences to reduce post-hoc rationalization.
Caveat: Do not lead the customer toward the answer you want; let the timeline reveal the cause.

## Distinguish the Big Hire from the Little Hire
Use when: When diagnosing whether problems sit in acquisition or in onboarding, activation and repeat usage.
- Define the Big Hire as the moment of purchase or signup: what triggered it, what was compared, what anxiety was overcome.
- Define the Little Hire as each subsequent decision to actually use and keep using the product.
- Interview separately for each: the Big Hire timeline often differs from the Little Hire experience.
- Look for mismatch: a customer may buy enthusiastically and then never activate, signalling the job promised at purchase was not the job they experienced.
- Record both in the avatar: the buying job and the using job, with their distinct triggers and obstacles.
Caveat: Confusing the two produces avatars that explain acquisition but not retention, or vice versa.
Caveat: A strong Big Hire can mask a broken Little Hire for months before churn shows up.

## Build the job spec: tradeoffs, competition and obstacles
Use when: After a job is identified, when translating it into requirements for product, offer or messaging.
- List the tradeoffs the customer is willing to make, and those they are not; these bound acceptable solutions.
- Identify the competing solutions that must be beaten in the customer's mind, including doing nothing, workarounds and adjacent products.
- List the obstacles that must be removed before the customer can make progress, both practical and emotional.
- Express the spec as the sequence of experiences the customer expects, in the order they encounter them.
- Use the spec as the yardstick for product, offer and message decisions rather than adding features by request.
Caveat: A spec describes experiences, not a feature list; converting it into features is product work that follows.
Caveat: The spec changes when the circumstance changes, so re-check it against new interview evidence.

## Storyboard the customer's struggle
Use when: Synthesizing interview material into a shared internal view of the job before writing avatar records or messaging.
- Lay out the timeline as scenes: first thought of change, search, consideration, purchase, first use, repeat use.
- At each scene, note what the customer did, felt, said and worried about.
- Mark the trigger moment where the status quo became unacceptable.
- Mark the points of anxiety and the moments of habit that nearly stopped the decision.
- Mark where competing solutions, including doing nothing, appeared.
- Use the storyboard to check that your avatar record, offer and messaging address the actual sequence rather than an assumed funnel.

Caveat: Resist smoothing the story into a tidy funnel; the messy path is where the insight lives.

## Give every avatar quoted evidence
Use when: Writing or reviewing avatar records, especially when tempted to summarize without evidence.
- Attach at least one verbatim quote to each key claim: the job, the circumstance, the trigger and the opposing forces.

## Inventory candidates before fixing the number of avatars
Use when: Planning avatar research and deciding how many distinct avatars to maintain.
- Collect job candidates from the five mining sources: personal life, nonconsumption, workarounds, negative jobs and unusual uses.

- Merge clusters that differ only superficially; keep clusters that involve genuinely different tradeoffs, triggers or obstacles.

## Use negative jobs as an opportunity lens
Use when: When hunting for new offers or messaging angles, especially in categories where customers complain about the process rather than the product.
- Ask what part of the process customers visibly procrastinate over, complain about or delegate whenever possible.
- Distinguish dread from mere inconvenience: a negative job involves reluctance and avoidance, not just time cost.
- Find the workaround people use to avoid the task, such as paying someone else, batching it, or ignoring it entirely.
- Test whether removing that task is the real promise: 'you never have to do X' may beat feature comparisons.
- Record the negative job with a quote describing the dread in the customer's own words.

Caveat: Removing a task can reduce perceived value for some buyers who take pride in doing it themselves.

## Diagnose a stalled decision with the competing-solutions list
Use when: When conversion is weak or customers describe long deliberation before buying.
- Ask the customer what they almost bought or did instead, and what made them hesitate.
- Include non-obvious competitors: doing nothing, a spreadsheet, an intern, an agency, a template, a friend's advice, or a different category entirely.
- For each alternative, record the advantage the customer saw in it, such as price, familiarity or lower risk.
- Identify the specific advantage your solution must have to beat the winning alternative.
- Feed the resulting comparison into the avatar record as evidence of the real competitive set.

## Capture trigger moments as discrete, datable events
Use when: When your avatar or messaging lacks urgency and prospects agree with the message but never act.
- Ask what changed in the week or month before the purchase: a move, a hire, a failed task, a deadline, an incident.
- Write the trigger as a concrete event with a rough date rather than a general condition such as 'wanted to grow'.
- Check how much time elapsed between trigger and purchase; a long gap suggests unresolved anxiety or habit.
- Use the trigger to time messaging and offers rather than presenting always-on urgency that means nothing.
- Record the trigger in the avatar as part of the job statement's circumstance.
Caveat: Some categories have recurring triggers rather than one dramatic event; record the pattern, not a fabricated story.
Caveat: Correlation between an event and purchase in one interview does not establish it as the general trigger.

## Treat habits of the present as the silent competitor
Use when: When prospects acknowledge the problem and the solution yet fail to switch.
- Identify what the customer currently does and what makes it easy, such as existing workflow, learned skill or a sunk-cost tool.
- Ask what would have to change in their routine for adoption, and who else would be affected.
- Look for frictionless switch mechanisms: import tools, migration help, templates, guided onboarding, a service that does the setup.
- Estimate the strength of the habit against the strength of the push; if habit is strong, reduce friction rather than amplify messaging.
- Record the habit in the avatar as an obstacle to be removed, not an objection to be argued away.
Caveat: More persuasive claims do not overcome a strong habit; friction removal usually matters more.
Caveat: Some habits represent genuine preference and should be respected rather than attacked.

## Jobs Theory: Innovation Built on Causality, Not Correlation
Use when: Framing an innovation or product strategy, or when your team's data is descriptive (who buys, what they look like) but cannot predict which new offer will succeed.
- Reframe the innovation question from 'how do we make our product better, more profitable, or more differentiated?' to 'what job did the customer hire this product to do?' (Christensen's central question).
- Treat buying as hiring and quitting as firing: when a product does the job, it gets re-hired in the same circumstance; when it does the job badly, the customer fires it and looks for a replacement.
- Accept that customer attributes (age, ZIP code, income, shoe size — Christensen's own example is that his height, age and shoe size never caused him to buy the New York Times) are not causal. Correlations can help describe a market but cannot reveal why a purchase happened.
- Look for the circumstance, not the customer: a job arises from a situation ('long boring commute', 'want to say yes to my kid once this week'), and the same person can hire the same product for entirely different jobs at different times of day.
- Use the job to redefine the competitive set. If margarine is hired to moisten bread so it's easier to chew, its competitors may be cream cheese, olive oil and mayonnaise; if hired to keep food from burning in a pan, competitors are Teflon and cooking spray in a different aisle.
- Recognize that a market defined by internal category language ('yellow fats', 'spreads') is not how customers shop — they arrive with a specific job.
- Expect the payoff to be predictability, not just post-hoc storytelling: the value of the theory is in predicting new successes, not celebrating past ones.
Caveat: Jobs Theory tells you why customers make choices and what to build; it does not tell you whether a job is ethical to serve (Christensen notes cigarettes can be hired to satisfy jobs but harm the customer — ethical judgment is separate).
Caveat: A job is a highly specific construct, not a catchphrase for something a customer wants or needs; the loose use of 'jobs' as a buzzword defeats the purpose.
Caveat: Christensen claims the odds of creating exactly the right product even when a firm intends to disrupt are probably less than 25% 'if that' — presented as his estimate, not a measured benchmark.
Caveat: Identifying the job is only the beginning; you still need to design the accompanying experiences and integrate internal capabilities, or the insight does not convert to advantage.

## The Milk Shake Study: Circumstance Interviews Beat Attribute Surveys
Use when: Designing customer research for a product with flat or declining sales, or when survey feedback ('cheaper, chunkier, chewier') is producing changes that don't move the number.
- Run the conventional effort first if you must, to see its limits: the chain surveyed target-profile customers with 'How can we improve our milk shakes so you'd buy more? Do you want it cheaper? Chunkier? Chewier? Chocolatier?' and acted on the answers. Within months, sales of the category were unchanged.
- Then observe instead of asking. The team stood in a restaurant for eighteen hours watching: what time people bought, what they wore, whether they were alone, whether they bought other food, and whether they stayed or drove off.
- Note the pattern that emerged: a surprising share of shakes sold before 9 a.m. to solo customers who bought nothing else and left with it in the car.
- Interview those buyers about the job, opening with an admission that you are puzzled ('What job were you trying to do that caused you to come here and hire that milk shake?'). Customers often cannot answer directly, so probe on what else they sometimes hired instead.
- Collect the substitute set and each substitute's failure mode: bananas 'gone too quickly', doughnuts too crumbly and sticky on fingers and steering wheel, bagels dry and requiring two hands to spread, a Snickers bar that created guilt about candy for breakfast.
- Identify the winning job statement in the customer's own terms: help me stay awake and occupied and make the morning commute more fun, while heading off mid-morning hunger. The thick shake through a thin straw took ~20 minutes and fit the cup holder.
- Serve each job with its own design: for the commute job, a thicker shake (fruit or chocolate bits as surprise, for interest, not health) plus faster self-serve dispensing at the front counter with a swipe card; for the afternoon parent job, half sizes that finish quickly and reduce dad's guilt.
- Note that the same product was competing against different things in each job — morning against bagels, bars and juice; afternoon against stopping at the toy store or a later game of catch.
- Do not average the two jobs into one product. Christensen's explicit warning: averaging dad and commuter responses yields a one-size-fits-none shake that does neither job well.
Caveat: This is a case study of one chain's discovery, not a promised sales outcome.
Caveat: Demographics did not separate the morning buyers — what they shared was a job, not a profile.
Caveat: The afternoon job is inferred from Christensen's own experience as a father in line; it illustrates the reasoning rather than reporting measured sales.

## Jobs Interviews: Track the Timeline of Triggers, Not Product Attributes

## Push, Pull, Anxiety and Habit: The Forces Behind a Purchase Decision
Use when: Diagnosing why prospects stall mid-funnel, or structuring interview notes and avatar definitions around what actually triggered and blocked a switch.
- Map the push: the magnitude of the frustration in the current situation that makes the customer want to change at all.
- Map the pull: the attraction of the new solution relative to the alternatives available for the same job.
- Map the anxiety: the fears and uncertainties about adopting the new solution.
- Map the habit: the inertia of the present behaviour that must be overcome for the switch to happen.
- Treat the job spec as the output of this analysis: the tradeoffs the customer is willing to make, the competing solutions that must be beaten, and the obstacles that must be removed.
- Use the balance to explain timing — a job sitting under strong habit and anxiety will not convert, however good the pull.
Caveat: The four forces are a diagnostic frame for the decision, not a scoring model with published weights.

## What to Skip: Demographic Personas and Feature-Wish Surveys
Use when: Auditing an existing avatar or persona deck, or approving a research brief, to check it is built on causal evidence rather than demographics.
- Skip persona templates that carry demographic fields and no job — anything that produces a fictional character with a stock photo.
- Skip segmentation based on age, gender or income when it does not define the underlying causal mechanism.
- Skip focus group data or surveys that simply ask customers what features they want added, without understanding the context of the job.
- Avoid the Fallacy of Active Data: do not prioritise operations-related data (sales, customer characteristics) over the passive data of customer struggle.
- Replace all of the above with the baseline: every avatar must contain quoted evidence, a trigger moment and a job statement.

## Why Big Data and Customer Satisfaction Metrics Miss the Point
Use when: When a dashboard of demographics, market share and CSAT is being used as the basis for a product or growth decision.
- Recognise the shape of the data most companies hold: 'this customer looks like that one', 'this product has similar attributes to that one', 'these people behaved the same way in the past', or a preference split like '68% of customers say they prefer version A over version B'. None of it states why the choice was made.
- Keep the correlation-versus-causation distinction operational, not decorative. Christensen cites Nate Silver: ice cream sales and forest fires correlate because both rise in summer heat, but buying a pint of Häagen-Dazs does not set the Montana brush on fire.
- Watch for processes that operationalise a belief in randomness: if you believe innovation is unknowable and messy, you design a process that perfectly delivers messy, unknowable results. As Deming put it, every process is perfectly designed to deliver the results it gets.
- Treat customer satisfaction metrics with suspicion for innovation purposes: they tell you whether a customer is happy, not how to do the job better — yet they are how most companies track success.
- Ask better questions instead. Deming: if you do not know how to ask the right question, you discover nothing.
- Beware stage-gates, six-sigma-style precision and rapid iteration applied to the wrong question: all that activity can give the illusion of progress without causing it, with R&D spend rising while breakthrough innovation is missed.
Caveat: Christensen's framing of the data problem is an argument about explanatory power for innovation, not a claim that operations data has no value for running the business.
Caveat: The 'less than 25%' odds figure is his estimate for hitting the right product, not a documented statistic from the sources cited.

## Avoid Innovating Toward 'Average': The Jagged-Profile Lesson
Use when: When a roadmap item or landing page is being designed to 'work for everyone', or when multiple jobs are being averaged into a single offer.
- Recall the sequence: the Air Force assumed pilot control problems were training or pilot error, then found a cockpit designed around the 1920s average pilot.
- They measured over four thousand pilots on nearly a dozen dimensions of size to update the average — then found that none of the pilots actually fell within the definition of average; every pilot had a jagged profile (some long legs, others long arms, height not corresponding to chest or head size).
- The revised cockpits designed for everyone fit no one; the fix came only when baseline assumptions were swept aside and the adjustable seat was invented.
- Apply the same logic to a jobs analysis: if morning commuters need a thick, slow shake and afternoon parents need a small, quickly finished one, do not design the average shake — design for each job or pick one deliberately.
- In practice this argues for adjustability or genuinely distinct variants rather than one compromise offer.
Caveat: This is an analogy from military procurement borrowed from Todd Rose's The End of Average; it is illustrative, not an ecommerce experiment.
Caveat: It does not follow that every product must be customised — the lesson is against averaging incompatible jobs, not against standardisation.

## Rewrite the Competitive Set as a Résumé of Alternatives
Use when: Doing competitive analysis, sizing an opportunity, or repositioning a product that looks like a commodity inside its own category.
- State the job precisely first, because the competitor set changes with it. 'Moisten the crust on my bread so it's easier to chew' brings in butter, cream cheese, olive oil and mayonnaise. 'Keep my food from burning while cooking' brings in Teflon and nonstick spray, none of which sit in the cold foods aisle.
- Write a résumé for each competing product: what job it gets hired for, in what circumstance, and where it falls short.
- Accept that the same product can be hired for different jobs at different points in a customer's life, so one product may appear on several résumés.
- Use the resumé lens to expose growth you cannot see through market share: Unilever held about 70% of US margarine, but no customer walks in asking for 'something in the yellow fats category'.

## From Broken Category Assumptions to Growth: Disruption vs Jobs
Use when: Choosing the strategic frame for an innovation programme, or when a disruption lens has identified a threat but produced no product roadmap.
- Use disruption for what it does: it explains and predicts incumbent behaviour in the face of a threat and helps incumbents judge which innovations on the horizon are the greatest disruptive threats.
- Do not use disruption as an opportunity map. Christensen is explicit that it does not tell you where to look, how to undermine established leaders, where to create new markets, or which new products will succeed.
- Be careful with the term itself: over two decades it has been stretched to mean anything clever, new and ambitious, which drains it of predictive power.
- Use Jobs Theory for the offensive question — what causes customers to pull a product into their lives — since that is the causal mechanism behind successful innovation.

- Expect different competitors, different priorities and different results once you adopt the jobs lens.

## Why Smart Managers Fail: Processes Perfectly Designed for Mediocrity
Use when: Post-mortems where leadership failure is the assumed explanation, or when building an innovation process that you expect to produce breakthroughs.
- Distrust the management-quality narrative. Digital Equipment Corporation's success was attributed to the brilliance of its management and its collapse to the ineptitude of the same people — Christensen asks how smart people get so stupid so fast.
- Test the individual-blame hypothesis against the industry: almost every minicomputer company in the world collapsed in unison, which points to something structural rather than a few bad leaders.
- Accept that disciplined processes can be the problem. Careful stage-gates, rapid iteration, checks and balances, calculated risks and six-sigma-derived requirements give the appearance of a mastered scientific process while innovation stays hit or miss.

## Define a job as progress in a circumstance
Use when: When framing a new product, offer or campaign and you need to state clearly what the customer is actually trying to accomplish rather than describing features or audience demographics.
- Write the job as a sentence about progress: 'I want to ___ in this situation.' Avoid naming your product or 'I need ___'.
- Specify the circumstance: where, when, with whom, while doing what, and what happens before and after. Circumstance is the unit of analysis because the same person wants different things in different situations.
- Describe the functional, social and emotional dimensions of the progress. Social and emotional needs can outweigh functional ones (e.g. trusting someone with your children).
- Treat the job as ongoing and recurring rather than a single event; people re-hire solutions whenever the circumstance recurs.
- Check it is a job, not a 'need' or a life theme. 'I need to eat' and 'I want to be a good dad' are too general to guide a specific product choice.
Caveat: A job is discovered, not created; the underlying job endures even as solutions change.
Caveat: Jobs are fragile, story-like insights: breaking them into binary segments (male/female, new/existing) destroys meaning.

## Ask 'what has to prove true' before committing resources

## Learn from Toyota: design defects out with if-then process tests
Use when: When a repeatable process (fulfilment, creative production, onboarding) keeps producing errors that get patched rather than prevented.
- Stop defaulting to added inspection, inventory and rework, which the source says ballooned cost and complexity while leaving the root cause untouched.
- Treat each defect as a scientist treats an anomaly: find its specific cause, then change the process so the error cannot recur.
- Standardise each value-adding step: do it the same way every time, so that managers know whether the next step works with it.
- See each standardised repetition as a test of the if-then statement: 'if we do this, then that will be the result.'
- Accept that complexity (thousands of parts, hundreds of suppliers, many countries) does not make errors inevitable if the process focuses on identifying and removing causes.
Caveat: The automotive quality story is used here as an analogy for innovation, not as an ecommerce case with measured results.
Caveat: The source explicitly says innovation is still in a 'pre-quality revolution' state, so do not overstate how far this transfers.

## Job-hunting method 1: mine your own life for jobs

## Job-hunting method 2: hunt nonconsumption, not competitor share

## Job-hunting method 3: read workarounds as demand signals
Use when: You're scanning for unmet needs and notice customers jury-rigging spreadsheets, manual processes or odd substitutes.

## Job-hunting method 4: negative jobs — what customers don't want to do

## Map the Four Forces: Push, Pull, Inertia, Anxiety
Use when: You are designing for conversion or adoption and want to reduce friction, not just add appeal.
- Identify the push: the frustration or problem substantial enough to trigger action (a merely annoying problem may not qualify).

- Identify inertia: 'habits of the present' — the comfort of the current workaround, even an imperfect one.
- Identify anxiety: fear of the new — cost, learning curve, the unknown. The source cites Kahneman and Tversky that loss aversion is roughly twice as psychologically powerful as the allure of gains (their finding).
- Design so the pull clearly exceeds the sum of inertia plus anxiety. Address anxiety explicitly with reassurance.

## Writing a Job Spec: From Customer Job to Design Blueprint

- Write the spec from the innovator's point of view, answering: what must I design, develop and deliver so this job is solved well?

- List the obstacles and anxieties that must be overcome, and pair each with a planned experience that surmounts it.
- Use the spec as the blueprint for purchase and use experiences. The source argues that without a clear job spec, even advanced products are likely to fail because there are too many details to guess right.

## Competing with Nonconsumption and Unlikely Alternatives
Use when: Doing competitive analysis, category positioning or identifying barriers to adoption in a low-penetration market
- List obvious competitors, then deliberately add: workaround behaviours, product substitutes, borrowing, postponement and nonconsumption.
- In the mattress example, the effective competitors included painkillers, energy drinks and the old mattress itself, not just rival mattress brands.

## The Three Fallacies of Innovation Data

## Conforming Data: Why You Miss the Obvious
Use when: Running innovation, range or investment reviews where sales, marketing, product and finance all present supporting numbers.
- Expect predictable positions: sales claims to know customer needs from constant contact; marketing proposes brand-leverage variants (new versions, flavors, colors, offers); product/R&D pushes features and technology; the business head pushes whatever can hit the P&L this year. - Counteract confirmation bias with Nate Silver's point: prediction failures cluster where we focus on signals that tell the story we want, not the story that is; and Holmes's 'nothing is more deceptive than the obvious fact.'

## All Data Is Made by Someone with an Agenda
Use when: Choosing or defending a data source for a positioning or product decision, or when a team treats dashboards as ground truth.

## Why Mission Statements Fail as Daily Guides

## Levitt and Drucker: The Drill-vs-Hole Disconnect
Use when: Teaching or re-anchoring a team on customer-centric framing before writing positioning, roadmaps or campaign briefs.
- State the premise plainly: people do not want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole; customers do not want products, they want solutions to their problems. The authors attribute the popularization to Ted Levitt, who in The Marketing Imagination credited Leo McGinneva.
- Use Drucker's parallel warning that the customer rarely buys what the company thinks it is selling to frame the company-customer disconnect.
- Expect company drift after success: most successful organizations were de facto founded on an identified job, but after launch the job gets lost and the company defines itself by products — this is a common pattern even among the best companies.
- Apply the railroad lesson: the railroad industry declined not because transportation demand fell (it grew) but because cars, trucks, planes and telephones did the job better; as Levitt wrote in 1960, railroads assumed they were in the railroad business rather than the transportation business.
- Note the self-defining market frame: no customer has a job called 'I need to spend a couple of hours going to the store,' so brick-and-mortar-organized retailers tend to play defense; OpenTable, Salesforce.com, Airbnb, craigslist and IKEA are cited as businesses organized around a distinct job with minimal competition.
- For startups, treat the founder's own struggle as a research asset: the Netflix origin story began with an entrepreneur in a circumstance with no clear solution saying 'I'm going to fix this,' collapsing the distance between innovator and customer job.
Caveat: The 'no customer job to go to the store' claim is illustrative of the authors' framework, not an empirical finding about all retail.
Caveat: The listed successes are examples chosen to illustrate job-centered organizing; their outcomes are not attributed to this framework alone.

## Validate a Job Statement by Abstraction Level
Use when: When checking whether a job statement is genuinely a job, or when a team's 'job' is really a preference, a spec or an aspiration.
- Reject job statements made of adjectives and adverbs. 'Convenience' is not a job — it may be an experience that causes a customer to choose you, but it isn't the job.
- Express the job in verbs and nouns, e.g. 'I need to write books verbally, obviating the need to type or edit by hand.'
- Apply the category test: if the architecture of the product or system can only be met by products within the same product class, you haven't found a job.
- Move up one level of abstraction until candidates come from different categories. - Distinguish technical specifications from jobs: a spec such as a thin sheet with a high coefficient of friction and low thermal conduction is not a job; the underlying job is keeping the family warm and dry through Boston winters and summers while minimizing heating and cooling costs — which wood pulp, fiberglass, Tyvek, dehumidifiers and fans or even relocating could all serve.
- Do this work seriously — the source insists calling something a job should not roll off the tongue with minimal thought.
Caveat: Setting the right level of abstraction is described as more art than science; expect iteration.
Caveat: Jobs theory is bounded — applying 'job' to every human motivation dilutes its clarity and predictability.
Caveat: The theory was built inductively and its author explicitly says anomalies are useful signals of its boundaries, not disproof.

## Four-pronged buyer research methodology
Use when: You need to build or rebuild buyer personas and want evidence-based insights rather than assumptions or template personas.
- Run qualitative customer conversations first (about 20 x 30-minute calls) to surface the buyer's why, trigger moment, journey, pain points and emotional drivers.
- Design a quantitative survey to verify the interview trends at scale and to collect data-centric attributes that are easier to capture in a form (role, company size, years in role).
- Use digital intelligence tools (for the source: SparkToro, Audience, IBM Watson-based data) to see how the wider audience actually behaves: platforms they use, influencers who shape buying decisions, and buying mindset (e.g. move fast vs needs a sales team, risk-taker vs conservative, self-serve vs wants support).
- Run social listening (source used Brand24) to track topics and pain points people discuss, not to monitor your own brand mentions.
- Compile all four data sets, then segment the personas by jobs to be done rather than by age, gender or job title.
- Use the digital data for how people behave and the conversations for why they behave that way; cross-check what people say against what the tools show they do.
Caveat: The four-prong framework is universal in the source's view, but the number of personas and specific data points gathered are not universal and should match the business.
Caveat: Digital tools show behavior but never explain motivation; conversations explain motivation but can be inaccurate about behavior.

## Run 30-minute discovery calls, not sales calls
Use when: Recruiting and conducting customer interviews to extract buyer insights for personas, messaging or product decisions.
- State clearly up front (in the scheduling email and at the start of the call) that you are not a product expert, cannot solve their problem, and are not there to demo or upsell.
- Tell them exactly what the call is about and how much time you need (source uses 30 minutes) so there is no confusion or concern about a hidden sales motive.
- Ask about their full customer journey: how they found the product, why they use it, their experience and emotions around it.
- Aim for about 20 conversations; the source cites Nielsen/company research (via Deploy Empathy by Michele Hansen) that 20 conversations yield about 97% sentiment coverage of the audience.
- Expect customers who love or hate the product to want to talk; if few people will book, that itself signals weak feeling toward the product.
Caveat: The 97% figure is a cited research claim, not a guarantee for every audience.
Caveat: Fear of bothering customers or triggering cancellations is largely unfounded per the source's experience, but interviews should still be handled with care.
