## Build a Bill of Materials Before Anything Else
Use when: Setting up or renegotiating product sourcing for any physical-goods store, and before you start sourcing a new SKU or assume your landed cost is fixed.
- List every component of the finished unit (e.g. a gold solitaire ring = diamond, gold, labor, factory overhead, factory margin).
- For each component, capture: component cost, sub-supplier/source, lead time, country of origin, and payment terms.
- Get the sub-vendor layer too — your factory may not make its own inputs (lab-grown diamonds, gold, zippers, chipsets).
- Split out labor and factory overhead as separate lines; these are the hardest to ask about but the ones that reveal where the money is. Verify sourcing claims (e.g. test that 'organic cotton' is actually organic).
- Track component prices that move intraday or monthly (gold, diamonds, chipsets) so a supplier's markup stays visible and you can reprice when markets move.
- Review the BOM with your supplier at least yearly and request price-break thresholds per line item.
- Use the BOM as the first audit when acquiring or taking over a store: ~99 of 100 founders in the source's M&A experience did not have one.
Caveat: The source says a BOM is worth building once you spend roughly $1M/year on COGS (~$100k/month to supplier), and some businesses benefit as low as ~$2.5M annual revenue; it is a judgment call below that.

## Use BOM to Rethink Product Specs, Not Just Costs
Use when: Merchandising and product-development decisions — deciding what materials, specs, and SKU variants to sell and at what price.
- After building the BOM, ask structural questions per SKU: can this be made in a cheaper input (e.g. 10K vs 14K gold)? Can a premium tier be offered that lets you raise price more than cost?
- Test whether a higher-end variant can be priced $200 above the base version while adding only ~$100 of real cost, creating more absolute dollars of margin.
- Check whether you actually need every spec — if a component is a small share of COGS, price is your lever; if it's a large share, material substitution is your lever.

- Keep monitoring the component markets: a price move can swing a SKU's economics and force a repricing decision.

## Use Payment Terms to Widen Your Float, Not to Chase Sales
Use when: Deciding what payment terms to request from suppliers and how much working capital to hold before scaling ad spend or order volume.
- Map the actual deposit, invoice, shipping, receipt, sale and processor-payout dates. If net 90 begins when production starts and stock arrives in 30 days, 60 days remain before payment; a different contractual start changes this calculation.
- Push for longer net terms as you mature — the source went from net 7 to net 90, citing net 180 as an extreme others use.
- Recognize the most valuable outcome is margin of error: marketing is a daily variable cost you can switch off, a purchase order is not.
- Factor in inventory mistakes (e.g. wrong size mix on rings) that eat your float in the real world.
- Target consignment if you can get it: the factory owns goods until you sell, converting a made-to-order business to near-dropship cash dynamics.
Caveat: Consignment is easiest with categories where the supplier can reclaim/resell inventory (the source notes gold's melt value and Hong Kong fulfillment made it feasible for one business); it isn't universally available.
Caveat: Time from warehouse arrival to sale and payout consumes the remaining headroom; payment terms alone do not establish cash conversion.

## Calculate Reorder Point with Velocity, Lead Time and Safety Stock
Use when: You are setting reorder triggers and planning replenishment shipments for any SKU.

- Set a safety stock buffer to cover sales spikes or freight delays so you keep selling.

- Feed weekly performance and stock-level reviews into the reorder plan, and communicate them to suppliers before problems appear.

Use when: Supplier payment: you are about to send a deposit or balance to a Chinese supplier and choosing the payment method.
- Record product specifications, agreed quality, quantity and shipment terms in the Trade Assurance order.
- Pay through Alibaba's authorized checkout or official bank details for that order. A wire transfer through Alibaba differs from paying a supplier directly off-platform.
- Keep specifications, communications, samples and inspection evidence for a potential dispute.
- Verify current order eligibility, coverage and claim deadlines before payment. The source commenter's off-platform discount proposal sacrifices platform protection and is not a substitute for it.
Caveat: Protection depends on the order and applicable terms; it does not replace supplier vetting or quality inspection.

## Get a Direct Messaging Channel to Avoid Supplier Ghosting
Use when: Supplier communication during quoting, sampling, production or shipping: you need reliable contact to confirm specs, defects or shipment timing.
- When you first make contact through Alibaba chat, immediately request the salesperson's email plus WhatsApp, WeChat or whatever messaging app they use.
- Treat the native Alibaba chat as unreliable for time-sensitive matters: the source says suppliers will go offline without prior notice.
- Keep the direct channel for anything that affects a shipping deadline or defect claim — for example, confirming FedEx tracking or flagging a defective batch before a reorder.
- Confirm key terms in writing on the direct channel as well, so you have a record if the sale rep changes or the Alibaba thread is abandoned.
Caveat: The source does not specify what to do if a supplier refuses to share direct contact details; that may itself be a diligence signal.

## Match Shipping Method to Item Weight and Deadline
Use when: Fulfillment planning and replenishment timing: you are ordering heavy or bulky products and deciding how they get to your warehouse.
- Check unit weight before committing to a product. The source's kitchen-product example weighed 1.2 kg and was stainless steel — too heavy for cheap fast shipping.
- Compare landed timing: sea containers were the affordable option but carried a roughly one-month transit; air cargo was fast but quoted at ~$2,000 for that order, which the author considered expensive when Q4 was already close.
- Order heavy items well ahead of your selling season so you can use sea freight instead of paying a rush premium.
- For large orders where you have a good supplier relationship, ask for premium courier (FedEx/UPS) instead of ePacket. The source's supplier upgraded large orders to FedEx, cutting California delivery from 10–14 days to 4–5 days.
- Note the context: the source author cites ePacket's decline after 2019/COVID, so verify current carrier options rather than designing around ePacket.
Caveat: The $2,000 air quote, one-month sea transit and 4–5 day FedEx times are examples from a specific order and route, not fixed rates.
Caveat: Premium courier availability depends on order size and relationship, not a standard entitlement.

## Negotiate Down Per-Unit Cost Before You Commit Volume
Use when: Supplier negotiation: you have a quote in hand for a replenishment or new-product bulk order and are deciding whether to push back on price.
- Treat the first quote as an opening position, not a final price. The source got a per-product cost from $6 down to $4.80 through direct negotiation.
- Quantify the saving before you decide how hard to push: the author notes that $1.20 reduction saved roughly $3,500 per order at his volume, which is why the effort was worth it.
- Negotiate alongside volume, payment and shipping terms rather than price alone — suppliers may offer freight consolidation or other concessions instead of a price cut if cash flow constrains your order size.
- A commenter with sourcing expertise suggests unused discounts (volume, consolidated freight) can be repurposed creatively — for example, funding a larger order to unlock a lower tier and structuring payments around it — so ask what other levers exist beyond the sticker price.
- Do not present a source's negotiated outcome as your expected result; your leverage depends on volume, category and supplier.
Caveat: The $6→$4.80 and $3,500 figures are from the author's own order and are examples, not benchmarks.
Caveat: Aggressive negotiation can damage a relationship, so weigh it against long-term supply reliability.

## Use Sourcing Agents Deliberately and Vet Them
Use when: Product selection and sourcing strategy: you need customisation, factory-direct access, or better prices than trading-company quotes on Alibaba.
- Consider a sourcing agent when you need to reach the actual manufacturer rather than a reseller, when you want customisation applied to a product, or when quoted prices on Alibaba appear inflated.
- Expect the agent to negotiate directly with the manufacturer on your behalf and potentially unlock better unit economics than you can get alone.
- Vet the agent carefully. The source author states plainly that agents can be great but warns to use a better one, without giving specific selection criteria.
- Because the source does not define selection criteria, apply your own diligence: verify references, sample their work on a small order, and confirm they disclose the manufacturer relationship before you commit volume.
- Do not treat agent savings as guaranteed; the source only asserts potential for better prices and customisation.
Caveat: The source offers no concrete vetting checklist or red flags for agents — only a general warning.
Caveat: An agent adds a margin layer, so confirm the net landed cost is actually lower after their fee.

## Structure Bulk Purchases Around Cash Flow, Not Just Unit Price
Use when: Replenishment and cash-flow planning: you are ordering a recurring SKU several times a year and weighing a larger order tier against working capital.
- The source describes a 30% deposit and 70% pre-shipment balance as one structure; confirm actual milestones and contractual terms for your order.
- Note the commenter's scenario: a client re-ordering the same product multiple times a year at roughly $10 landed cost. An outside party adds capital to increase the order to a full year's supply, unlocking a volume discount while keeping the client's per-unit cost unchanged at $10.
- The client receives the full year of stock, and only pays the outside party as they consume inventory — removing lead time and MOQ on the portion already warehoused, and improving cash flow.
- For an ecommerce operator, this suggests a decision: if a volume tier would strain cash flow, look for investors or distributors willing to fund the gap and be repaid as you sell through, rather than declining the discount entirely.
- This is a commenter's specific client arrangement, not a standard market practice; structure any such deal with clear inventory ownership and repayment terms.
Caveat: This is one commenter's model, not a proven universal method.
Caveat: Splitting inventory ownership introduces legal and accounting complexity that the source does not address.
