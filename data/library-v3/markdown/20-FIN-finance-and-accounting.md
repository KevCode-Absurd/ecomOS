## Calculate true gross margin with the "all in until it's in" COGS rule
Use when: When setting product prices, deciding which sales channels to sell on, or reviewing whether a SKU is actually profitable.
- Identify purchase and attributable inbound/conversion costs that bring inventory to its present location and condition; not every incremental cost qualifies.
- Include supplier purchase price less applicable discounts, inbound freight and non-recoverable duties under the accounting policy.
- Allocate shared inbound costs across SKUs using a documented driver such as weight, volume or value.
- Carry qualifying costs in inventory until the goods are sold, then recognize the related COGS.
- Recalculate landed unit cost when supplier prices, exchange rates or freight change.
Caveat: The source is an accounting practitioner's guidance for statutory reporting; different KPIs such as contribution margin may be more useful internally than statutory COGS for some decisions.
Caveat: Manufacturers have an additional layer of cost above simple bought-in goods; the walkthrough assumes a buy-and-resell model.

## Split packaging into product packaging (COGS) and shipping packaging (expense)
Use when: When coding packaging supplier invoices, or when packaging spend is large enough to distort margin on fragile or gift-oriented products.
- Ask of each packaging item: does it sit on the shelf as part of what the customer buys? The branded retail box, inner tray and any presentation element is COGS.
- Anything whose only job is to survive transit — the plain outer carton, void fill, tape, labels — is a shipping/operating expense.
- When one invoice contains both types, split the line items before posting rather than coding the whole invoice to one account.
- For multipacks or gift sets where the outer box is part of the offer, treat that box as product packaging and keep it in COGS.
- Review the split whenever you change packaging suppliers, since mixed invoices are the common source of misclassification.
Caveat: The retail-box rule comes from physical retail logic the source explicitly extends to ecommerce; there can be genuine grey areas for premium unboxing experiences — pick a defensible treatment and apply it consistently.

## Code customer returns correctly and turn returns into a second sale
Use when: When managing return rates in apparel or other high-return categories, and when designing return-flow offers at the returns step.
- Track return shipping separately using the documented accounting policy.
- Assess returned inventory condition, recoverable value and refurbishment costs; do not automatically capitalize all return labor or post it immediately to COGS.
- At the point of return, consider offering a voucher, discount, alternative item or upsell. The source reports success from encouraging a replacement purchase at the return step, based on the observation that the original item simply wasn't quite right.

- Expect the accounting to get harder when returns generate vouchers or discounts: you must track what was actually sold, at what margin, net of the incentive.
- If returns are material, track return-specific metrics alongside standard gross margin rather than forcing everything into one COGS figure.

## Capture freight and duty against the right purchase order at the time it happens
Use when: When inbound logistics costs are piling up unallocated, or when setting up the purchasing workflow for a growing ecommerce operation.
- When raising a purchase order, record the agreed unit price and verify it against the supplier invoice that later hits the financials.
- At the moment a freight or duty invoice arrives, identify which purchase order(s) it relates to and record the cost against them — do not file it to be sorted out later.
- Avoid the common failure mode the source describes: a growing pile of freight and duty invoices never matched back to the products they belong to.
- When one shipment carries multiple products, allocate the total freight or duty across them, weighting where the cost differs by unit (bulk, weight or value).
- Treat this as a people-and-process discipline first; software helps execute it but does not replace defining who captures what and when.
Caveat: Spreadsheets can work at small scale, but the source advises inventory management software as the business scales and allocations become weighted.

## Troubleshoot a slipping P&L by reading upward, not sideways
Use when: Monthly or weekly review when net profit, contribution margin or gross margin misses target and you need to decide whether to cut spend, fix returns, reprice or renegotiate freight.
- Build the P&L as a stacked ladder: Revenue, Cost of Delivery, Gross Margin, Marketing (MEA), Contribution Margin, OPEX, EBITDA, Interest, Net Profit.
- Express every line as a percentage of revenue as well as dollars, and track month-over-month movement rather than a single snapshot.
- Apply the fault rule: if a target line is missed, the cause is everything above it. Missed EBITDA = OPEX, MEA or cost of delivery. Missed contribution margin = MEA or cost of delivery. Missed gross margin = cost of delivery or revenue (both gross and net).
- Chase the specific lever inside the guilty line: refunds, discounts, shipping collected at checkout, landed cost, pick/pack, transaction fees, or media spend.
- Set your contribution margin target by working up from the bottom: net profit target + forecast OPEX = required contribution margin, in both percent and dollars. A $1M net target with $1M OPEX requires $2M contribution margin.
- Check whether revenue growth came from gross revenue or from a shrinking refund/discount allowance, since accounting software usually only surfaces net.
Caveat: Contribution margin is used loosely — the source defines CM1 as product margin, CM2 as gross margin, CM3 as the revenue-minus-cost-of-delivery-minus-marketing figure; confirm which a report means.
Caveat: Source is an agency operator's framing at 7–9 figure brands; percentage bands are illustrative, not benchmarks.

## Separate gross and net revenue to find hidden profit
Use when: When revenue moved between two periods and you cannot explain it from marketing or OPEX, or when you are deciding where to hunt for margin before cutting ad spend.
- Pull gross revenue, refunds/chargebacks, discount allowance and shipping collected separately, per period. Shopify shows this gross-to-net view; many platforms and accounting tools (Xero, QuickBooks) show only net or a cash read.
- Compare the same gross number across months and check whether the net movement came from refunds or discounts rather than new demand.
- Treat each line as a lever: fewer refunds, fewer discount codes redeemed, and more shipping collected at checkout all increase net revenue without extra traffic. The source claims most stores under-collect shipping at checkout.
- If a gross-to-net view is not flowing into your accounting software, have your bookkeeper reconcile it into a custom report so decisions are not made off a net-only figure.
- Investigate refund-rate spikes by product and cohort before assuming a media problem, especially in fashion where the source says refund rates have been seen from 10% up to 80%.
Caveat: Gross-to-net detail may require custom reporting work; the source notes this gap exists in most accounting stacks.

## Recalculate contribution and break-even acquisition cost before discounting
Use when: Pricing and offer decisions — planning a promo, a bundle, a gift-with-purchase, or a clearance push, and setting the media efficiency target that promo must hit.
- Calculate pre-ad contribution per order as net revenue less landed product cost, fulfillment, payment fees and expected return costs, without double-counting discounts or refunds.
- With $100 net revenue and $60 variable costs, contribution is $40; break-even CAC is $40 and revenue ROAS is 100/40 = 2.5.
- At $70 discounted net revenue and unchanged $60 costs, contribution is $10: break-even CAC is $10 and ROAS is 70/10 = 7.
- Compare a bundle or gift-with-purchase using actual basket costs and measured incremental conversion; a $4 gift does not guarantee another $100 sale.
- Check clearance cash recovery separately from profitable ongoing acquisition.
Caveat: Break-even here excludes fixed overhead and target profit; set a lower operating CAC ceiling.
Caveat: Shipping, fees and returns can change with the offer; use store-specific assumptions.

## Calculate pre-marketing contribution using explicit delivery costs
Use when: Before setting marketing KPIs on a product, deciding whether to keep or cut a SKU, or comparing your margin to competitor pricing.
- Start from revenue excluding pass-through taxes and recorded discounts/refunds.
- Subtract landed product cost, fulfillment, outbound shipping, payment fees and incremental expected return costs exactly once.

- Use pre-ad contribution dollars to fund acquisition, overhead and target profit; calculate separately for each SKU and channel.

## Split variable and fixed marketing costs inside the P&L
Use when: Structuring your P&L for monthly review, or deciding whether to bring creative production in-house versus using agencies and freelancers.
- Put media spend, influencer payments, events and out-of-home into MEA — anything variable with revenue.
- Classify content production by whether it is fixed or flexes with demand. A permanent in-house team is OPEX.

## Waterfall pricing from bottom to top
Use when: Launching a new product, repricing an existing one, or evaluating whether a hero SKU can carry paid acquisition.
- Set a target post-ad contribution rate on net revenue, allowing room for overhead and profit.
- Estimate dollar costs per order: product, shipping, fulfillment, CAC and expected return losses.
- For fixed dollar costs C and percentage charges f, solve net price P = C / (1 - target contribution rate - f). The denominator must be positive.
- Use one currency and revenue basis; do not add a percentage directly to dollar costs or double-count returns.
- Compare the required price with competing offers and test willingness to pay before committing to the SKU.
- Stress-test CAC, return rates and fulfillment costs after launch.
Caveat: The source estimates roughly 40% of startups price using keystone, a rough impression rather than a study.
Caveat: CAC estimates for new products are uncertain; revisit the model once real acquisition data exists.

## Read product-level economics to beat maximize-conversions bidding
Use when: Campaign structure and budget allocation decisions when a low-CPA product is absorbing spend while a higher-margin product under-sells.
- Build a product-level table: retail price, cost of goods, gross margin dollars, CAC, contribution margin per sale.
- Source example: a $90 hoodie with $21 COGS gives $69 gross margin; at $45 CAC it yields $24 contribution. A cheaper t-shirt with $27 gross margin at $15 CAC yields $12 — half the contribution despite a lower CPA.
- Recognise that maximize-conversions bidding on Meta, Google or TikTok will send spend to the lowest-CPA item, which here is the less profitable product. Switching to maximize conversion value also fails here, because the hoodie has the worse ROAS.
- Fix it structurally: segment campaigns by product category using product-level economics, or split budgets so high-contribution products get deliberate spend.
- Apply a cannibalisation test: if a SKU acquires at an implausibly low cost, it may sell organically without media. Cutting spend there can lift its contribution per order and free budget for products that genuinely need paid support to clear.
- Revisit allocation for omni-channel and large brands where in-store or organic demand inflates apparent efficiency.
Caveat: The dollar figures are a single illustrative example, not category benchmarks.
Caveat: Organic-versus-paid attribution is a judgement call; avoid cutting spend on a SKU without a holdout test.

## Geo-lift testing to measure true incremental spend
Use when: When platform ROAS is untrustworthy and you need to decide how much more (or less) to spend in paid media, or validate a channel's real contribution before scaling budget.
- Choose a control region and a treatment region with reasonably comparable demand and seasonality, and define the test window up front.
- Hold out spend in the control region for the test period rather than relying on platform-reported lift.
- Build a predicted counterfactual revenue for the control region from its pre-test history (and, where possible, comparable regions).
- Measure the difference between actual revenue and predicted counterfactual revenue as the incremental effect of the spend.
- Feed the incremental result back into CAC and break-even modelling rather than into platform-reported CPA.
- Treat this as the answer to the source's core argument that attribution is unreliable and the gap between the ad account and the bank account is where brands get into trouble.
Caveat: Region comparability and seasonality are the main failure modes; the source gives the design but no minimum viable region count or duration.
Caveat: Results are estimates, not proof of channel-level causation, especially in small samples.

## Forecast new and returning revenue as separate models
Use when: Annual or quarterly planning, budget setting, or when deciding how hard to push acquisition versus retention investment.
- Build two separate forecast models. New customer revenue: forecast marketing spend and expected new-customer CAC, then derive new customers and multiply by new-customer AOV.
- Returning customer revenue: apply historical cohort decay rates to existing customer cohorts and layer on expected product launches or restock events.
- Keep new-customer AOV and gross profit separate from blended AOV, since returning customers carry higher AOV and inflate blended figures.

- Use the split to decide whether acquisition can be subsidised by repeat profit, and how much.
Caveat: The revenue split is described by the source as typical for a business of that size; it is an example, not a benchmark.
Caveat: Cohort decay rates degrade if assortment, pricing or market conditions change; refresh them regularly.

## Fix CAC measurement before setting CAC targets
Use when: Setting acquisition targets, reviewing agency performance, or diagnosing why a 'healthy' blended CAC is not producing profit.
- Calculate CAC as total advertising spend over the period divided by total new customers acquired in that period — not platform CPA, which runs on attributed numbers and typically flatters the result.
- Refuse channel-level CAC breakdowns; the source argues attribution makes per-channel deltas unreliable.
- Pair CAC against gross profit on first purchase, not blended AOV times a possibly-wrong gross margin percentage. New customers typically have lower AOV and heavier discount-driven front-end offers.
- Worked check: a $100 blended AOV at an assumed 70% gross margin looks like $70 GP and a comfortable $35 CAC — but if the new-customer AOV is really $90 and front-end discounting compresses margin, true first-purchase GP might be $50, making the CAC target wrong.
- Answer 'what is a good CAC?' as a non-question: a $100 CAC against $10,000 first-order GP is excellent; a $100 CAC against $20 GP is a loss. Judge CAC only relative to first-purchase gross profit.
Caveat: Attribution-free CAC is a deliberate simplification; channel decisions then rely on experiments such as geo-lift.
Caveat: Requires clean new-versus-returning customer tagging in your platform and analytics.

## Separate new-customer and returning-customer profit contribution
Use when: Deciding budget split between acquisition and retention, or when a founder wants to increase ad spend but the profit source is unclear.
- Split monthly revenue into new-customer revenue and returning-customer revenue.
- For new customers: new revenue minus ad spend, minus cost of delivery/gross margin, equals new-customer profit contribution.
- For returning customers: returning revenue minus gross margin cost, minus retention tooling (email/SMS platform) and any retention agency, equals returning-customer profit contribution.
- Source illustration: $140k new revenue minus $50k ad spend at 50% gross margin gives ~$20k; $60k returning revenue minus ~$2.5k tooling and agency gives ~$27.5k — the smaller revenue stream produced the larger profit.
- Use the returning-customer profit pool to judge how aggressively you can subsidise new-customer acquisition, and whether increasing new-customer spend is self-funding.
- Route the result into retention planning: repeat purchase is driven by product, experience and brand, not by media spend.

## Measure 90/180-day LTGP, not open-ended LTV
Use when: Setting maximum allowable CAC for CPG, fashion or subscription models where first-order profitability is negative or thin.
- Cap the measurement window — the source recommends 90-day or 180-day windows because LTV keeps rising as you extend the horizon, even where it asymptotes.
- Compute lifetime gross profit (LTGP), not revenue-based LTV. Many tools report LTV as revenue; in e-commerce the distinction matters because software-style LTV is often close to gross profit while e-commerce LTV is not.
- Compare LTGP against CAC to establish payback. The source notes most agencies run a 3–6 month CAC payback period, and CPG brands frequently lose money on first purchase.
- Expect competitors with larger repeat value to outbid you at auction; if your LTGP is thin, paying their CAC level is not viable.
- Use the window-consistent LTGP to set the ceiling bid, then check it against the 90/180-day actuals before scaling.
Caveat: Window choice changes the answer; compare cohorts measured over the same window only.
Caveat: Cohort LTGP is backward-looking and can overstate value for brands with changing retention.

## Ring-fence OPEX to protect margin while scaling
Use when: Budget review when revenue is growing but net profit is flat in dollars, or when deciding whether to hire, add software or upsize an office.
- Define OPEX as anything not in cost of delivery or marketing. The three big buckets are people, software and office/fulfillment space.
- Keep true fulfillment costs — 3PL, warehouse, and warehouse staff doing pick/pack/ship — inside cost of delivery. Allocate warehouse staff per order (source example: $400/day ÷ 100 packages = $4/order) and have your bookkeeper tag and reclassify them into a custom P&L so gross margin stays accurate.
- Keep everyone not directly fulfilling orders (head of marketing, head of operations) in OPEX.
- Model the intended scaling shape: OPEX flat, marketing and cost of delivery rising with revenue, so profit expands as a percentage. Watch for OPEX rising faster than revenue, which leaves profit flat.
- Be sceptical of 'reinvest in the business' leading to hiring and software that do not drive revenue. Marketing is the revenue lever; people set the skill ceiling but poor hires inflate cost without output.
- Put interest expense below EBITDA, never in OPEX — it is financing leverage, and buyers evaluating EBITDA zero out loans.
Caveat: The source argues OPEX should stay near-fixed, which assumes no step changes in headcount or systems; treat it as a discipline target, not an absolute.
Caveat: The source concedes seven-figure brands often lack the skill to hire top talent, so the advice is to delay heavy people investment rather than never hire.

## Measurement pyramid: platform metrics, finance-grade KPIs, incrementality
Use when: Setting up or auditing which marketing metrics you report on and make budget decisions from
- Bottom tier: in-platform ROAS, CPA, CTR, multi-touch attribution tools (e.g., Triple Whale). Fast feedback but lowest reliability — use only for directional campaign/ad feedback inside a single platform.
- Middle tier: finance-grade KPIs such as acquisition MER, blended MER, profit contribution and CAC — calculated from actual new-customer counts, actual new-customer revenue, and actual ad spend, not platform-attributed numbers.
- Top tier: incrementality testing (geo holdouts) — most reliable but slow, can only be run infrequently, and needs data-science capability.
- Accept the trade-off as you move up: reliability improves but speed decreases. Finance-grade metrics need longer windows before you act on their movements.
- The source says incrementality testing is likely not applicable to ~80% of operators; it matters mainly for businesses spending over roughly $10M/month, omni-channel or multi-market.
- Never compare ROAS across platforms (e.g., Google campaign vs Meta campaign) — different attribution models and funnel positions make the numbers non-comparable.
Caveat: The 80%/20% and $10M/month figures are the source's heuristics for who needs incrementality testing, not a hard rule.
Caveat: The source explicitly warns against building DIY geo holdouts without proper data-science test design.

## Why platform ROAS misleads: all attribution is correlation
Use when: Interpreting channel-level ROAS reports or defending/attacking a channel's budget based on platform numbers
- Recognise that an ad click followed by a purchase is correlation, not proven causation — the source's analogy is ice cream sales and swimming deaths both rising in summer.
- Account for the funnel sequence: billboard/TV/influencer creates awareness, a Meta ad 'closes' the sale, and only the Meta click gets attributed. Top-of-funnel gets no credit.
- Look for the TikTok→Meta→Google pattern: TikTok drives new-market impressions without much click-off, Meta retargets and captures the demand in-platform, Google captures branded search. Increasing TikTok spend raises Meta and Google ROAS, not TikTok's.
- Do not let a C-suite reader use raw cross-platform ROAS to cut TikTok and pile into Google — that removes top-of-funnel generation, and bottom-of-funnel stops working without it.
- Use platform ROAS only within a platform (e.g., two Meta campaigns side by side) for directional reads.
Caveat: The TikTok→Meta→Google dynamic is explicitly framed as a current, possibly time-dependent observation for some large retail brands, not a universal law.

## The profit frontier: solve for the next best dollar
Use when: Deciding incremental budget allocation across channels or whether to pull budget from one channel into another
- Frame the question as: if we spend an extra $10,000 next month, where does it get the best incremental impact?
- For each primary channel (Meta, Google, TikTok), estimate the revenue return of an additional $10,000 on top of current spend — ideally via controlled incrementality tests, or intuitively over time for seven-figure brands.
- Run the inverse too: could $10,000 pulled from Google and reallocated to Meta return better (e.g., 2x on the marginal Google dollar vs 4x on new-customer revenue on Meta)?
- Push each channel up to the point where it makes no more profit past that marginal dollar — the profit frontier is where incremental acquisition MER reaches break-even, and that's where you stop.
- Only after reaching the profit frontier on your three primary channels should you add tertiary channels.
- Avoid spreading spend across many tertiary channels before the primaries are maxed out.

## Cash conversion cycle explains why ecommerce can't scale arbitrarily fast
Use when: Setting growth targets, planning purchase orders, or evaluating whether rapid ad-spend scaling is fundable
- Formula: cash conversion cycle = days inventory outstanding + days sales outstanding − days payable outstanding.
- Work through the compounding: if $50 gross profit lets you buy 11 units next cycle and yields $55, which buys 12, inventory purchasing grows only incrementally off prior gross profit.
- Add supplier lead time — e.g., a 90-day lead time with full upfront payment means you must fund inventory 90 days before selling it, potentially forcing a 90-day stockout while waiting for cash.
- Levers to fix it: negotiate better supplier terms (e.g., only 20% upfront, paying the balance on shipment — the source's example reduces stockout from 90 days to ~20), reduce lead time via a different manufacturer, or use inventory financing.
- Compare financing cost, repayment timing, guarantees and collateral in actual quotes; stress-test slower sales before borrowing.
Caveat: The source describes spend rising from $20k to $200k per month, a tenfold increase, with substantial inventory financing; this is an anecdote, not a benchmark.
Caveat: Growth capacity depends on working capital, supplier terms, lead times and margins; no universal annual growth ceiling follows from this example.

## Build returning-customer forecasts from cohort repeat rates
Use when: Forecasting returning-customer revenue for next month's budget or board plan
- Build a cohort table of new customers acquired each month going back to your earliest data (e.g., Jan 2021 onward).
- Derive the repeat rate by customer age: e.g., 7% in month 1, 6%, 4%, 2%, 1%, tapering to a small asymptote.
- For each cohort, take its original new-customer count, apply the average repeat rate for the month it is about to enter (e.g., month 32 ≈ 0.1%), then multiply by returning-customer AOV (e.g., $100 → 100 × 0.1% × $100 = $1).

- Product COGS per order = total product COGS / total orders (example: $62).
- Fulfillment per order = total fulfillment / total orders (example: $71).

- Keep customer counts, order counts and unit counts distinct — orders exceed customers, and units exceed orders when baskets average more than one item.
Caveat: Blended per-order figures hide product-mix differences and seasonality.
Caveat: AOV varies by promotion and channel; a trailing average may hide a recent shift.

## The Core Unit Economics Stack: CAC, AOV, CM, LTV and Their Ratios
Use when: Setting up your ecommerce financial model or explaining unit economics to your team before scaling spend.

- Treat deteriorating unit economics as a hard stop: at 10x size, worsening economics can drive profit to zero or negative.

Caveat: LTV:CAC of 1.0 is not acceptable by the presenter's standard because it leaves nothing to cover overhead.

## Ecom Gross Margin vs Contribution Margin: Two Different Buckets
Use when: Building your P&L model or diagnosing why margin differs between orders and shipping destinations.
- Bucket one - product costs: manufacturing, import freight, duties and taxes, i.e. everything to get product to the fulfillment center and ready to ship. In the source example this was 27% of a $175 order, or $47.
- Bucket one result is the ecom gross margin: revenue minus product costs ($175 - $47 = $128 in the example).
- Bucket two - fulfillment costs: pick and pack, materials, 3PL charges and shipping. These vary order by order, with shipping usually the largest line.
- Contribution margin = revenue minus both buckets; in the example $175 - $47 - $40 of fulfillment = $83, and CAC should be at or below that $83.
- Use the split diagnostically: product costs should be stable and improve with scale/supply-chain fixes, while fulfillment costs need per-destination or per-weight scrutiny.
Caveat: This naming convention (ecom gross margin) is the source's, and differs from standard GAAP gross margin presentation.
Caveat: Fulfillment cost variability means blended averages can hide unprofitable shipping lanes.

## Splitting COGS Into Product and Fulfillment Buckets for Promo Modeling
Use when: Building a promo P&L for Black Friday, bundles or free shipping tests, or recalculating contribution margin per order.
- Product COGS bucket: manufacturing cost plus duties and taxes, packaging, and inbound freight to the fulfillment center - in the example $15 manufacturing plus $6 other = $21.
- Fulfillment COGS bucket: shipping and packing ($12), warehouse storage and pick costs ($3), and merchant/payment processing fees ($4) = $19 in the example.
- Net AOV should be reduced for returns and refunds before computing anything else; the source notes losing roughly 5-10% of gross revenue to returns/refunds in many verticals ($135 to $124 in the example).
- Contribution margin = net AOV minus both buckets ($124 - $21 - $19 = $84), then subtract CAC to get CM-after-CAC ($84 - $75 = $9).
- When modeling a promo, hold product COGS fixed and adjust only the lines that actually move (merchant fees on lower revenue, marginally higher shipping for extra units in the same box).
Caveat: The 5-10% returns figure is described as typical for many verticals, not universal - use your own returns data.
Caveat: Promo best practice of not offering refunds or returns is a modeling assumption you must decide on deliberately.

## Three-Statement Model as the Diagnostic for a Profitable-but-Cash-Poor Store
Use when: Investigating why your store's bank balance declines in months when the P&L shows profit.

Caveat: Positive net income is described as often misleading for startups; free cash flow clarifies what is really happening.

## Balance Sheet Cash Traps: Inventory, Capitalized Software, Quarterly Taxes
Use when: Planning a buying season, an engineering spend, or a tax payment quarter and forecasting whether cash can support it.

## Reading the Cash Flow Statement: Operating, Investing, Financing
Use when: Reviewing monthly cash movements and classifying spend the way a lender or investor will read it.
- Operating section starts with net income from the P&L, then adjusts: accounts receivable up = cash negative (customers owe you); accounts payable up = cash positive (you still owe suppliers); depreciation is stripped back out as non-cash; inventory change is booked from the balance sheet movement.
- Investing section holds long-term asset purchases - buying assets, investing in the software platform, buying shares in another company. Salary paid one month is an expense, not an investment.
- Financing section holds anything about capital structure: owner distributions, loans taken or repaid, outside capital in. Not day-to-day operations.
- Free cash flow = operating cash flow minus investing cash flow; it is the money left after internal operations are fully funded, with no further obligations other than financing.
- Use FCF as the decision number for what you can do next: pay down debt, take a distribution, or fund the next inventory buy.
Caveat: The definition given is operating cash flow minus investing cash flow - some practitioners compute it other ways; be explicit about which you use.
Caveat: In the example, one month showed net income of only $140 while cash flow was $272, so never substitute one for the other.

## Free cash flow vs net income: inventory and tax timing as cash drivers
Use when: Monthly or quarterly cash review for your store, especially when net income looks positive but your bank balance is falling, or you are deciding how much inventory to buy for the next selling season.
- Pull the cash flow statement alongside the income statement, not instead of it. Attribution: ecommerce finance commentary treats the cash flow statement as the operational view of the business.
- Compare the change in inventory line to net income. If inventory rose (e.g., 335 to 448 in the source example), that is a cash outflow even though it may sit as an asset; if inventory fell because you sold through it, that is a cash inflow.
- Check tax payments as a separate line. In the source example, months with tax payments showed cash out, and months with no payment effectively conserved cash — the payment timing, not the underlying tax liability, moved the number.

## Calculate contribution margin per order and post-marketing contribution
Use when: Setting or reviewing product-level and order-level profitability, or diagnosing why a store that looks profitable at gross margin is not producing operating income.
- Start at gross revenue (product sales plus tax collected where applicable) and subtract discounts and returns, then add shipping income you charge customers. Result: net revenue, which is effectively your AOV after discounts and returns for the period.
- Split COGS into product cost (manufacturing plus import freight plus duties/taxes to get product to the warehouse) and keep it separate from fulfillment cost (pick and pack, packing materials, 3PL charges, shipping to customer). A generic P&L lumps these together; splitting them is what lets you see which line is moving.
- Compute gross margin = net revenue minus product COGS. In the source case study it ran about 70–75%.
- Compute contribution margin = net revenue minus product COGS minus fulfillment. In the source example, $134 net revenue with $64 contribution margin, just under 50%.
- Compute CAC as total marketing spend divided by first-time purchases, then post-marketing contribution = contribution margin minus CAC. Source example: $22 and $12 post-marketing contribution per order, i.e. profitable on the first order at roughly a 1.5 contribution-margin-to-CAC ratio.
- Check merchant/credit card processing fees and include them — the source uses about 2.75% of revenue as a working assumption.
- Finally subtract operating expense per order to reach operating income. In the source example opex was about $8 and $6 per order and operating income was 11% and 5%, i.e. about $15 and $6 profit per unit.
- Remember the leverage: small per-order changes (knocking a couple of dollars out of one line) multiplied across thousands of orders is the difference between tens of thousands of losses and tens of thousands of profit.
Caveat: Gross margin is the highest figure, contribution margin lower, post-marketing contribution lowest — mixing the terms (calling gross margin contribution margin) causes wrong decisions.
Caveat: All percentages in the source are one case-study supplement brand in a specific period; do not treat them as targets.
Caveat: After the first order, returning customers generally carry little or no marketing cost, so first-order profitability is the ideal state — the source calls businesses with strong first-order profit exceptional.

## Treat discount and CAC as one acquisition cost pool
Use when: Planning next quarter's promotional calendar and ad budget, or deciding whether a deeper discount or more ad spend will grow profit for the whole store.
- Build the scenario grid with the same ad spend in every column, then vary the discount. Source case: $100,000 spend, $1.50 per click blended, ~66,667 sessions in all scenarios, undiscounted price $200, COGS $75 ($50 product + $25 fulfillment).

## Price prepaid bundles by comparing margin loss against retention gain
Use when: Deciding whether to launch or expand 3/6/12-order prepaid bundles, subscribe-and-save tiers, or any offer that trades discount depth for committed repeat orders.
- Model three segments side by side: one-time buyers, a three-order prepaid, and a six-order prepaid. Source assumptions: $80 undiscounted AOV, $19 product COGS, $15 fulfillment, $34 total COGS, $46 gross profit and 58% gross margin for one-time buyers.
- Apply the discount and record the margin hit: 25% off for three orders gives ~$60 collected vs $80, gross profit falls to $26; 30% off for six orders gives ~$56 collected, gross profit falls to $22 and margin to about 39%. The source notes gross profit is roughly cut in half at the six-order tier.
- Expect most operators to conclude at this point that the discount is not viable. That is the moment to add the retention data rather than stopping.
- Model retention on a cohort of 1,000 new customers over 12 months. One-time buyers: about 10% reach order 2, 5% reach order 3, 3% reach order 4 → 1.2 average orders per customer in year one.
- Three-order prepaid: 100% retention for three consecutive months, then roughly 50% roll off and 50% re-enroll, repeating → about 5.6 average orders in 12 months. Six-order prepaid: 100% retention for six months, then ~50% churn/re-enroll → about 9 average orders.
- Multiply lifetime orders by gross profit per order. One-time: 1.2 × $46 = ~$54 LTV. Three-order prepaid: 5.6 × $26 = ~$146 LTV (about +169%, +$92). Six-order prepaid: 9 × $22 = ~$198 LTV (about 4x, +$144 per enrolling customer).
- Then model the mix effect, because only a fraction of customers will take prepaid. Source sandbox: shifting from 100% one-time to 85% one-time / 10% three-order / 5% six-order raises weighted-average LTV from $54 to $71, about +30% on the same customer base, flowing to gross profit and operating income.
- Consider prepaid as a working-capital tool as well as an LTV tool: collecting cash upfront can let you finance the inventory you will ship to that customer with their own money (negative net working capital), which the source notes is common in B2B SaaS and education but rare in ecommerce.
Caveat: The retention curves (10% to order 2, 50% prepaid roll-off and re-enroll) are described as realistic for the presenter's merchants, not guaranteed results.
Caveat: LTV figures ignore the marketing cost to acquire those customers, so a prepaid tier with lower cash collected can produce lower LTV-to-CAC than a one-time buyer if acquisition cost is unchanged.
Caveat: Prepaid cash is partly a liability: you owe fulfillment over future months, so spent prepaid cash is not free profit.
Caveat: The source also notes increasing discounts over the customer lifetime (e.g. 10% first, 15% second, 20% third, 25% fourth+) as an alternative to flat subscribe-and-save, because flat incentives get customers who subscribe for the one-time offer and cancel.

## Define gross margin, contribution margin and post-marketing contribution consistently
Use when: Setting up your store's reporting definitions, briefing an analyst or account manager, or comparing your margins against others' quoted figures.
- Define gross margin as revenue minus product cost only — the profit made at the product level, and the highest of the three percentages. Example given: sell for $100, cost $20 to make and get to the warehouse, gross margin about $80.
- Define contribution margin as revenue minus product cost minus fulfillment — everything needed to sell and deliver the product to the customer's door. The source treats this as the profit you have available to spend on marketing and other operating expenses, and the margin you work back from when optimizing the business.
- Define post-marketing contribution as contribution margin minus marketing spend — the test of whether you actually made a profit after producing, delivering and acquiring the customer. The source calls this the lowest and a very important view of whether the business generates profit.
- Keep the terms separate in your reports even though people use gross margin and contribution margin interchangeably; the source recommends defining them according to how you run your business and then measuring them as unique ideas.
- Use contribution margin as the ceiling on what you can spend to acquire a customer. In the source case, $64 contribution margin means you have roughly $64 to spend before breaking even on the product.
- Interpret the CAC ratio accordingly: contribution margin to CAC of about 1.5 on the first order in the source example meant profitable on the first order, so all subsequent orders were effectively pure profit because they carry little or no marketing.
Caveat: The source notes these terms are commonly used interchangeably in the industry, so when comparing to external figures, verify which definition the other party is using.
Caveat: Contribution margin excluding marketing can look healthy while post-marketing contribution is negative, which is why the source insists both be tracked.

## Build a Linked Acquisition-to-Retention Model for Transactional + Subscription Mix
Use when: Financial planning for a store selling both one-time purchases and subscriptions (subscription box, supplements, consumables) where you need to model growth, breakeven and cash needs.

- Model transactional repeat purchasing with a rolling return rate: divide returning customers last month by total customers acquired in the trailing 6 months. Source's example uses ~3.3% of the 6-month customer base returning in any given month.

## Forecast Inventory with Lead Time and Cycle Time Before You Model Cash
Use when: Replenishment planning for any physical-product store to avoid stockouts and to see the true cash hit of inventory buys.
- Pull forecast order volumes from the demand model to know how many units each month must be fulfilled.
- Set a lead time (source scenario: 3 months from order to receipt at the fulfillment center) and a cycle time (source scenario: 4 months of sell-through).
- Order enough today so that inventory arriving after the lead time lasts through the cycle: order in month 1, receive in month 4, cover 4 months, then place the next PO.
- Roll starting inventory minus orders each month, adding received POs when they land, to project inventory balances.
- Cost each PO using a weighted-average product COGS across your order mix — the source weights transactional COGS ($7.70) and subscription COGS ($6.50) by order share to get ~$7.20 average.
- Scale the PO cadence to reality (the source models quarterly-ish POs, but monthly POs may be needed) and stress-test demand because you are committing cash months before revenue.
Caveat: Lead and cycle times are supply-chain specific; the source's 3-month lead and 4-month cycle are illustrative.
Caveat: A single-SKU simplification is used — more SKUs multiply complexity substantially.

## Build a Mini Cash Flow Statement to Find Working Capital Needs and Runway
Use when: Deciding how much funding or cash buffer the store needs and when, especially for inventory-heavy CPG or subscription-box businesses.
- In a simplified bridge, start with net income, add back inventory COGS expensed, then subtract all actual inventory supplier cash payments, whether goods have sold or not.
- Add other non-cash and working-capital adjustments separately. Do not also subtract inventory/payables movements already captured by the supplier-payment bridge.
- Add any starting capital (source scenario begins with $500k raised) and roll the cash balance month by month.
- Expect cash to be choppier than net income: the source's example shows cash going negative around month 8 and again needing a large inventory outlay shortly after.
- Use the trough of the cash curve to size the raise: in the source's example, the business needed roughly $1-1.5M cumulative to reach profitability even though operating income turned positive earlier.
- Separately note the store's revenue run rate (last month's revenue x 12) as a real-time pacing metric alongside trailing 12-month actuals, and be explicit which one you quote.
Caveat: This mini cash flow ignores other assets and liabilities by design — it is not a full three-statement model.
Caveat: Capital need figures are the source's scenario and depend heavily on lead times, payment terms and growth rate.

## Reconcile Your Model: Gross Profit Percentage Must Match Unit Economics
Use when: After building or editing a financial model, before presenting numbers to investors or using them for decisions.

## Calculate churn against the prior period's base, not the current month
Use when: Reporting or setting targets for monthly churn on a subscription store, and auditing any churn number a team or agency has provided.
- Gather three figures per period: starting subscribers, new subscribers added in the period, and cancellations in the period.
- Divide cancellations by the subscriber base from the prior period — not by the ending or current total. Source example: 100 customers in month one, 50 new in month two, 9 cancellations in month two → churn is 9/100 = 9%, not 9/141 = 6%.
- Roll the formula forward across periods to see the trend: the source example moves 9%, 13%, 17%, 11%, 16% across months.
- Apply the identical logic annually: source example with 53 customers in year one, 31 new, and 18 cancellations gives 18/53 = 34% annual churn.
- Note that a growing subscriber count can coexist with high churn — the total hides the leak, which is why you need the split inputs.
Caveat: Without new-customer and cancellation counts you cannot calculate churn; a simple beginning-versus-ending subscriber count is insufficient.
Caveat: The source's own example shows monthly churn in the ~9–17% range and annual churn ~20–40%, which look wildly different but are two different periods, not a contradiction.

## Calculate CAC payback from cohorts, not from flat monthly gross profit
Use when: Deciding how fast a subscription or replenishment offer repays acquisition spend before scaling the media budget.
- Estimate payback the naive way first to see the size of the error: source example takes $28 monthly gross profit against $90 CAC and reports 3.2 months.
- Layer in retention by order: source high-churn cohort of 3,500 buyers retained 100% at order one, 75% at order two and 50% at order three, with most customers gone by order 18.
- Multiply CAC by the full original cohort count to get total marketing outlay — 3,500 × $90 = $315,000 in the source example.
- Sum gross profit across periods using each period's surviving customer count, then find when cumulative gross profit crosses total CAC. Source high-churn example breaks even around month 8, not 3.2.
- Repeat for a lower-churn cohort: source software-style example retained 45% at 12 months and 15% at 24 months, breaking even around month 9 against a 7.1-month naive estimate — roughly a 27% error versus roughly 150% in the high-churn case.
- Scenario-test CAC itself: the source notes a move from $125 to $225 CAC shifted breakeven from about month 5 to about month 11.
Caveat: The source warns payback is 'almost always' calculated incorrectly and that errors typically make real payback 20–100% (sometimes 200%) longer than reported.
Caveat: Source numbers are worked examples, not universal benchmarks; rates vary by category and retention curve.
Caveat: If your subscription allows skipping or irregular order frequency, count payback in number of orders rather than months.

## Convert monthly and annual churn on a consistent survival basis

## Blended vs paid CAC: calculate both and know why
Use when: You run paid ads plus organic channels and need to decide whether you can profitably increase ad budget.
- Collect monthly ad spend, total new customers, and the split of new customers attributed to paid vs non-paid channels using Google Analytics, Google Ads and Facebook Ads Manager.
- Compute blended CAC: total ad spend ÷ total new customers. Source example: $6,500 ÷ 134 = $48.51 per new customer.
- Compute paid CAC: ad spend ÷ paid-attributed new customers. Source example: $6,500 ÷ 101 = $64.
- Always exclude returning customers from the denominator — including them produces a meaningless CAC, since CAC is about acquiring the first purchase.
- Compare paid CAC to LTV ($69 in the example — yes, profitable, but 'barely').
- Ask whether organic traffic is scalable; the source notes paid channels can scale from $500/day to $20,000/day quickly, while organic often cannot — so paid CAC is the number that constrains growth.
Caveat: Channel splits require tracking setup; not every store will have it.
Caveat: Blended CAC flatters performance when a large share of new customers come from non-scalable organic sources such as friends and family.

Use when: Deciding how far to scale media this quarter or evaluating whether a spend increase is realistic.
- Define AMER as new-customer revenue divided by ad spend, and build a regression over historical months relating spend to AMER.
- Include a seasonal effect and an AOV consideration so a December curve is not applied to April.
- Derive the slope: if you spend X, you get efficiency Y; if you spend 2X, model what happens to Y rather than assuming Y stays fixed.
- Combine the resulting new-customer revenue with the cohort-specific LTV model to produce an existing-customer revenue forecast.
- Have one side of the team building the model and another tracking daily actuals to expectation so you learn by day five whether the plan is holding.
- Verify current AMER and platform definitions if tools change, since reporting fields and metric names shift.
Caveat: The source describes regression modeling as a historical relationship, not a causal engine; incrementality studies are needed for true causal effects.
Caveat: Regressions on short, volatile datasets are unreliable.

## Incrementality Testing New Channels with DMA Hold-Outs
Use when: Adding a new channel or a new spend level beyond your core platform and needing a defensible effect estimate.
- Have a data science team model DMAs (or geo sub-markets) by revenue similarity to create comparable control and variable regions.
- In one region, turn spend off; in the comparable region, keep or turn spend on.
- Compare the revenue gap between the regions — that gap is your estimate of the media's actual effect in that market.
- Establish a benchmark factor, the ratio between the platform-reported result and the incrementality result (e.g. Meta reports 1, incrementality shows 1.2), and apply that multiplier to daily platform numbers for decision-making.
- Add one new channel at a time with its own isolation test so you can build a factor for each place you go; a mature multi-channel mix makes disentangling single-channel effects much harder.
- Keep the test design honest: contamination between test and control DMAs biases results.
Caveat: Geo hold-outs produce estimates with noise, not exact causation.
Caveat: Source's claim that multi-channel mix is nearly impossible to attribute causally is an opinion but consistent with incremental testing practice.

## Aged Inventory Tiers and Liquidation Funnels
Use when: When you have aging SKUs sitting in the warehouse and need to decide whether to discount, liquidate, or hold.
- Tier inventory A, B, C, D by days outstanding; aim for days-of-inventory remaining to roughly match production lead time so a PO placed today arrives just as you run out (just-in-time ideal).
- When sales velocity drops and you're stuck with far more inventory than production time (e.g., 1,000 days outstanding), treat it as a cash flow problem, not a margin problem.
- Liquidate non-moving stock always — it decays in value, and sophisticated buyers won't assign balance-sheet retail value to 2-year-old stock.
- Build liquidation funnels: create an unlisted PDP, run ads to it, exclude existing customers, and accept lower efficiency (e.g., ~1.0 or 0.8 ROS versus a 2:1 target on good inventory) or discount deeply.
- Build relationships with third-party liquidators as a standing capability for large inventory positions.
- Alternatively, sell to your large customer list with a discount and a narrative where it won't damage the brand.
Caveat: Liquidating harms the P&L and reported margin — that's accepted in exchange for cash.
Caveat: Holding aged inventory to prop up asset ratios overstates its true value.

## Match Product Development to a Specific Business Problem
Use when: Scoping a new product or category and deciding which business problem it must solve.
- Identify your core problem first: the source says businesses exist in one (or all) of three states — a margin problem, a volume problem, or a cash problem.

- Ask before greenlighting: what problem does this product need to solve for the business? This is the source's recommended filter.

## Start with free cash flow, not net income, to judge business health
Use when: Monthly or quarterly financial review of an ecommerce store, when net income looks healthy but the cash balance is shrinking or we are unsure whether to hire, buy inventory or take on debt.
- Reconcile net income to operating cash flow using non-cash expenses and working-capital movements.

- Do not subtract a negative investing cash flow number or assume every investing transaction is capital expenditure.

## Map the three cash flow statement sections to store activities
Use when: When setting up or reviewing an ecommerce cash flow statement and deciding where to record inventory buys, software build costs, loan drawdowns or owner distributions.
- Operating cash flow (also called cash flow from operations): everything related to running day-to-day operations — paying salaries, marketing, fulfilment and supplier bills. Start this section with net income linked from the income statement, then adjust.
- Investing cash flow: purchases of long-term assets such as warehouse equipment, a software platform, or shares in another company. A one-month employee salary is an expense (operating), not an investment.
- Financing cash flow: non-operational capital movements — owner distributions, money lent into the business, loan proceeds or repayments. This reflects capital structure, not trading performance.
- Adjust operating cash flow for balance sheet movements: a rise in accounts receivable is negative to cash (customers owe you), a rise in accounts payable is positive (you owe suppliers and hold cash longer), and depreciation is stripped back out because it never left the bank.
- Apply the same treatment to inventory: an increase in the inventory balance (e.g. 335k to 448k) is a cash outflow, and a decrease means you converted stock into sales and released cash.
- Only after completing all three sections do you arrive at free cash flow, which the source describes as cash with no further obligations other than financing.
Caveat: The exact interface and line-item naming varies by accounting software and template; verify current layouts when building or importing statements.
Caveat: The source teaches the general structure via one ecommerce example, so line items specific to your store may need judgement.

## Separate near-cash expenses from non-cash items when reading the P&L

## COGS Drives Unit Economics and Valuation
Use when: Deciding whether a product can fund its own acquisition and reinvestment, or preparing gross margin figures for investors or lenders.
- Take the sale price of a unit. Subtract only the true cost of delivering that unit (materials, direct labor, factory overhead, freight-in, packaging, or for digital: servers, delivery APIs, support and implementation wages depending on model).
- The remaining gross profit per unit is the pool available for marketing, R&D and overhead. If gross profit per unit cannot cover customer acquisition cost plus contribution to overhead, the unit is not economically viable at that price.
- Recognise that external valuers look at revenue growth and gross margin together; a misclassified cost that understates COGS inflates gross margin and distorts the valuation multiple.
- Use the corrected gross margin to decide whether to make more of the same product, reprice, or discontinue and build a new product.
- Keep a simple per-SKU gross profit calculation so repricing and promotion decisions are grounded in the same numbers shown to investors.
Caveat: Gross margin is not the only valuation input; growth, retention and market matter too.
Caveat: The source frames this for finance leaders and founders; the application to SKU-level decisions is an adaptation of that principle.

## Balance Sheet to P&L Timing for Inventory Versus Services
Use when: Forecasting cash and monthly profitability, or explaining to a founder-investor why a stock build-up hurts cash before it hits COGS.
- For physical products, capitalise inventory (including freight-in) as an asset on the balance sheet when produced or purchased, then move it to COGS on the P&L only when the item sells.
- Understand that building stock consumes cash now and reduces reported profit later — useful when planning seasonal buys or bulk purchasing for cost savings.
- For a SaaS/digital product, most COGS (support wages, implementation wages) hits the P&L immediately; only capitalised fixed assets such as owned servers or computers flow from the balance sheet into COGS via depreciation over time.
- If you rent server capacity (AWS/Azure), the monthly fee goes straight to COGS rather than being depreciated.
- Use this distinction when forecasting: a stock-heavy quarter depresses cash before it depresses profit, while a services-heavy quarter depresses profit immediately.
Caveat: The source notes most modern small companies rent rather than own servers; ownership changes the accounting treatment.

## Waste Control: Expiry Rotation, Fire Sales and Inventory Controls
Use when: Managing perishable or seasonal inventory and shrink in an ecommerce warehouse or 3PL.
- Track expiration and obsolescence dates per SKU and ship stock with the nearest expiration first, before touching stock with a longer window.
- When stock is very close to expiry, run a fire sale to move it quickly at much lower margin rather than losing it entirely.
- Log inventory when it enters and when it leaves the warehouse, and run periodic physical counts — the source identifies missing outbound logging as the control gap that enables theft.
- Investigate recurring count variances as a signal of shrink or process error, and feed the cost back into your COGS assumptions.
- Use the same rotation logic for seasonal goods: move the oldest season first and discount aggressively before the next season lands.
Caveat: Fire sales protect cash but depress margin; the source presents them as a last-resort recovery, not routine strategy.
