## Reconcile effort reduction and delight across the journey
Use when: When allocating post-purchase CX or retention budget and deciding whether to invest in support/returns fixes or unboxing/surprise moments.

## Apply the peak-end rule to post-purchase design
Use when: When choosing where to spend packaging, insert, follow-up or returns budget for a store's post-purchase experience.

- Do not dilute budget across the middle of the journey; the forgettable stages contribute little to remembered experience.
- Time advocacy asks (reviews, referrals) to the peak moment just after delivery rather than at random.

## Problem-solve with low effort and over-recover failures
Use when: When writing support policy, refund authority limits, or handling a specific failure such as a lost parcel or damaged item.

- Track first-contact resolution, CSAT and repeat rate among customers who experienced a failure.

## Engineer positive disconfirmation, not satisfaction

## Classify features with the Kano Model before budgeting CX
Use when: Deciding where to spend packaging, service, and site budget — and which problems must simply be eliminated rather than innovated on.
- Must-be (threshold): secure checkout, accurate size charts, correct item shipped, working navigation. Presence adds nothing; absence causes severe dissatisfaction. These are non-negotiable fixes, not delight opportunities.
- One-dimensional: shipping speed, durability, support response time. More spend yields proportional satisfaction — tune to market standard, don't expect delight.
- Attractive (delighters): unadvertised free gifts, handwritten notes, premium haptic packaging. Absence causes no dissatisfaction; presence triggers disproportionate joy.
- For each touchpoint, ask which quadrant it currently occupies in your category, then allocate the delight budget only to attractive-quality items.
- Plan for delight decay: free two-day shipping was once a delighter and is now baseline. Re-audit delighters on a cycle and introduce new ones as old ones decay.
Caveat: Kano category is category- and era-specific; the same feature can sit in different quadrants for different audiences.
Caveat: Delight decay means a static delight program will quietly become table stakes — the source frames delight as a continuous innovation cycle, not a one-time initiative.

## Track service experience alongside cohort repurchase and contribution

## Define Lifetime Gross Profit with a Time Constraint
Use when: Deciding how much you can afford to pay for new customers, or setting the retention and LTV targets your store will be managed against.
- For the source's LTGP operating metric, subtract product cost, delivery and handling, transaction fees and refunds from revenue over a fixed cohort window. If revenue is already net of refunds, do not subtract refunds twice.
- Always attach a time constraint: 30-day LTGP, 90-day LTGP, 180-day LTGP. A raw 'lifetime' number spanning decades is unmeasurable and irrelevant to cash needs.
- Build the cohort view by measuring the cumulative gross profit of each acquisition cohort at 30, 60, 90, 180 and 365 days. A customer might be worth $60 gross profit on day 0 and grow to $100 by day 90.
- Calculate from real order-level data (e.g., Shopify order and refund line items) rather than assuming AOV x margin. Starting AOV and margin move around, especially during discount periods, so refresh at least monthly.
- Use the constrained LTGP as the input to CAC targets and to KPI retention programs; ignore abstract lifetime revenue claims.
Caveat: The source calls this LTGP; because it includes multiple variable costs, label the included costs explicitly rather than assuming it equals accounting gross profit. Keep revenue LTV and contribution measures distinct.
Caveat: Source is explicit that refunds should not be folded into CAC — doing so corrupts paid media signals — but must be captured in LTGP.

## Set a CAC Target from LTGP and an Efficiency Ratio

- Choose the LTGP window you'll manage against. The source recommends 30-day LTGP for most stores; 90-day for CPG, supplements or high-repeat categories.

## Run RFM analysis to segment on real purchase behavior
Use when: You are building customer segments for win-back flows, replenishment, VIP campaigns, or first-to-second order conversion.
- Use RFM scoring: score each subscriber on how recently they purchased, how frequently they purchase, and how much they spend — all relative to your own subscriber base, not external benchmarks.
- Classify subscribers into segments: Champions (top percentiles across all three), Loyal (just below Champions), Recent (recent purchase but not frequent/high-spend), At Risk (metrics slowing), and Needs Attention.
- Treat At Risk and Needs Attention similarly in practice: get in front of them to prompt a purchase, especially if they were previously a Champion.
- Build a high-value win-back segment of former Champions who are now At Risk or Needs Attention, and target them in the ~20% specific-goal campaigns.
- Replace arbitrary win-back triggers (e.g., 120 days) with RFM-driven triggers so a 200-day-cycle high-spender isn't sent a generic discount prematurely.
- Use RFM segments across flows (win-back, replenishment, post-purchase, abandoned cart/checkout) where you can trigger off real behavioral data.
- Target Recent-but-not-Loyal/Champion customers as a first-to-second order conversion segment to elevate them before they lapse.
- Set up RFM in your platform's analytics tab (e.g., Klaviyo marketing analytics) or wherever your software supports it, and verify current interface.
Caveat: RFM scores are relative to your own base, so definitions shift as your customer mix changes.
Caveat: The cohort labels are the source's framework; exact percentile cutoffs aren't fixed.

## Use paid ads to surface new arrivals to existing customers
Use when: You run frequent product drops or new arrivals and need to decide whether to spend paid media against your existing customer base.
- Recognize paid ads is primarily an acquisition tool; the source's default position is to spend little on existing customers — verify this fits your model before reallocating.
- Exception: in fashion and continuously-dropping categories, paid ads are needed to surface new arrivals because email/SMS alone won't reach the whole existing base and new products shouldn't be missed.
- Run a dedicated existing-customer spend surfacing new arrivals and current campaign shoots.

- Instead of a percentage allocation, compute a dollar budget: frequency (source suggests 4–7 per month) × number of existing customers × CPMs ÷ 1,000.

## Use zero-party data to improve acquisition targeting
Use when: You are designing acquisition creative, personas, or angles, or setting up post-purchase surveys to inform paid media.
- Distinguish first-party data (email, phone, name) from zero-party data (personal info shared by the customer): what they do on weekends, what problem the product solves, what they like about the brand.
- Collect zero-party data via surveys (post-purchase surveys mentioned as a method) and store it tied to customer records.
- Analyze the top 20% of customers (which the source says typically drive 80% of revenue): identify their persona, the angles they responded to, and the offer they purchased on.
