## Find the Curiosity Gap 'Goldilocks Zone' to Open a Loop
Use when: Writing or auditing a Reel hook when engagement stalls because the opening is either too obvious or too vague for a cold audience.
- Picture the hook as a gap between two cliffs: the viewer must jump (watch the rest) to close the loop. If the gap is too small, they can see the landing and have no reason to jump; if too wide, they are confused and see no reward.
- Diagnose the 'too small' gap: for example, 'Creating a spreadsheet for my Pokemon cards changed everything in my collection challenge' tells the viewer exactly what the rest of the video will be, so only people already interested in that task stay.
- Diagnose the 'too wide' gap: 'This changed everything for me' gives no niche, no subject, and no identifiable reward, so a stranger has no reason to care.
- Aim for the middle: name the domain and the stakes while withholding the mechanism, as in 'This one thing changed everything about my Pokemon card collection, and after four years, I finally finished.'
- Expect this hook type to require more iteration than others; the source calls it the one that takes the most practice.
Caveat: There is no formula for calibrating the gap; it depends on what the audience already knows about you and your niche.
Caveat: Vague curiosity hooks underperform for cold audiences who lack the context of an existing following.

## Outcome, Data-Backed, and Direct-Address Hooks for Credibility and Targeting
Use when: Writing Reels for a product where proof, specificity, or audience self-selection matters, such as coaching, supplements, or specialized services.
- Outcome hook: lead with a specific, concrete result, e.g. 'This reel got 2.1 million views' or 'My client put on 12 pounds of muscle in three months.' The more concrete the proof, the more viewers stay.
- Use realistic numbers rather than extreme ones. The source notes that enormous figures can cause viewers to scroll because they believe the result is unattainable for them.
- Authority or data-backed hook: attribute the claim to a trusted source - a study, a named organization, or a prominent figure - so the viewer trusts the data rather than having to trust you. This reduces the skepticism a stranger brings to a Reel.
- Direct-address hook: call out the exact viewer, e.g. 'If you're a solo entrepreneur trying to figure out Instagram, listen up.' Specific callouts make aligned viewers lean in, and adjacent viewers often keep watching to check whether it applies to them.
- Keep the specificity honest. These templates only work if the outcome, source, and audience callout genuinely describe what the Reel delivers.
Caveat: Data-backed hooks depend on the credibility and relevance of the cited source; misapplied or fabricated data claims undermine trust.
Caveat: Direct address narrows the hook's pull; broader topical content may need a less targeted opening.

## Urgency, List, and Comparison Hooks for Predictable Value and Self-Comparison
Use when: Writing Reels that teach product use, compare before/after results, or create a deadline-driven reason to watch now.
- Urgency/time-bound hook: attach the message to a moment, e.g. 'Before you go to the gym today, do this,' 'Before your head hits the pillow tonight...' The time pressure creates attention. The source also shows a news-style variant, 'Breaking Instagram news: you can now...'
- List hook: promise a countable set - 'Three ways to improve your bench press,' 'Five foods you should avoid,' 'Two things to check next time you snowboard.' The structured format signals predictable value and feels easier to consume, so viewers are more likely to continue.

- Keep the promised count or comparison honest; the format only retains viewers if the video delivers the structured payoff.
Caveat: Urgency hooks lose credibility if the time pressure is arbitrary or repeated without relevance.
Caveat: List hooks set an expectation; failing to deliver all items creates disappointment and reduces retention.

## On-Target Virality: Maximize Penetration Within a Niche Slice, Not Broad-Spectrum Hits
Use when: When evaluating whether a store content topic is worth doing, or reviewing high-view videos that brought no sales.
- Distinguish two types of virality: pure virality chooses extremely broad, universally relatable topics to expand the audience pool at all costs (most 10M+ views come from here); on-target virality maximizes penetration within a very narrow audience slice without spilling outside that slice.
- Use the '800-million-person pie' metaphor for trade-offs: pure virality wants people outside the slice to watch; on-target virality only wants to cover as many people inside the slice as possible.
- When selecting topics, ask of every idea: will my core audience avatar find this interesting, useful, valuable, or entertaining? If the answer is no, don't post it even if it might go viral—the multiple audience groups brought by broad-spectrum hits will confuse the algorithm's direction for your next video.
- Remember the algorithm prefers precision over breadth: it would rather let a 'fan favorite' in a niche category receive continuous distribution than be broadly pushed to all consumers of broadly related content.

## AI Writes Slop Because It Doesn't Know Which Commodity Ideas You Agree With
Use when: Writing or briefing product-description and blog copy for a store, and AI output reads generic or off-brand and you're tempted to re-prompt rather than supply the missing perspective.

- For your niche (e.g. minimalist home goods, value-priced kitchen gear), list the recurring commodity statements the whole category makes. Example: cheap cookware ruins quickly, you must buy heavy pans, nonstick is bad.

- If you find a statement the whole category repeats and you genuinely can't pick a side, note it as deliberately neutral rather than letting the model default.

## Write From 'I', Not 'You', to Escape Commodity Copy
Use when: Drafting product descriptions, founder notes, or landing page copy and the paragraph reads like generic category advice that any store could publish.
- Audit a draft and highlight every sentence that begins with or implies 'you should', 'you need to', or 'you must'.

- The source's operational rule is that 'you' framing is commodity (repeating what everyone says) and 'I' framing is personality content (what this particular person thinks about the world) which cannot be matched by category competitors.

## AI Should Repeat You, Not Write For You

## Timely vs Timeless Content: Set Expectations Before You Publish

## Voice Is Built From Identifiable Variables, Not Magic

## Write Conversational Hooks That Bypass the Ad-Blocker
