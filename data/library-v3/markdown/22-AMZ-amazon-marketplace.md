## Choose FBA or FBM before setting up a seller account
Use when: Deciding your fulfilment model before registering an Amazon seller account or sourcing inventory.
- Understand the two models: FBA (Fulfilment by Amazon) means you send stock to Amazon's warehouse and they pick, pack and ship each order, charging fulfilment fees that reduce your margin. FBM (Fulfilment by Merchant) means you ship every order yourself.
- Source view: the presenter chooses FBA because he wants a lifestyle, semi-passive business rather than packing orders at home. Treat this as his personal preference, not a universal rule — FBM can suit heavy, fragile, low-volume or made-to-order items.
- Note that your choice changes the fee lines in every profitability calculation, so fix the model before you analyse products.
- If in doubt, run one small FBA test batch and compare actual per-unit fulfilment fees against your own shipping cost before committing your full range.
- Verify current fee schedules and programme terms in Seller Central, as Amazon changes them.
Caveat: Source is a single practitioner's preference, not a benchmark; FBM is not inherently worse.

## Pick an Amazon business model: arbitrage, wholesale or private label
Use when: Choosing which Amazon selling model to launch next quarter and how much capital to allocate.
- Map the three routes as the source describes them: (1) Retail/online arbitrage — buy discounted stock from supermarkets or retail websites and resell on Amazon; lowest capital, quick-cash but not scalable and often capped (e.g. quantity limits per order). (2) Wholesale — buy branded products in bulk from a wholesaler or the brand and resell at retail price; more scalable, no need to create demand or run ads, source cites roughly £1,000 starting capital. (3) Private label — create your own brand, source from China, build your own listing and run ads; source cites roughly £5,000–£10,000 needed for stock, ads, images, listing, barcodes and trademark.
- Note the trade-off the source highlights: wholesale means other sellers can buy the same branded product and compete on your listing; private label removes that but raises cost and learning curve.
- His recommendation for beginners is to start with wholesale or arbitrage to learn how Amazon works, then move to private label — cite this as his opinion, not a rule.
- Choose your route now because it determines which product-analysis checks (BSR, seller count, ROI) are even relevant.
Caveat: Arbitrage and wholesale-specific sourcing metrics (BSR, seller counts, 30% ROI) do not transfer to private label or your own DTC store.

## Analyse a resale ASIN with four pass/fail checks

- Check Best Seller Rank, found under the listing's 'see all information' or product-details area. The source's rule of thumb is BSR under 100,000, because the lower the rank the faster the product sells — treat 100,000 as his guideline, not a fixed law.
- Check actual selling eligibility, authentic supply documentation and whether the product exactly matches the ASIN. Competitor seller count is not proof of permission to sell.
- Check Amazon is not on the listing. Open the buy box and check 'sold by' — if it says Amazon, avoid it, because Amazon typically undercuts and wins the buy box (cited case: Amazon priced more than £10 below the next cheapest seller). 'Amazon Warehouse' is acceptable because it is used stock.
- Check ROI of at least 30%. Use Amazon's revenue calculator with the ASIN, set your sell price (source suggests matching the lowest FBA seller), enter cost of goods, and compute ROI = profit ÷ cost of goods × 100.
- Only sell if all four checks pass. The source allows one adjustment: for a fast-selling item he would accept around 20% ROI.

## Set Up Your Business Identity Before Listing
Use when: Account setup and brand-building: choosing the legal structure behind your Amazon store.
- Choose between: sell as an individual (works, uses your SSN); form a DBA to get a business bank account; or form an LLC for liability protection.

## Order 3-6 months of inventory as your first production run

## Ship from factory to Amazon: air vs. sea, freight forwarder and duty
Use when: Fulfillment and inbound logistics: getting a finished production run from the factory into Amazon's warehouse.
- Compare modes. Air via DHL or FedEx takes roughly 5-10 days and costs much more; sea freight takes roughly 4-6 weeks plus customs and is cheaper. The source's example: a student's inbound sea shipment cost about $1,250, and doing it by air would have been about 3-4x more expensive.

- If you ship by sea into the US you need a customs bond: the source cites about $100 for a single-use bond or about $400 for an annual/multi-entry bond, which makes sense if you'll run several shipments.

## Inventory availability as the core driver of Amazon revenue growth
Use when: Planning reorder quantities and reorder timing for a product that is selling faster than expected, or diagnosing a revenue drop after a good month.

## Estimate monthly storage cost and avoid long-term storage fees
Use when: Replenishment and inventory planning: deciding how many units to send to FBA and when to pull slow stock.
- Measure product height, width and depth to get cubic inches. Divide by 1,728 (cubic inches in a cubic foot) and multiply by the per-cubic-foot rate. The source's example: a 3x4x5 in product is 60 cubic in, giving about $0.026 per unit per month at $0.75/cubic foot.
- Account for the seasonal rate: the source cites roughly $0.70 per cubic foot January-September and $2.40 per cubic foot October-December, because Amazon raises fees to discourage Q4 overstocking. Confirm current rates.
- Estimate total storage by multiplying per-unit monthly cost by the months you expect the unit to remain in the warehouse, and fold that into your unit economics.
- Avoid the long-term storage fee: if inventory stays past 365 days, the source says Amazon charges $6.90 per cubic foot or $0.15 per item, whichever is greater.
- Monitor sell-through. If a SKU is slow, request removal before the one-year mark, even if you intend to re-ship it back in, or let it sell down and reset the clock with fresh inventory.
- Size shipments so you don't end up with residual units sitting past a year: the source contrasts 100 units selling 50/month (clock resets continually) with 100 units selling 10/month (20 units remain after 12 months and start accruing long-term fees).
Caveat: Rates are cited from the source's experience and change; verify current Amazon storage fee schedules.
Caveat: Removal and re-shipment costs can rival the long-term storage fee for very cheap, very small items.

- Only after getting ungated, buy analytical software (SellerAmp, Keepa), since tools bill monthly and are useless before you can source approved products.
- Begin sourcing — the source calls this the core of the business and stresses diligence over speed.
- Prep, label, and ship units; then track inventory receipt (add SellerBoard for reimbursement and review automation once listings are live).
- Reprice listings as the market moves, and only later outsource sourcing and prep.
Caveat: This is one reseller's workflow from a January 2025 video; tool pricing, ungating rules, and Seller Central UI have since changed.
Caveat: The model shown is retail/online arbitrage reselling of established brands, not private label.
