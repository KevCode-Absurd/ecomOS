## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator write a long-form advertorial (an article-style sales page) that sits between a cold-traffic ad and the product page. It gives 2 selling principles, the ad's job, and a 10-section page structure with the reason for each section. It is strongest on copy sequence and on how each section sets up the next. It is thin on page design, mobile layout, CTA placement, testing method, traffic volume and measurement. The example is a pain-relief device for women over 50, so the tone and claims are health-category and need heavy adaptation for other products.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 Selling psychology

1. **Selling is creating separation.** Advertorials are "salesmanship in print". Selling means widening the gap in the reader's mind between where they are now and where they want to be, then placing the product as the vehicle between the 2. Before writing any line, define (a) the current state: pains, daily frustrations, and what they currently believe about the problem; (b) the desired state: goals and "the specific moments that they want back". Condition: presenter's framework, no evidence given. Applies to any problem-solving product. [5f8bf0a170d8]

2. **The pain threshold.** Source says people are motivated "10 times" more by moving away from pain than toward pleasure, but pain only produces action "when it becomes more painful than the action itself". The threshold differs per person. Below it, people do not buy even while in daily pain (his example: overweight people who do not start a diet). The page's job is to push the reader over it. The "10 times" figure is stated with no source. Treat it as the presenter's claim. [web check: loss aversion research, which is usually cited near 2x, not 10x]

3. **Repetition is the mechanism.** Find the 3 to 5 biggest pain points and the 3 to 5 biggest desires. Hit them "the whole time" across the page "in different ways". "Do not be scared of repetition." Each hit moves the reader closer to the threshold. Condition: presenter's opinion. Not tested. The source does not say how much repetition is too much. [needs brand data: ranked customer pains and desires from reviews, surveys, support tickets]

4. **People buy when they feel understood, not when they understand the product.** "People do not buy something when they understand the product. They buy something when they feel understood." So the page proves it knows life with the problem before it explains anything. [20262eb9059c]

5. **A common enemy drives action.** The page personifies the condition ("like a thief in the night, stealing little pieces of my life one by one") and later casts "the system" (the medical system that "processes you") as the enemy. The enemy returns in section 7 and in the price justification ("do you think our common enemy wants you to know that there's a $59 device..."). Condition: presenter's framing. Blaming professionals or a whole industry can conflict with card ff648126e918 ("Avoid unsupported competitor failures or claims that retail products replace professional treatment"). [ff648126e918]

6. **Whoever explains the problem best is believed to have the best solution.** If the reader accepts a new root cause, "every competitor becomes irrelevant by definition because they're all solving the wrong problem". You are not fighting over the same solution; "you're changing which solutions exist". Card c916e37c3b02 limits this: the cause must be genuine and supportable. Do not invent an expert or a proprietary scientific cause. [c916e37c3b02]

7. **The mechanism must map 1 to 1 to the root cause.** "If the mechanism doesn't map one to one to the root cause, the page collapses" and the reader feels it is a scam. [c916e37c3b02]

8. **Specificity creates credibility.** In the story (exact times, sides, durations) and in the mechanism (every component gets a spec and an exact number; "the specs read like engineering"). Card limit: use specs only when verified for the actual SKU; label uncertainty. [c916e37c3b02] [20262eb9059c]

9. **Only the combination works.** The most commercially important belief in the mechanism section is that only this specific combination delivers the result, so partial solutions and cheap substitutes cannot. In the example: heat alone, massage alone, compression alone do not work; all 3 together do. Find "your version" of this reason. Condition: it must be true for your product. [c916e37c3b02] [needs brand data: what the product does that single-feature alternatives cannot, with proof]

10. **Price is perceived relative to an anchor.** The reader does not know if a price is high or low; they compare it to another price. A strong marketer "controls the environment" of that comparison. By the time the price shows, the reader "has mentally spent a fortune on alternatives the page had already killed". Card limit: the anchor must be a genuine comparable product, bundle contents or total ownership cost. [54399c0a8159]

### 2.2 Ad to page congruence

11. **The advertorial continues a story; it cannot start one.** The ad starts it. "A perfect advertorial behind the wrong ad converts really bad." [d107bfda3ceb] [5f8bf0a170d8]

12. **The ad has 2 jobs.** Get the click, and set the frame for the page: the anger, the pain and the level of curiosity. [d107bfda3ceb]

13. **The ad must not look like an ad.** Cold viewers are "never really shopping" (couch, bus stop, at work) and skip ads on reflex. Open with something that looks like "a person sharing a discovery", not a brand running an ad. The ad does not show or name the product. It talks only about the problem and hints at a new solution. Condition: presenter's view for cold traffic to an advertorial. It is not a rule for retargeting or product-page traffic. Card 5f8bf0a170d8 adds that the page itself must be identified as advertising. [d107bfda3ceb] [5f8bf0a170d8] [web check: Meta and FTC rules on native-style ads and disclosure]

14. **A strong ad does 4 things (from the example).** Breaks the pattern (cancelling surgery and getting better contradicts what the viewer expects); opens a loop ("told me something off the record"); qualifies the audience in 1 line ("over 50"); teases the mechanism without revealing it ("my shoulder was never damaged. It was starving"). The reader then "arrives in the advertorial mid-story". [d107bfda3ceb]

### 2.3 Page structure

15. **Each section answers the question the last one raised.** The page follows "a straight line". Objections are removed before the product is pitched. Card 5f8bf0a170d8 turns this into the rule: make each section answer the question raised by the preceding section, and carry the same angle from ad to page to offer. [5f8bf0a170d8]

16. **Most pages skip 2 sections that this source calls critical.** Section 3 (why nothing else worked): "most pages just ignore that and go straight into the mechanism". Section 4 (root cause): "the section that most people just skip", and "where cold traffic actually gets hurt" (the transcript says "call traffic"; read as cold traffic). [ff648126e918] [c916e37c3b02]

17. **The page must look like an article.** Headline, byline, image, trust badges, long copy with images between. "Not like an ad." See the disclosure conflict in section 9. [d107bfda3ceb]

18. **Do not copy the page; copy the structure.** "Don't just completely copy and paste this advertorial for your product. It's just not going to work." Keep the ideas and sections, replace every pain point from your own customer research. [20262eb9059c] [needs brand data: customer research]

## 3. Procedures

### A. Before writing: research inputs

1. Write the current state: pains, daily frustrations, current beliefs about the cause. [5f8bf0a170d8]
2. Write the desired state: goals, and the specific moments they want back.
3. Rank the 3 to 5 biggest pains and the 3 to 5 biggest desires.
4. List every alternative the customer is considering or has tried, including doing nothing. For each, find the specific reason it fails or disappoints. Card: include keeping the current solution; use accurate features, total cost, compatibility or effort. [ff648126e918]
5. List the 5 biggest objections that stop people buying. [cca2df9310c1]
6. Find a root cause competitors do not talk about, and confirm it is true and supportable. [c916e37c3b02]
7. Name the mechanism and list each component with a spec and a number. [c916e37c3b02]
The source says all of this depends on "a crystal clear understanding of our customer avatar" but does not say how to do the research. [needs brand data: reviews, surveys, support tickets, returns reasons, competitor list, product specs with proof]

### B. Write the ad (cold traffic)

1. Pick 1 researched shopper problem and a supportable benefit. [d107bfda3ceb]
2. Write copy that reads as a person sharing a discovery. Do not name or show the product.
3. Break a belief the viewer holds. Source example: "I cancelled my shoulder surgery 3 weeks before the date and my shoulder has never felt better. My physical therapist taught me something off the record about why shoulders over 50 never heal and nobody in the medical system is talking about it." Do not reuse its medical claims. [d107bfda3ceb]
4. Open a loop the page will close.
5. Qualify the audience in 1 line (age, situation).
6. Tease the mechanism without revealing it.
7. Visual: the source says these images "work extremely well": a comic or illustration, a biology-textbook style diagram (for example a cell breakdown), a medical-textbook style image. None show the product. No numbers are given for this. [verify UI: ad format and placement specs]
8. Check that the page's first screen continues this exact story, audience and tone. [d107bfda3ceb]

### C. Write the advertorial, 10 sections in order

The source numbers 10 sections. The doc header calls it a "10-step psychological framework". Card 5f8bf0a170d8 compresses the order to: ad promise, hook, researched customer situation, alternatives, product mechanism, proof, objections, price and guarantee. [5f8bf0a170d8]

**Section 1. Hook and intro.**
- Goal: the reader clicked 3 seconds ago, "the guard is up", they are thinking of leaving. The hook must "buy the next 30 seconds of attention". "If this fails, nothing else on the page matters." It is the single most important section and the one to A/B test hardest. [d107bfda3ceb]
- It must win 2 battles at once: (a) look like an article, not an ad; (b) attack a belief the reader holds about their problem and set up the new mechanism.
- Example headline: "The real reason your shoulder won't heal (hint: it's not wear and tear) and the 12-minute fix that's making cortisone shots obsolete." Then "written by Dr. [name]", a photo of a woman with the product, and trust badges.
- Then drop into a personal story that opens on a tiny, humiliating moment, not the symptom: "I couldn't hook my own bra." Expand why the moment is emotional. Tie it to identity: "At that moment I wasn't Linda anymore. I wasn't a wife, a mother, grandmother. I was just a body that had betrayed me."
- Frame: PAS (problem, agitation, solution). Make a small problem feel big, because of the pain threshold.
- Card limits: match audience, promise and tone to the ad; do not borrow the medical claims. A "Dr." byline on a story is an expert endorsement; do not fabricate one. [d107bfda3ceb] [5970767eda77]

**Section 2. Current situation and emotional pain (resonate).**
- Goal: turn 30 seconds into full commitment to the page. Mirror the reader's life "with more precision and with more detail than they could describe it themselves", so they think "this really is me" and read the page like a letter to them. [20262eb9059c]
- Structure: walk the pain from very small to taking over their life "in slow and concrete steps". "You're never describing a symptom. You're describing a life shrinking." A shrinking life is something people pay to reverse.
- Example ladder: age and duration ("at 58 I've been dealing with shoulder aches for about 2 years"); started small ("the kind of thing you pop an ad for", likely a pain pill brand in the original); enemy metaphor (thief in the night); hobby lost (quit golf, "hot knife into my shoulder blade"); everyday objects (coffee mug on the top shelf; "rearranging my whole life around this invisible prison"); sleep (wakes at 2 a.m. rolling onto her left side); marriage (husband sleeps in the guest room); 8 months in a recliner, repeated 3 times ("8 months of..."); rock bottom (husband hooks her bra "like I was a child"); list of losses (independence, sleep, hobbies, intimacy, identity).
- Every concrete detail (2 a.m., left side, 8 months, recliner) is there so the reader recognises their own life.
- End on an open loop: "I had no idea that in just 3 weeks, everything was about to change."
- Card: use authentic customer language and observed details, not invented personal testimony. If you tell a real customer's story, keep its real limits and get permission. [20262eb9059c]

**Section 3. Why nothing else worked (burn down alternatives).**
- Goal: answer the silent objection "I've tried so many things. Why would this be different?" before the mechanism. [ff648126e918]
- 3 jobs: (a) name every alternative and "burn it down" so only your solution is left, which also pre-empts the reader going to Google; (b) say doing nothing is also bad, since all the pains stay; (c) let the narrator earn the right to recommend: "I've wasted years and thousands of dollars. I don't want you to do the same."
- Format: "I tried everything, and I mean everything", an image of all the alternatives, then "Before I tell you what finally worked, you need to understand what didn't... I get it. I've been there."
- Then 1 alternative per block, each with why it failed: heating pads (3 brands, lukewarm after 20 minutes, a cold pad at 3 a.m.); physical therapy (14 weeks, twice a week, $200 per session); and so on. Close with "Nothing worked. Not really, not permanently."
- Then remove the last option, the expert path: the doctor says "I think it's time we talk about surgery"; an orthopaedic surgeon with "a long waiting list"; "for patients your age these surgeries only work about half the time"; $35,000 and 6 months. "That's when I realized the system isn't designed to heal. It's designed to process you." This installs the enemy.
- Card limits: list the alternatives customers actually consider; compare accurately (features, total cost, compatibility, effort); name the tradeoff and admit who may prefer another option; no unsupported claims that competitors fail or that a retail product replaces professional treatment. [ff648126e918] [needs brand data: real competitor and alternative list with verifiable limits]

**Section 4. Root cause.**
- Goal: the reader knows they have the problem but not why. Give them the why and you "own the frame for the solution". [c916e37c3b02]
- Name a root cause competitors do not talk about.
- Delivery in the example: back to the physical therapist "to vent"; he closes the door, "which he never did", and says "can I tell you something off the record?"; "the problem with your shoulder isn't that it's broken. It's starving"; he shows a diagram on his tablet. This closes the loop the ad opened ("off the record").
- Example cause: after 40, blood flow to the shoulder "decreases by 3% every year". He then explains how and why, and ties it back to why nothing else worked. The reader gets an "aha" moment.
- Transfer example given by the presenter (weight-loss supplement): "it's not calories, genetics or the gym"; the new root cause is mitochondria not producing energy well. This example is his and is not evidence.
- Card limit: the source's medical root cause and percentages are not reusable evidence. Identify a genuine, supportable cause. Do not invent an expert or a proprietary scientific cause. [c916e37c3b02] [needs brand data: evidence for the cause]

**Section 5. Unique mechanism (the key).**
- Goal: the reader now "holds a lock and is looking for the key". Present the mechanism as the key. [c916e37c3b02]
- The mechanism must solve exactly the root cause. Give it a name ("Triple Therapy Method"), explain why it works, why it is different, then present the product.
- Build belief first: "The science is clear even if your doctor never mentioned it. I went home that night and started researching... What I found shocked me." She reads studies.
- Then the PT reveals the device, "quietly recommended to worst-case patients like me who tried everything and were facing surgery".
- Map each feature to the cause: heat opens blood vessels; rhythmic compression forces blood through tissue; vibration triggers cellular regeneration. "Heat alone doesn't work. Massage alone doesn't work. Compression doesn't work. But when you get everything together, you get a 340% increase in blood flow."
- Low-effort ask: "Try it for 2 weeks before surgery. What do you have to lose? 12 minutes a day."
- Cast of 3: the bad doctor, the good physical therapist, and the narrator who acts as "the voice in the mind of the reader", a sceptic who checks for herself.
- Card limits: connect each relevant feature to the problem with a clear explanation or demonstration; use specs only when verified for the SKU; move on to proof once the buyer can explain the connection. [c916e37c3b02] [needs brand data: verified SKU specs and test results]

**Section 6. Proof story and future pacing.**
- Goal: the reader's last question is no longer "does it work?" but "does it work for someone like me?" Everybody thinks they are a special snowflake. [cca2df9310c1]
- Best case: several personas and customer stories with demonstrations and a lot of proof.
- Example: "Here's what happened over the next 14 days." Ordered; "I'd spent over $5,000 in treatments and it didn't work. What was 1 more thing?"; arrived 3 days later; used it the same evening. Then Day 1, Day 3, Day 4, Day 7, Day 9, Day 14.
- The day-by-day log future-paces the reader: you buy, it arrives, this is what you see on day 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 14.
- Result close: "I cancelled the surgery the next morning. That was 4 months ago." Sleeping on her side, golf, gardening, hugging grandkids. "I got my life back."
- CTA sells the outcome, not the product: "Click here to get yours back now", not "buy the product".
- Card limits: use real usage timelines only where documented, and do not promise the same result to everyone. [cca2df9310c1]

**Section 7. Objection handling.**
- Goal: sceptics who want to believe still hold specific objections. Break down the main ones one by one. [cca2df9310c1]
- Testimonials first: pick the 5 biggest objections; write 1 testimonial per objection with a different customer avatar. Example set (4 to 5 avatars): a woman who could not garden, a man who could not golf, a woman who could not sleep and now cooks again, a person who could not work. Opener: "I'm not the only one. Since sharing my story, I've heard from dozens of people."
- Then the authority objection: "If this works so well, why hasn't my doctor told me?" Answer: the doctor has 12 minutes with you, is trained in surgery not rehab technology, and "the medical system rewards interventions, not prevention. I'm not saying doctors are bad. Mine was wonderful." This returns to the common enemy.
- Then an FAQ, 1 objection per question.
- Then "why does this work when everything else failed, and why is it so cheap?", answered through the enemy: they would not make money if you knew a $59 device beats a $35,000 surgery.
- Card limits: source says "write" testimonials per avatar. Card cca2df9310c1 says: find genuine reviews that address each objection, preserve the customer's wording and result, "do not write testimonial personas". [cca2df9310c1] [needs brand data: reviews tagged by objection]

**Section 8. Recommendation, scarcity and price reveal.**
- 3 things happen: (1) a heartfelt recommendation, framed "here's what I'd tell you if you were my sister", including how she uses it; (2) scarcity: hard to get, "medical grade components", sold out 3 times this year, "through this page you can check if there are still units available"; (3) price reveal against anchors. [54399c0a8159]
- Price stack in the example: "Let's talk about what it's worth", then earlier costs shown ($2,800; a figure transcribed as "$35,500", likely the $35,000 surgery; "spent over $5,000"), then suggested retail $159 ("really fair" next to $35,000), then $59, "over 50% off". CTA: "Check if it's still in stock."
- Value stacks ("this is inside, this is inside, and that's the value, but now you get it for this price") are named as the same anchoring idea.
- Card limits: use a genuine comparable product, bundle contents or total ownership cost as anchor; show actual price, quantity, any recurring charges and delivery cost; use a compare-at price or stock deadline only when accurate. [54399c0a8159] [needs brand data: real compare-at price history, stock levels, landed delivery cost]

**Section 9. Guarantee, future pacing and close.**
- Goal: kill the last objections and make buying easy for readers who still have not clicked. [54399c0a8159]
- Offer an easy guarantee: "90-day nothing-to-lose guarantee. Use it. If you don't like it, return it. That's how confident I am."
- Present 2 paths: leave and keep the pain, or buy now, try it risk-free, and return it if you do not like it.
- Card: place a guarantee the merchant can honor beside the CTA, with its terms. [54399c0a8159] [needs brand data: actual return window, who pays return shipping, refund conditions]

**Section 10. Comment section.**
- Goal: keep readers on the page and answer final questions with social proof. [5970767eda77]
- Example comments: "Has anyone tried this?" / "This is the best thing ever" / "I bought mine and now they're 60% off, that's not fair" (which also tells readers to buy now) / "How long does shipping take?" "Received it after a week" / "Bought this for my wife, amazing" / "I just ordered mine, can't wait" / "I want to buy one for my dad".
- Source says the smartest comments are "usually a complaint", such as the one about the price dropping after purchase.
- The doc header calls this a "fabricated comment section". Card 5970767eda77: do not fabricate comment threads, customer avatars or expert endorsements. Show authentic customer comments if you have them, or clearly label merchant-written answers as an FAQ. Also recap benefit, what is included, price and purchase action, and check the CTA goes to the promised SKU and offer. [5970767eda77]

### D. Test the page (card addition, not in the transcript)

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Testing new creatives and pointing them all at the same product page, then hitting the same scaling ceiling. [5f8bf0a170d8]
2. Putting a good advertorial behind the wrong ad. "Converts really bad." [d107bfda3ceb]
3. Ads that look like ads; cold viewers skip them on reflex. [d107bfda3ceb]
4. Showing or talking about the product in the ad. [d107bfda3ceb]
5. A hook that fails; then "nothing else on the page matters". [d107bfda3ceb]
6. Pages that look like ads instead of articles.
7. Describing a symptom instead of a life shrinking. [20262eb9059c]
8. Vague copy. Generic pain with no times, places or durations. [20262eb9059c]
9. Copying this advertorial's pains and story for another product. "It's just not going to work." Do your own customer research. [20262eb9059c]
10. Skipping "why nothing else worked" and going straight to the mechanism. [ff648126e918]
11. Skipping the root cause. [c916e37c3b02]
12. A mechanism that does not map 1 to 1 to the root cause; the reader feels it is a scam. [c916e37c3b02]
13. Fear of repetition. Source says repeat the top pains and desires.
14. A CTA that asks the reader to buy the product instead of getting their outcome back.

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Conflicts with the cards and with common ecommerce and legal practice**
- Fabricated comment section and written testimonial personas. The source recommends both. The cards forbid both. In the US, fake reviews and testimonials are a legal risk. [5970767eda77] [cca2df9310c1] [web check: FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, FTC Endorsement Guides; local equivalents such as India's ASCI and CCPA rules for any India brand]
- "Don't look like an ad" versus disclosure. The source wants the ad and page to not look like ads. Card 5f8bf0a170d8 says identify the page as advertising. [web check: FTC native advertising guidance; Meta ad policy on misleading formats]
- Doctor byline and medical claims. "Written by Dr.", "340%", "3% per year", surgery "half the time", "making cortisone shots obsolete", and a device as an alternative to surgery. These are unsupported health claims. [c916e37c3b02] [web check: Meta health and wellness ad policy; FDA or local medical device claim rules]
- Audience call-outs such as "over 50". [web check: Meta personal attributes policy on ad copy that asserts or implies age or health conditions]
- Scarcity and compare-at. "Sold out 3 times", "check if there are still units" and $159 suggested retail are only allowed if true. [54399c0a8159] [needs brand data: stock history, prior selling prices]
- Blaming "the system" or doctors. Common enemy framing may be fine for some categories, but card ff648126e918 warns against unsupported claims that alternatives fail.
- Repetition versus brevity. Common landing-page advice favours short, scannable pages. This source argues long copy with deliberate repetition for cold traffic. It gives no data. Keep both views; test.
- Advertorial versus product page. Common advice sends traffic to a PDP or collection. This source says the product page is the scaling ceiling. It gives no data for that claim.

**Where the source is thin or unclear**
- No definition of the 3 to 4% conversion metric (per click, per session, or ad-to-purchase) and no source for it.
- No test method: no sample size, run time, success metric or test tool. Only "A/B test the hook hardest".
- No layout guidance: mobile, image count, CTA count and placement, page length, load speed, ad disclosure placement, pre-lander to PDP handoff or direct checkout.
- No guidance on how to do the customer research it calls essential.
- No guidance on which products fit an advertorial. The example is a health problem with a clear pain. The source asserts it works "in every niche" but shows 1. Products bought for pleasure or status (for example jewellery) may not have a pain to agitate. [needs brand data: whether the brand's product solves a painful, researchable problem]
- Section numbering in the transcript slips once ("after section two and section three, we then tell them why nothing else worked"); the order in section 3 above follows the rest of the talk, where alternatives is section 3 and root cause is section 4.
- Transcript errors: "$und00 million", "call traffic" (cold traffic), "advtorial/inventory/invertoriatorium" (advertorial), "hear" (heal), "$35,500" (likely $35,000). Numbers affected by these are marked above.
