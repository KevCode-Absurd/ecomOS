## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a 5-step checklist to run on any new or existing page (navigation, landing page, PDP, collection page, cart) to lower "cognitive friction": the mental effort a shopper spends to decide and buy. The 5 steps are: remove elements, swap text for images and icons, replace vague messaging with benefit headlines, fix visual hierarchy, and simplify product selection and add-to-cart feedback. Its strongest part is the number of single-change client A/B tests with conversion rate (CR) and revenue per visitor (RPV) results. It is thin on build steps inside any tool and gives no test design detail.

## 2. Principles

1. **Cognitive friction kills conversion; cognitive ease drives it.** Cognitive friction is the brain power a visitor must spend to shop and decide. The source says the brain has a "cognitive threshold" (how much it can process at once). Above it, navigation feels confusing, there is too much information and too many choices, and people leave. Cognitive ease comes from 5 levels: streamlined navigation, crystal-clear benefits, scannable text, an easy product selection process, and images that reinforce the value prop. Condition: all ecommerce pages. Evidence: the presenter's framework, no study cited. Card caveat: treat each step as a hypothesis to test, not a validated method. [3f164438706e]
2. **Remove before you add (paradox of choice).** "Too much choice equals no choice at all." The first move is to cut elements, not add more CRO widgets. Applies to navigation, PDP elements, bundle tiers and the cart. Condition: any page where recordings show overwhelm, cheap-option picking or exits. Evidence: Wild Men Drinking bundle test (section 7). [3f164438706e] [bc8e0b03fd58] [af10d9d13cab]
3. **Navigation: 7 items maximum, and only buying links up front.** Source's rule of thumb: never show more than 7 items on the navbar; above that he sees "diminishing returns and very poor conversions". Cut items that get close to 0% of clicks in Clarity (he says you can "easily cut out 2 to 3"). Remove "Home" because the logo already goes home. Move About, Contact, Blog and FAQ out, or into a sub-menu in much smaller, less prominent text. Condition: when the focus is conversions. Evidence: his experience plus click data; card calls 7 a rule of thumb, not a tested threshold. [bc8e0b03fd58] [needs brand data: nav click share per item from Clarity or similar]
4. **Many products: nest, but do not hide the shop.** If you have many products and collections, use a nested menu that limits how many items show at once. But do not add redundant clicks: expose the Shop menu up front instead of hiding it behind a click. Condition: large catalogs. [bc8e0b03fd58]
5. **Clean PDP layout beats a crowded one.** Source contrasts a PDP with "so many different elements" (overwhelming, no clear next step) with a clean, organized layout where "I know exactly what to do next". Opinion from side-by-side examples; no test number for this alone. [3f164438706e]
6. **Fewer bundle tiers can raise both CR and AOV.** When recordings show shoppers overwhelmed by many bundle options (picking a low-priced option or exiting), test cutting the tiers. Source's case: 5 tiers cut to 3. Condition: multi-tier bundle pages. Card caveat: 1 client case; do not apply a fixed tier count without testing. [af10d9d13cab] [needs brand data: tier sales mix and session recordings of the bundle block]
7. **A stripped-down cart beats a cart stacked with CRO tactics.** Source shows a cart "where everything is screaming at me at once" with many upsells and cross-sells and says it is "probably hurting their conversions". Keep the basics: higher-tier options, the items in the cart, a few minor upsells and cross-sells, simple next steps. Evidence: opinion from observation, no test. [3f164438706e] [af92a8953b92]
8. **Use images and icons wherever possible instead of text.** Source says humans are visual and quotes "the brain processes images 60,000 times faster than text" (no source given). Most brands "litter their pages" with paragraphs "no one ever actually reads". Condition: navigation, gallery, bundle options, long copy sections. [7caa022c540c] [634fdf0d882d] [2d9c1b326b14] [web check: 60,000x image processing claim, widely repeated and not traced to a study]
9. **Visual navigation beats text-only navigation.** A text-only menu gives no reason to click deeper and no picture of the product. Show the product in the menu through images or icons. Evidence: Parcel Safety and pet client tests (section 7). [7caa022c540c]
10. **Product images are among the most-clicked elements, so each must sell.** Source says heatmaps on "any website" show product images as one of the most-clicked page elements. Galleries of different angles that add no value waste "one of the most crucial important sections of the page". Turn photos into infographics: the product photo plus scannable value props and USPs on the image (benefits, social proof, how it works). Evidence: Wild Men Drinking +32% CR. Card caveat: do not drop accurate product depiction for overlays. [634fdf0d882d] [needs brand data: heatmap click share on gallery images]
11. **Chunk text into icons, bullets and subheads.** Large blocks of text are not read. Condition: long product descriptions and landing page sections. Evidence: Wild Men Drinking and Fiddler's Green (section 7). Card caveat: decorative icons add clutter without reducing effort. [2d9c1b326b14]
12. **People scan; write as if only the headlines get read.** Source says people read 20% of the content and scan the other 80%, so "operate under the framework that somebody will only read the headlines". Condition: every section headline, not only the hero. [b2e00b510c25] [web check: origin of the 20% read / 80% scan figure]
13. **Every headline answers "why should I care?"** The only thing a headline needs is the end benefit: "that one hard-hitting benefit that makes people think and say, take my money". Mistakes: vague headlines, jargon, and "the biggest conversion mistake of all time", which is explaining who you are and what you do in the headline. The test: if a visitor read only the headlines, would they have an incentive to buy? Evidence: Parcel Safety +80%, Carolina +157% (section 7). [b2e00b510c25]
14. **Quantify the value in the headline when you can.** Source says the best headlines in his A/B testing "quantify the value in the headline itself". Example: "trusted by happy customers" becomes "trusted by 700,000 happy customers". Condition: only with a number the brand can prove (card caveat). [b2e00b510c25] [needs brand data: real customer counts, review counts, results figures]
15. **Visual hierarchy: emphasize few things so they stand out.** Visual hierarchy is guiding the flow from the most important element down to the least important with font, size, color and weight. "In trying to emphasize everything, they've emphasized nothing at all." To implement: decide what matters most, give it the most prominence; make what matters less, less prominent. Good example: Prepared Hero (bold standout headline, subdued lighter body text, clearly separated icons and images, prominent button). Evidence: Parcel Safety above-the-fold test (section 7). [d9610985ed8c]
16. **Don't make people do math.** Show exactly how much the shopper saves with each bundle option. Source says "trust me, we've tested this a lot". Put the exact dollar amount saved in the tier headline, not a percentage in small sub-headline text. Criticizes Javvy coffee for not showing savings per option (opinion on a public site). Evidence: Wild Men Drinking +25% CR, +28.5% RPV. [c8277a1ff908]
17. **Show an image of each bundle and variant.** If bundle tiers are text only, add an image of the 3-pack, 4-pack and so on, so the shopper sees the difference without reading. Yeti is the good example: the image changes with both color and size. Evidence: Wild Men Drinking +32% CR. [4638fcca1797]
18. **Guide the choice with badges.** On collection pages, badge New, Most Popular and, "the most critical of all", Best Seller, because people want what others already buy. On variants and bundles, a contextual badge on the option you want chosen: I Restore Laser puts "for faster results" on the second (pricier) bundle; Perfect Snacks uses "Most Popular". Source says both nudge toward higher spend and raise AOV. Evidence: observation of public sites, no test. Card caveat: only badge claims that are true. [530251f31987] [needs brand data: which products are actually best sellers and most popular]
19. **Give immediate add-to-cart feedback with a slide-out cart drawer.** Reloading the whole page to confirm an add is "a completely disruptive process". A sliding cart drawer confirms instantly and gives room for upsells and cross-sells. Add a progress bar at the top that shows what the shopper must do to unlock higher discounts (Perfect Snacks example, source calls it "completely brilliant"). Evidence: opinion, no test. Card caveat: threshold discounts cost margin; model before rollout. [af92a8953b92] [needs brand data: margin and AOV distribution before setting unlock thresholds]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 The 5-step cognitive friction checklist (run on every page you create)

Source says to use this "every single time you create any website page". Order as given. [3f164438706e]

1. Remove elements: navigation items, PDP elements, bundle tiers, cart clutter.
2. Use images and icons instead of text: navigation, gallery, text blocks.
3. Replace vague messaging: benefit headlines, quantified where possible.
4. Fix visual hierarchy: one standout headline, one standout CTA, the rest subdued.
5. Simplify product selection: dollar savings in tier headlines, bundle images, badges, slide-out cart drawer with feedback.

The source does not say to test all 5 at once or in sequence; each of his results is reported as its own test. Test them one at a time so results can be attributed (card advice). [2d9c1b326b14]

### 3.2 Cut the navigation

1. Install Microsoft Clarity (or another session recording tool) and look at which navigation items get clicked. [verify UI] [bc8e0b03fd58]
2. Remove items with close to 0% of clicks. Source expects 2 to 3.
3. Remove "Home".
4. Remove About, Contact, Blog and FAQ from the main bar, or move them to a sub-menu in smaller, less prominent text. The curator header also names the footer; the speaker says "removed or put on a submenu".
5. Check the count: 7 items or fewer.
6. If the catalog is large, use a nested menu that shows a limited number of products at a time.
7. Expose the Shop menu directly. Do not hide shopping behind an extra click.
8. No test result is given for the nav cut alone. Source is thin on how to measure it; test against CR and RPV (see 3.3 metrics). [needs brand data: nav clicks, CR and RPV before and after]

### 3.3 Visual navigation test

1. Audit the nav for text-only menus. [7caa022c540c]
2. Variant A (Parcel Safety pattern): add images and icons of the product to each nav item, plus nested layers.
3. Variant B ("social media icon test"): lay all nav items out in the open as round, stories-style icons, like stories on social media apps. This saves a click and gives a fast visual snapshot of all product lines. The source does not say where on the page the icon row goes.
4. Keep the 7-item ceiling in mind so the visual treatment does not become clutter (card advice, not stated by the speaker).
5. Measure CR and RPV, not clicks only. [7caa022c540c]

### 3.4 Bundle tier reduction test

1. Watch session recordings on the bundle block. Signs of overwhelm: shoppers pick a lower-priced option, or exit the site. [af10d9d13cab]
2. Hypothesis: too many options cause analysis paralysis.
3. A/B test fewer tiers (source's case: 5 down to 3).
4. Measure CR and AOV together. In the source's case the 3-pack and 5-pack sold more after the cut.
5. Source does not say which tiers were cut. Sources 10 and 11 say the Wildman tiers were 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 packs cut to 1, 3, 5.

### 3.5 Bundle and variant block rebuild

1. Write the exact dollar saving in each tier headline. Source's before: savings as a percentage in small text in the sub-headline. After: exact dollar amount in the headline. The literal "Save $20" example comes from the curator header and card, not the speech. [c8277a1ff908]
2. Add an image of each bundle (the 1-pack, 3-pack, 4-pack as they look together). [4638fcca1797]
3. Make the image update when the shopper changes color or size (Yeti pattern). [verify UI: the store theme or bundle app must support per-variant images] [4638fcca1797]
4. Add 1 contextual badge to the option you want chosen ("Most Popular", or a benefit badge like "for faster results"). [530251f31987]
5. Test each change as its own variant. Source ran the dollar-savings and bundle-image changes as separate tests.

### 3.6 Gallery to infographics

1. Check heatmaps for click share on product images. [634fdf0d882d]
2. List gallery images that are just more angles of the same product.
3. Replace them with infographics: real product photo plus scannable value props, benefit sections, social proof and how-it-works callouts on the image.
4. Keep each image scannable, not a dense block of copy (card).
5. Test as its own variant.

### 3.7 Text chunking

1. Find large paragraph blocks on PDPs and landing pages. [2d9c1b326b14]
2. Rewrite each block into scannable icons, bullet points and sub-headlines.
3. Pair points with visuals where possible.
4. Test as a controlled variant.

### 3.8 Headline rewrite

1. List every headline on the page, top to bottom. [b2e00b510c25]
2. Read only the headlines. Ask: would I have an incentive to buy from these alone?
3. Flag any headline that is vague, uses jargon, or explains who the brand is and what it does.
4. For each, find the single end benefit that makes the shopper say "take my money".
5. Add a real number where one exists (customer count, result).
6. A/B test the rewritten page. Source ran full-page rewrites (Parcel Safety, Carolina), so the lift is not only from headlines.

### 3.9 Visual hierarchy fix (above the fold first)

1. Rank the elements: what must stand out most (usually the headline), second (the main CTA), and the rest. [d9610985ed8c]
2. Give the top elements the most size, weight and contrast: one large, bold headline; one standout CTA button (Parcel Safety after: red button).
3. Subdue the rest: lighter body text; badges on a neutral gray bar that does not compete with the CTA; icons and images clearly separated.
4. Check the flow top to bottom: headline, then supporting proof, then action.
5. Test it. Source ran the Parcel Safety above-the-fold redesign as an A/B test.

### 3.10 Cart feedback and drawer

1. Check what happens after Add to Cart. If the page reloads to confirm, flag it. [af92a8953b92]
2. Switch to a slide-out cart drawer that opens on add. [verify UI: theme setting or cart app]
3. Keep the drawer to: higher-tier options, items in cart, a few minor upsells and cross-sells, a clear next step.
4. Add a progress bar at the top showing what unlocks the next discount. Model margin first (card). [needs brand data: margin, AOV distribution]
5. Source gives no test result for the drawer or the progress bar.

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Numbers lack test detail.** No traffic, sample size, duration or significance for any result. Several "tests" were full page rebuilds (Parcel Safety +80%, Carolina +157%, Fiddler's Green +158%), so the lift cannot be credited to one change such as headlines or icons. Fiddler's Green reports store orders, not CR. The pet client revenue is "projected".
- **Two identical 32% results.** Bundle images and gallery infographics at the same client both show +32% CR. They may be separate tests with the same result, or one number reused. Treat both with care.
- **Same data as sources 10 and 11.** The 5-to-3 test (+53% CR, +13.5% AOV) and the dollar-savings test (+25% CR, +28.5% RPV here; +28.57% "revenue per user" in 10 and 11) appear in all 3. They are 1 agency's results repeated, not 3 independent confirmations. Do not count them 3 times in pass 2.
- **Conflict with sources 10 and 11 on the Parcel Safety above-the-fold change.** Sources 10 and 11 say no isolated number was given for it, and put Parcel Safety's overall gain at "upwards of 80%" from several quick wins. This source gives the above-the-fold redesign its own A/B result (+64% CR, +72% RPV) and gives +80% to a benefit-headline page rebuild. Keep both readings; do not merge.
- **Curator header overstates in 2 places.** It says visual nav increased "click-through rates"; the source measured CR and RPV. It gives "Save $20" as the headline example and says "rather than just listing the percentage"; the speaker gave no literal wording. It also says to move secondary links "to the footer"; the speaker said removed or a sub-menu.
- **Internal tension on navigation.** "Never more than 7 items" and "use nested menus" sit next to "expose everything, save a click" (story icons, exposed Shop menu). The source does not say how to reconcile them for a large catalog.
- **"Don't explain who you are" vs clarity.** The source calls "who we are, what we do" headlines the worst mistake. Source 01 in this folder shows a user tester who could not tell what Parcel Safety sold. A benefit-only headline must still make the product obvious somewhere above the fold; source 10's above-the-fold formula pairs the value prop with a supporting visual and proof. The source does not address this risk.
- **Badges must be true.** "Most Popular" or "Best Seller" on an item that is not can be a deceptive practice (card). The source frames badges as a subconscious nudge to raise AOV and does not mention truthfulness. [web check: consumer protection and dark pattern rules for the brand's market, for example India's dark pattern guidelines, FTC guidance]
- **Cart progress bars cost margin.** The source praises discount unlock bars with no margin math (card). [needs brand data: margin and AOV distribution]
- **Borrowed stats.** "60,000 times faster" and "read 20%, scan 80%" are stated without source. [web check: both claims]
