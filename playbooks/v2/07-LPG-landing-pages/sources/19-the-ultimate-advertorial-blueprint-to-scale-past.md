## 1. What this source is for

It gives a section-by-section and copy-level teardown of 1 long-form advertorial for a problem-solving product (a golf swing trainer). Use it to: place a pre-sell page between cold ads and the offer page; order the sections of an advertorial; build a short list of 3 to 5 pains and desires and repeat it; name 1 mechanism and thread it through the page; map testimonials to objections; write a risk reversal, a soft CTA and a "2 options" close. It is strong on copy structure and on how pain, desire and mechanism alternate line by line. It is thin on how to research the pains, how to test the page, what metrics to watch, what the offer page looks like, and on any proof that this page beats a product page. It gives no conversion rates, page lengths, word counts or design specs.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 Why and where an advertorial sits

1. **Put a pre-sell page between the cold ad and the offer.** Flow: prospecting ads, then the advertorial, then an offer page, then cart "or even directly into the checkout". In this example the CTA goes to an offer page, then checkout. Condition: cold prospecting traffic. The source's reason: "consumers are less willing to buy while your ad costs are increasing and more competition is entering the market". Evidence: the agency's claimed results only. [1ec80c7e259e]

2. **Do not work only on ads and product pages.** The presenter says "the mistake that most people do is they obsess too much with their ads and product pages rather than working with pre-sell pages and utilizing the full funnel". Condition: his opinion. He gives no test that shows the gap is where most funnels lose money. [1ec80c7e259e]

3. **Selling is showing the gap, then placing the product as the vehicle.** "Selling to people is creating a separation between where they are and where they want to be and then positioning your product as a vehicle." To do it you need the prospect's current state (pain points, problems, beliefs) and their desired state (goals, desires, aspirations). Condition: he gives it as a general rule for all selling. The source does not say how to research either state. [70ebc6c92534] [4da8204c9b2b]

### 2.2 Pain, desire and mechanism

4. **Push past the reader's pain threshold, not just their pain point.** He credits the idea to Tony Robbins: people live with pain until it passes a personal threshold. Only past that point do they act. So the copy must "really hit the pain points a lot". Evidence: opinion plus the page reading. His own illustration uses weight and dieting in blunt, stigmatising words. Do not reuse that wording. Condition: problem-solving products with a felt pain. The card adds a caveat the source does not give: heavy agitation can hurt trust with premium or repeat-purchase buyers. [70ebc6c92534]

5. **Alternate pain, desire and mechanism all the way down the page.** He colour-codes the page in 3 colours: pain points, desires, and the unique mechanism. His finding: "it's always going to be a mixture of hitting pain, hitting the desires and then somewhat positioning the product in between to bridge the gap". Example: the headline opens on a pain ("buy new training aids every year"), names the mechanism, then states the desire ("the last one you'll ever need"). [70ebc6c92534] [4da8204c9b2b]

6. **Pick 3 to 5 pains and desires, then repeat the same ones in many formats.** "You just literally hit the same three to five things throughout the whole advertorial." Formats he names: a listicle, a first-person story, feature copy, social proof. "We just bombard them again and again with the same things." Desires on the example page: confidence, enjoyment of the game, better ball striking, more control, consistency, "makes it easier", fast results, feeling like "a new me". Pains: mishits (fat shots, thin shots, slices, hooks), embarrassment, complication, wasting money on aids that fail. Condition: long-form advertorials. The card adds that the same density can read as pushy on short or premium pages; that is the card's caveat, not the presenter's. [4da8204c9b2b]

7. **Name 1 unique mechanism and use it everywhere.** The mechanism is the named feature that explains why this product works when others failed. On the example page it is "[fear or feel]-based feedback" (see name note). It appears in the headline, the subhead ("the only swing trainer designed to give you instant ... feedback"), every listicle point ("here they always link to the unique mechanism"), the origin story, the pain section and the close. Condition: categories where buyers have tried other fixes and doubt new ones. [46b86014e059]

8. **Contrast the mechanism with the failed old way.** Example lines: traditional training aids, "no matter how expensive and premium, often fail to provide instant feedback throughout your swing, causing those dreaded slices and hooks". Later: rival aids "could actually be killing contact and increasing your scores". Condition: only where the contrast is true and can be shown. The page backs it with unnamed "golf experts" and a "hidden design flaw"; that is an unsupported claim. [46b86014e059] [needs brand data: a real, provable difference between the product and the common alternatives]

### 2.3 Proof

9. **Tell an origin story for the mechanism.** Example: the brand consulted "golf engineers, coaches and seasoned instructors", studied what worked and what did not in traditional trainers, set out to replace methods that are "hard to master", "pioneered" the feedback system, and took "almost 2 years to perfect" it. Then it "added advanced materials and a weighting system". Condition: only with a true story from the brand. [46b86014e059] [needs brand data: the product's real development story]

10. **Use a long first-person proof story with a before-to-after arc.** Arc on the example page: low hopes ("I kind of had the feeling if this didn't work, nothing was going to help"); fast delivery ("within a couple business days"); first use at the range; small practical wins (not heavy, no setup, fits the bag's side pocket); first round with low expectation ("nothing works after just one round"); a modest result ("saved at least 4 to 5 shots", "I still had 2 or 3 misses"); hope ("imagine what could happen after a few more"); third-party notice (club pro and friends thought he took lessons); an ongoing habit (practice swings in the garage at night); a hedge ("can't say with 100% certainty that it will [work for you]"); then the guarantee ("they want you to get results or you don't pay"). Condition: the story must be a real customer's, with consent, and the results must be that customer's. The card calls the numbers fictional or illustrative. The source never says who the narrator is. Do-not-copy: a made-up narrator or invented results. [fc341da1e995] [needs brand data: a real customer story, with permission]

11. **Map each testimonial to 1 objection.** "If they have 5 different objections, then every testimonial should hit one of those objections." Not random praise. Example: a reviewer says the hardest part is the arm and hand position before squaring the face, and the trainer helped him "finally get the correct feel without having to guess". That answers the objection "will this help my arm and hand position?" He calls this a "quick bonus tip". Condition: any testimonial block. Do not edit or invent testimonials to fit objections. [5aaa4297b2ac] [needs brand data: top buying objections from reviews, support tickets and returns; real reviews that answer each]

12. **Claim fast results and a large user count.** The page says results come on the "very first swing" and first round; "over 33,000 golfers have eliminated fat shots, thin shots and other mishits" since testing began in 2023; the product "saves most amateur golfers 4 to 6 strokes on the very first round". It also suggests a tracking habit: count fairways and greens and "watch how it improves your scores". Condition: only numbers the brand can prove. The source gives no basis for any of these figures. Do-not-copy as written. [8bd4644d68ea] [needs brand data: real customer count, real time-to-result, real outcome ranges]

### 2.4 Risk reversal, CTA and close

13. **State a strong, plain guarantee and present it as confidence.** Example: 1-year money-back guarantee; "you will either love it or you get your money back, no questions asked"; "every single penny with zero hard feelings, no [hassles, transcribed "assets"], no annoying hoops"; "they know the likelihood of that happening is low" because of years of work; "if you aren't 110% satisfied" you get your money back. Paired with the offer (new customers get 50% off and free shipping; sold only on the brand's website). Condition: the brand must honour the terms. The card warns that long, no-questions guarantees raise refund exposure. [651985640c1d] [needs brand data: real guarantee window, refund rules, margin after refunds]

14. **Use a soft CTA that implies demand instead of "Buy now".** The first CTA reads "Apply discount, check availability". He calls it "a really nice scarcity frame", "not really pushy", implying "we have so many people that want it". Condition: only if stock is actually limited or the discount is actually applied. If stock is not limited, "check availability" is a false scarcity signal: do not copy. [cb940d13cea9]

15. **Put the first CTA after the risk reversal, then add more CTAs.** He says the page "could be the end of the story" at that first CTA. After it come 3 more sections: a pain section, a social proof stack, and the close, each with a CTA. [cb940d13cea9] [79e0f9206043]

16. **After the proof, hit the pain threshold once more.** With trust, proof, pains and desires already built, the late pain section "is really about hitting their pain threshold". Opens with a question ("How much longer will you struggle with poor ball striking that kills your confidence?"), restates the mechanism, says rival aids may make things worse, compares cost to "a fraction of the cost of traditional golf lessons", and ends on the fear of more search and embarrassment. [70ebc6c92534]

17. **Close with 2 options and give the reader a way out.** "We don't want to box them in ... we want to give them a way out, but we then want to really directly position the offer as the better way." Option 1: leave, keep old methods, buy an expensive brand-name aid "that is poorly built" or a cheap one with "the same design flaws". Option 2: "do what thousands of golfers before you have done" and try it risk-free. Then: reaching the bottom of the page "means you're ready to try something new", "trust yourself", "tap the green button". Restate discount and guarantee next to the last CTA. Condition: works only after the reader has seen the proof and guarantee (card caveat). [e893c433c3b8]

18. **Add page-only price scarcity at the close.** Example: "the special offer to save up to 50% off is only available for people who take action on this page here today. If you leave this page, [the discount] will only be available ... for its full regular price, which is double." Then "this is the only time you're ever going to see this offer." Condition: only if true and enforced. Most brands cannot honour "only on this page, only today". If not true, it is a fake deadline: do not copy. [cb940d13cea9] [web check: local consumer law on price comparisons and urgency claims for the target market]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Build the funnel

1. Send cold prospecting ads to the advertorial, not to the product page. [1ec80c7e259e]
2. Point every advertorial CTA to 1 offer page (or straight to checkout). [1ec80c7e259e]
3. Make sure the offer page shows the same discount, shipping and guarantee the advertorial promised. The source does not describe the offer page itself; it only clicks through to it. [cb940d13cea9]

The source gives no traffic split, no test plan and no success metric for this step.

### 3.2 Prepare the copy spine before writing

1. Write the prospect's current state: pain points, problems, beliefs. [4da8204c9b2b] [needs brand data: customer research, reviews, support tickets]
2. Write the desired state: goals, desires, aspirations. [4da8204c9b2b]
3. Cut each list to the main 3 to 5 items. [4da8204c9b2b]
4. Name the unique mechanism: 1 short, coined phrase for the real feature that explains why this works when other fixes failed. [46b86014e059] [needs brand data: what the product does differently, and proof]
5. List the top buying objections (his example assumes 5). [5aaa4297b2ac]
6. For each objection, find 1 real testimonial that answers it. [5aaa4297b2ac]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

1. **Funnel check.** Look at where cold ad clicks land. Good: an advertorial whose CTAs all go to 1 offer page or checkout with the same offer. Bad: cold ads straight to a product page while costs rise, or an advertorial CTA that lands on a page with a different price. Next: build or fix the pre-sell step. The source gives no metric that proves the advertorial is needed; judge it with the brand's own funnel numbers. [1ec80c7e259e] [needs brand data: cold-traffic conversion rate and CPA to product page versus advertorial]
2. **Pain threshold check.** Read the draft as the buyer. Good: the same few pains appear many times in different forms (list, story, question) and each is followed by a desire and the mechanism. Bad: each pain is named once and dropped. Next: add pain restatements in the listicle, story and late pain section. Watch the other side: for premium or repeat-purchase buyers the card warns this can harm trust. [70ebc6c92534]
3. **Mechanism thread check.** Search the draft for the mechanism name. Good: in the headline, subhead, each listicle point, origin story, pain section and close. Bad: named once, or several different names for the same feature. Next: pick 1 name and use it throughout. Also check it is true and provable. [46b86014e059]
4. **Colour-code check.** Run procedure 3.4. Good: every section mixes all 3 colours. Bad: a long block of only features, or only pain. [4da8204c9b2b]
5. **Objection map check.** Write the top objections in 1 column, the testimonial that answers each in the next. Good: every objection has 1 real testimonial. Bad: several testimonials say "love it" and some objections have none. Next: find real reviews that answer the gaps; do not write them. [5aaa4297b2ac]
6. **Claim check (do before publishing).** List every number, endorsement, press mention, user count, "guarantees", "only", "sold out" and expert claim. Good: each has a file, link or record behind it. Bad: any line like the example's "top PGA pro", "featured on Golf Channel", "33,000 golfers", "4 to 6 strokes", "golf experts say". Next: remove or replace with real proof. [8bd4644d68ea] [46b86014e059] [needs brand data: proof file for each claim]
7. **Scarcity check.** For each urgency line ("check availability", "only on this page today", "full price is double"), ask: is it true, and will the brand enforce it? Bad: the same discount is on the site tomorrow. Next: remove it or use a real limit. [cb940d13cea9]
8. **Guarantee check.** Good: the page's guarantee matches the brand's real policy word for word, and ops and margin can carry it. Bad: the page says "1 year, no questions asked" and the policy says 30 days. [651985640c1d] [needs brand data: refund policy and refund rate]
9. **Disclosure check.** Good: the page makes clear it is from the brand or is an ad. Bad: a "review team" or neutral-writer voice selling the brand's own product with no disclosure. The source does not raise this. [web check: ad disclosure rules for the market and platform]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Obsessing over ads and product pages and skipping pre-sell pages and the full funnel. [1ec80c7e259e]
2. Naming pain points once instead of pushing past the pain threshold. He calls this the thing "most marketers just not get right". [70ebc6c92534]
3. Random testimonials that do not answer a specific objection. [5aaa4297b2ac]
4. A hard "buy now" CTA that feels pushy. He prefers a "check availability" frame. [cb940d13cea9]
5. Boxing the reader in at the close. Give a way out (option 1) and then show why option 2 is better. [e893c433c3b8]

Mistakes the cards add, not stated by the presenter: advertising scarcity the brand will not honour [cb940d13cea9]; inventing or editing testimonials [5aaa4297b2ac]; unproven mechanism claims [46b86014e059]; unprovable user counts and results [8bd4644d68ea]; a guarantee the margin cannot carry [651985640c1d]; using the close before the proof is in place [e893c433c3b8].
