## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator decide where to put CRO effort and how to write the top of a landing page or PDP. It gives 3 things: a case for fixing conversion rate before buying more traffic, a testing routine (1 test a week, headline first, then hero image, double down on winners), and a way to audit page copy against Hormozi's Value Equation (dream outcome, perceived likelihood, time delay, effort and sacrifice). It is strongest on what to test and where to place trust and speed claims. It is thin on test design, sample sizes and any tool steps.

## 2. Principles

1. **Conversion rate is the lever to pull when traffic gets more expensive.** Condition: paid traffic on auction platforms, where costs rise as more businesses bid for the same eyeballs. Hormozi (quoting a Neil Patel keynote) says the future of marketing is CRO, because whoever converts the same eyeballs better can outbid competitors. The presenter backs this with unsourced year-over-year cost rises: Meta +61%, TikTok +185%, Google CPMs +75%, search CPCs +14%. 2. **Doubling conversion rate beats doubling traffic.** Condition: when the page is not yet "fundamentally optimized". Presenter's reasoning: a higher-converting page raises the return on all traffic you send later, so any added traffic is also amplified. He says his agency has helped clients "literally double their conversion rate" through CRO alone (no numbers or names). Opinion plus agency experience. [c5c80c60d2f6]
3. **An efficiency gain lasts and widens LTV to CAC.** Condition: a real, held conversion improvement. Hormozi says efficiency improvements "typically are long-lasting". A better LTV to CAC ratio lets you spend more on ads and on hiring, and he calls it "a competitive moat". Reason: everyone wants to test 100 things; limiting to 1 forces focus on the idea most likely to move results. 19. **Effort and sacrifice: say what the buyer will not have to do.** Condition: products or services that remove work the buyer dislikes. 20. **For consumer goods, the dream outcome is often status, and it takes more translation than B2B.** Condition: premium or discretionary physical products. Hormozi: B2B is "pretty easy, it's just this is how much money it is", then you make it easier, less risky and faster. ## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Decide between more traffic and more conversion [c5c80c60d2f6]
1. Check whether the page is fundamentally optimized: does the top of the page say who you are, what you do and what's in it for the buyer within 5 seconds? [accfa461c58b]
2. If not, fix the page before adding traffic. The presenter says many companies that complain about low conversion rates have pages that are "not fundamentally optimized for conversions in the first place".
3. Card-added steps (not in the speech): model the LTV to CAC effect of a conversion gain, and gate spend increases on measured conversion. [needs brand data: LTV, CAC, conversion rate by page]

### 3.2 Weekly testing routine (source-stated parts) [91a259fd4a85] [bfdcedabc90c]
1. Check traffic. If under about 1,000 visitors a week, do not plan 1 A/B test a week (see 3.3). [needs brand data: weekly sessions per page]
2. Put baseline traffic and conversion rate into an online A/B testing calculator. Read how long each test must run and how many tests you can run. The source does not name a calculator or give settings. [verify UI]
3. In a weekly marketing meeting, pick 1 test only: the idea you think will move results most.
4. Default order: headline first, then the hero image under it, then other elements. [cb7820a5960b] [3baf2881eea1]
5. Run it as a split test. Change 1 element per test so the result can be attributed (card wording; the source implies it by testing headline and image separately).
6. Record wins and losses. Card adds: log hypothesis, variant and result in a shared document. [91a259fd4a85]
7. After a win, design the next test to push the same idea further before switching topics (the price-objection example: +32%, then +88% cumulative).
8. Carry the insight into ads and positioning; drop ideas that lost. [48195b82871f]

### 3.3 Low-traffic path [bfdcedabc90c]
1. Applies to the presenter's "10 to 20,000 visitors a month" clients.
2. Collect "objective data" in Microsoft Clarity. The source does not say which reports; the card names session recordings and heatmaps. [verify UI]
3. Make the change the data points to.
4. Measure what happens over time. The source gives no window. The card adds: keep the change only if the metric holds after a full business cycle, and re-run the calculator when traffic or baseline shifts.
5. Treat the result as directional, not as a split-test result.

### 3.4 Write and test the headline [accfa461c58b] [799ca4958608] [3baf2881eea1]
1. Write down what the product does.
2. Run the "so that" chain until you reach an outcome the buyer cares about in daily life (time, money, more deals, status, weight lost).
3. Check the headline answers who, what, and what's in it for me in 5 seconds or less. The benefit and core differentiator must be clear.
4. Quantify the outcome and time if you can ("lose 30 lbs in 90 days"). [c028f18c0982]
5. Use the body text under the headline to reinforce the value prop and add proof that makes the outcome likely (Inboxy: "the only inbox warm-up solution that utilizes a private network of headless browsers... to truly guarantee you better deliverability").
6. Only then write variants. Each variant is a different value prop or a different way to say the end benefit.
7. Split test the variants (see 3.2).

### 3.5 Test the hero image [96baec725a87]
1. Ask whether the current image says anything about the value prop. A "boring image" that conveys nothing is the failure case.
2. Look for existing proof assets you already have (Influence Hunter used the content it had produced for clients).
3. Build a new image from that proof, for example a collage or carousel that shows "social proof at scale".
4. Test it on its own, separate from the headline.
5. If it wins, try the same concept in ads. [48195b82871f]

### 3.6 Value Equation page audit [e05d68803613]
1. Dream outcome: is the end goal stated in the headline and subheadline above the fold?
2. Perceived likelihood: are there case studies, testimonials, a guarantee, a return policy, or a trial near the CTA?
3. Time delay: does the page say how fast the buyer gets the product or the result, in copy or on the CTA itself?
4. Effort and sacrifice: does any copy say what the buyer will not have to do?
5. Card-added step: score all 4 and fix the weakest first rather than rewriting everything.

### 3.7 Add a trust block under Add to Cart [e0053984b930]
1. List the buyer's uncertainties at the moment of purchase. The card adds: will it arrive, can I return it, is it guaranteed, does it work with my setup.
2. Pick only policies you actually offer. Parcel Safety's set: free 30-day return, 100% money-back guarantee, 1-year warranty, free US shipping. Prepared Hero's set: guaranteed secure checkout, ships in 24 hours. [needs brand data: actual policies]
3. Place them directly under the Add to Cart button, not in the footer.
4. A/B test the page with badges against the page without (Parcel Safety: +30%).
5. If price is the main objection, move the price lower on the PDP and put trust elements above it; if that wins, add more trust above the price and test again. [91a259fd4a85] [needs brand data: objection data, e.g. surveys, support tickets, chat logs]

### 3.8 Effort-reduction headline [0a3d01c3c5d8]
1. Interview users about the outcome they want. Note the manual, tedious work they complain about.
2. Write copy that names that work and says you do it for them ("so that you don't have to").
3. For heavy or complex goods, name service elements: white-glove delivery, fitting through doorways, assembly (Hormozi's table example). [e16763c803e8]
4. The card adds: place it high on the page and pair it with proof such as volume numbers.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Overlap with other sources in this folder.** The presenter describes himself exactly as the presenter of source 10 does ("400+ landing pages in 80+ niches") and uses the same client, Parcel Safety. Treat this as the same agency and the same person as source 10. Source 11 ("Brutally Honest Landing Page Advice from Alex Hormozi") was not read. - **Speaker attribution is inferred.** The transcript has no labels. Hormozi's clips (1 test a week, headline then image, the portfolio wins, the Value Equation, the table) and the presenter's commentary (all agency numbers, the 60% figure, the ad-cost figures) are separated here by wording only.
- **Internal inconsistency on traffic.** The floor for 1 test a week is "under 1,000 visitors a week", but the "low traffic" clients who skip A/B tests have 10,000 to 20,000 a month, which is about 2,300 to 4,600 a week. So the presenter's real cut-off for true A/B testing is higher than 1,000 a week. - **Conflict with common ecommerce testing advice.** Common advice is to run each test at least 1 to 2 full weeks to cover weekday cycles, which clashes with a fixed 1-test-a-week cadence unless tests overlap. [web check: current guidance on minimum test duration]
- **The 60% no-scroll figure is unsourced** and has no device split. Many ecommerce shoppers on mobile scroll. Do not use it as a benchmark. [web check: scroll-depth studies] [needs brand data: scroll depth from Clarity or analytics]
- **"Above the fold" on a PDP.** The advice is written for landing pages with a headline and hero image. On a standard ecommerce PDP the top screen is usually product title, gallery, price and Add to Cart. The source does not say how to apply "headline first" to a PDP.
- **Moving price down the page** runs against the common ecommerce advice to show price clearly near the title. The source's win came from a specific price objection versus competitors. Only test it when that objection is proven. [needs brand data: objection data]
- **Before/after changes are confounded** by seasonality, promos and traffic mix. The source admits it is not a true A/B test but gives no method to control for this.
- **Ad-cost figures** have no year, source or region. Do not cite them. [web check]
- **Lift numbers** (130%, 60 to 80%, 78%, 32%/88%, 30%) have no traffic, duration, device mix or significance. Big lifts often come from a weak starting page; the card for the image test says so.
- **The Value Equation is not given as a formula** here, and the source does not say how to weigh the 4 parts. Scoring and "fix the weakest first" come from the card, not the speech.
- **B2B skew.** About half the examples are B2B or SaaS (Inboxy, Scrubby, Ryden, Influence Hunter). The ecommerce ones are Parcel Safety, Prepared Hero, AVI and the unnamed price-objection client.
