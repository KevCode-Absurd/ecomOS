## 1. What this source is for

## 2. Principles

1. **Raising conversion rate beats raising traffic.** Condition: you already buy or earn traffic. The presenter's framing: 2 ways to double revenue, double traffic or double conversion rate; he picks conversion rate because every future visitor is then worth more, so ad spend is amplified and scaling becomes profitable. Converting the same traffic better than competitors becomes the "competitive moat". Evidence: agency client examples only (one client more than doubled conversion rate, revenue and profit; a B2B client went from 1% to 7% over several months). He notes there are "over 5 times more ad agencies" than CRO agencies (unsourced). Cards add: doubling traffic usually roughly doubles spend; CRO cannot fix a weak offer or wrong audience; volume doubling only helps if inventory and fulfilment can absorb it. [d82dfcf1d7d7] [8cf491c30c46] [needs brand data: current conversion rate by page and by source]
2. **Not A/B testing a page that already gets a lot of traffic is also a loss.** Condition: page has enough traffic (see rule 25). The presenter calls this "arguably even worse" than a low-converting page because competitors are squeezing out incremental gains. [8cf491c30c46]
3. **Today's buyer sees dozens to hundreds of ads a day, so a thin page burns cash.** Condition: paid traffic. The page must say clearly what the benefit is and what makes you different from every other company. Opinion based on his audits of leads. [338245ec724b]
4. **Answer "why should I care" in the first 5 seconds.** Condition: every page. Pages that only say who you are and what you do fail. People exit if the benefit is not clear in 5 seconds (presenter's claim). Source for the benefit: interview customers and reflect on their biggest pain points and what makes you unique. [338245ec724b] [fff98a4880b0] [needs brand data: customer interview notes, review mining]
5. **Good design raises trust and conversion (presenter's "hill to die on").** Condition: strongest for high-ticket offers; he says no one spends $5,000+ (his surgeon clients) on a page that looks poorly put together. He says an ugly page made "10 times better" looking would "chances are" convert "10 times higher"; treat that as hyperbole. He means clean, organized, digestible sections, not animations. He admits others disagree. Card caveat: less proven for low-cost impulse products. [338245ec724b]
6. **Treat the landing page as a salesperson.** Condition: cold traffic that knows nothing about the brand. Needs multiple value prop sections, multiple social proof sections, and handling of fear, uncertainty, doubt and objections. A cold visitor will not "magically convert" from a bare page. His best pages are "multiple sections long" with many value props, case studies and proof. Card caveat: relevance matters more than length. [338245ec724b]
7. **Match page to ad and to traffic awareness.** Condition: every paid page. Ad promise, page headline and offer must match. Meta traffic is mostly unaware or problem-aware, so the page must educate, build trust and state value. Google Ads and organic search traffic is more solution-aware or ready to buy, so the page can be direct (form above the fold), with the rest of the page reinforcing credibility and benefits. [338245ec724b] [0441a8cce669] [0c22726bae24] [af3eaeba1a44] [needs brand data: traffic mix by source per landing page]
8. **Do data analysis before designing.** Condition: always, before any build or rebuild. Watch converted sessions, rage clicks, heat and scroll maps; then add qualitative user tests. "Validate all of your assumptions with data." [19ceeeff7acf] [98544575e3db]
9. **Above the fold gets 80 to 90% of early effort.** Condition: when starting out. It is the only section 100% of visitors see, and the presenter says "statistically 60%" never scroll past it (unsourced). He reports conversion increases "upwards of 130%" from testing the fold alone (client result). [f226437ce140] [web check: current scroll-depth research on share of users who scroll past the first screen]
10. **Social proof belongs at the very top, and 2 forms beat 1.** Condition: above the fold. It gives the 60% who would leave a reason to scroll. "The world's top websites" always put proof at the top. Presenter says they A/B tested this "a lot" and 2 forms beat 1 (no data shown). An education client used 3 forms and saw a 132% rise in qualified leads and conversions (bundled redesign). [f226437ce140] [79b4db61ba63]
11. **People scan; write every heading as the benefit.** Condition: every section. Presenter's stat: people read 20% and scan 80% (unsourced). Assume visitors read only headlines. No generic headings like "Our services", "Here's what we do", "What makes us unique". [2877b10aa93f] [90790635ecd5] [web check: origin of the 20% read claim]
12. **Benefit, not feature.** Condition: value prop copy. Pair each feature with the benefit using "so that". Example: "Fast and easy 10-minute treatment" (feature) becomes "a life with clear vision" (benefit); "Clear and timely reports" becomes "make smarter and faster decisions" (so that I can grow my bottom line). [90790635ecd5]
13. **Proof must be verifiable.** Condition: review sections. Show the platform source (Google, Trustpilot) on each review so it can be checked. Cards add: never fabricate volume or attribution. [3f41ff67021b] [79b4db61ba63] [85e78b3e4eb7]
14. **Keep visitors on the page.** Condition: dedicated paid landing pages. Remove nav links to other pages, external links in testimonials (LinkedIn, websites) and outbound featured-logo links. If people click proof, send them to an on-page anchor or your own reviews page, not off-site. Card caveats: removing navigation can hurt pages that are also organic entry points, and core product pages may need some navigation. [e95750b86b05] [89e1eb35d667] [e225b45e3d43] [47c52d23ae2e]
15. **Every section is another chance to convert.** Condition: long pages. Think of the page as a journey: not convinced yet, show proof; still not convinced, show a value prop. Scatter reviews between value props and at critical points to show "it's not just us saying it". [e296505d973c] [85e78b3e4eb7]
16. **Make results feel likely and easy.** Condition: how-it-works section. 3 or 4 steps, ending with the outcome the buyer wants. [d6a9cf8c165e]
17. **Order sections by importance to the buyer, and move them up if people do not reach them.** Condition: scroll data shows key sections (case studies, testimonials, value props) are reached by few visitors. "The further a section is down the page, the less likely somebody reaches it." [98544575e3db] [a88919c97b72]
18. **AI copy is a starting point, never final.** Condition: headline generation. His team does "tons of iterations" because copy must be relevant to the brand and small wording tweaks matter. [d582b28ffa4e]
19. **Testing compounds.** Condition: enough traffic. Each win becomes the base for the next test ("snowball effect"). Example: one hypothesis produced a 32% first win and 88% cumulative lift (section 7). [de130801d763] [fe0b0cc42e76]
20. **No two landing pages are the same.** The presenter hedges his template: his team runs dozens of design explorations, discovery and brainstorming calls per project, so the template is a base, not a final answer. [e296505d973c]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Pre-build brief (4 questions)
Answer before designing. [fff98a4880b0]
1. Who is the page for?
2. What pain point are they dealing with? (From customer interviews, not assumption.) [needs brand data: interviews, reviews, support tickets]
3. What is the customer really trying to solve or achieve? (Card adds: split functional from emotional outcome.)
4. What stage of awareness are they in? (Set by traffic source: Meta = unaware or problem-aware; Google Ads and SEO = solution-aware or ready to buy.) [needs brand data: which sources feed this page]
5. Card adds: keep this brief open while writing headline, subheadline and CTA so the copy does not drift into describing the company. [fff98a4880b0]

### 3.2 Watch converted sessions in Microsoft Clarity [verify UI]
Clarity is free with "unlimited data" per the presenter [web check: Clarity limits]. [19ceeeff7acf]
1. Install Clarity on the site.
2. Open the Recordings tab. Have a notebook ready and write down insights.
3. Filters > scroll to Path > "visited URL" "contains" > enter the thank-you page path (for example `/thank-you/`, remove the domain part) > Apply. Every recording shown is now a converter. (For a store, use the order confirmation page path.) [needs brand data: thank-you or order-confirmation URL]
4. In the left list read referrer, location, entry URL and exit URL for each session. "More details" shows location and entry URL.
5. Favorite useful recordings in advance for review.
6. Watch each: which sections they read, where the cursor pauses, where they scroll back up, what they clicked, how long until conversion.
7. Log patterns over many recordings: where people convert, where they drop off, where they spend time, where they pause. Decide what to improve or double down on.
8. Card adds for stores: the equivalent proof is reviews, ratings, delivery and returns info rather than case studies. [19ceeeff7acf]

### 3.3 Find blockers with rage clicks [verify UI]
[ee5a74cd7f5c] [47c52d23ae2e]
1. Recordings > Filters > User actions > Rage clicks.
2. Watch for elements people click repeatedly that do nothing (things that look clickable and are not, or should be clickable and are not).
3. Decide carefully: on a landing page do not send people off the page. Example fix from his own site: visitors rage-clicked the customer ratings; the fix is to make them link to the site's reviews page, or on a landing page to scroll down to the on-page reviews section.
4. Featured-in logos draw the same clicks; do not link them out.
5. Cards add: apply to size charts, shipping thresholds, payment badges, bundle thumbnails and swatches; recheck rage clicks after the fix; some mobile rage clicks are accidental taps.

### 3.4 Read scroll maps [verify UI]
[98544575e3db] [a88919c97b72]
1. Heatmaps (top menu) > pick the most viewed page.
2. Select the Scroll metric. Pick Mobile first ("that's typically where the majority of traffic is").
3. If the screenshot looks wrong (for example hamburger menu open), click "Change screenshot" and pick a correct one.
4. Find the average fold (what people see without scrolling), then the 75%, 50% and 25% reach lines.
5. Look for big empty white blocks, above all between the average fold and the first proof. Cut them so proof (for example scrolling reviews) sits above the average fold. "Every piece of real estate is super valuable" on mobile; sections should breathe, but not that much.
6. Check whether the sections you know buyers care about (case studies, testimonials, value props) are reached. If a key section is reached by about 30% of visitors, move it up and improve everything above it.
7. Switch to desktop (PC icon), change screenshot if needed, repeat the checkpoints.
8. If 50% drop right after the fold, the fold (message, value prop, design) is "leaking". Fix the fold first.

### 3.5 Read click maps and area maps [verify UI]
[e225b45e3d43] [e95750b86b05] [89e1eb35d667]
1. Heatmaps > select Tap (click) map.
2. On the left pick "Area" to see the share of all clicks per region. Use "Edit areas" to select regions.
3. Compare the CTA buttons' click share with everything else. CTA getting fewer clicks than testimonials near the top is a "huge red flag". Presenter's benchmark: CTA buttons should get about 3 to 5% (he calls it click-through rate; in Clarity's area map it is share of page clicks).
4. Check navigation click share. In his example the nav took 33.2% of clicks. On a landing page, remove the nav and replace it with a CTA button ("book a consultation") and a phone number.
5. Check links inside testimonials (LinkedIn, customer websites). In his example they took 3.28% of clicks, each an exit. Remove them.
6. Check each CTA's look. A CTA that is plain text and not underlined got 0% of clicks. Make it a real, larger, prominent button.

### 3.6 Qualitative user test with UserBrain [verify UI]
Use when traffic is low (under about 25,000 visitors) or to learn why. Presenter pairs it with Clarity. [1255665f3d67] [e7747485521b]
1. Create a free account. Cost about $45 per tester at recording time [web check: UserBrain current price]. 3 to 5 tests are "more than enough" to get most insights.
2. Create new test > Create a website test. Enter name, URL, language, length 5 to 20 minutes. Choose "Start from scratch".
3. First question, before any scroll or click: "Based on what you see above the fold, do not scroll or click yet. What are your first impressions of the page?"
4. Tick "Ask testers if they completed the task successfully" on every task; it forces confirmation and shows they read the task.
5. Use the plain "Task" type. Avoid multiple choice, single choice and written response types; get people to talk aloud.
6. End every task with: "Please walk through your thought process. Once done, go to the next task." Testers hold back unless reminded to talk aloud and be honest.
7. Duplicate the task block for each task.
8. Ecommerce tasks: search (large catalogs), find the right product (large catalogs), add to cart and go through checkout, overall opinion of experience, design and emotions felt. B2B tasks: copy and messaging, first impressions, find specific info (services, about us).
9. Get testers: set device type (use your main device), country, age range, gender.
10. Screening: up to 2 questions, single select. Avoid yes/no. Use graded options. His example: "How involved are you in evaluating or purchasing solutions for your team?" with "I'm the final decision maker / I influence purchasing decisions / I use tools but don't choose them / Not applicable". Set approve or reject per option. Save.
11. Review recordings for repeated hesitation and turn them into page changes. Cards add: re-run the same tasks after changes. [e7747485521b]

### 3.7 Build the page layout (the template)
Section order for the default (problem-aware) page. Presenter shares it as a Figma file; open it, click the arrow, "Duplicate" to your drafts [verify UI]. [e6a8d5dff241] [f226437ce140] [e296505d973c]
1. **Navigation bar.** Logo left. A CTA button and a phone number, both clickable (tel link) and sticky while scrolling so people can convert at any depth; "especially critical" on mobile. No links to other pages. Phone is "super important" for service businesses (clinics, bookkeeping, lawyers, trades). [89e1eb35d667]
2. **Above the fold** (in this order in the template): social proof 1 above the headline; benefit headline; subheadline; benefit CTA button; image or video that reinforces the value prop; social proof 2 under the image or headline block. Tight spacing, clear visual hierarchy. [f226437ce140]
3. **Pain-to-proof section** (problem-aware traffic): call out the exact pain ("this is exactly me"), agitate (what they tried that failed), present your solution as the way out, CTA right there. For solution-aware traffic, swap: social proof goes here and pain moves below it, shorter. [52706461f320] [0441a8cce669]
4. **Social proof: "wall of love."** Mixed video and text reviews, fully exposed grid (no carousel), platform source (Google, Trustpilot) in the bottom right of each card, a headline on each review summarizing it. Alternative design: video testimonials plus client result stats. [3f41ff67021b] [79b4db61ba63]
5. **Value prop sections ("the meat").** 3 to 4 as a bare minimum, up to 8 to 10 for complex offers. Each: benefit headline, feature as support. [90790635ecd5]
6. **Another social proof section.** Examples: scrolling logos near the top plus case studies lower; reviews scattered between value props; HVAC rotating carousel showing the install photo plus a review. [85e78b3e4eb7]
7. **Benefit-driven section: 3 to 6 more selling points** (template shows 6 as placeholder). Headline, body, more on what makes you different. Design varies: 4 value props with graphic and embedded proof (SaaS); comparison table with X and check marks versus alternatives (laser eye versus traditional procedures and glasses or contacts); add a line of text in table cells for complex offers. [c3c1046803f6]
8. **How it works.** 3 or 4 steps, high level, results feel effortless. Last step is the end benefit. [d6a9cf8c165e]
9. **Meet the team.** Team photos or founder photo for personality, "people buy from people"; mainly service, B2B, coaching.
10. **Guarantee (optional).** Only if you have one; otherwise replace with a proof or value prop section.
11. **One more social proof section.**
12. **FAQs.** The most common questions from sales calls and client recordings. (Card adds for stores: support tickets, reviews, on-site search.) [e296505d973c] [needs brand data: top pre-purchase questions]
13. **Closer section** (see 3.9).

Note: the transcript also says "the next critical section right after the pain point area" is the value props, so the wall of love and pain section positions shift with awareness; the order is not fixed. [0441a8cce669]

### 3.8 Write above-the-fold copy
[4fb1b734b296] [ec11504efa22] [6dbf3e919d60]
1. **Headline:** "dream outcome" and "what's in it for me". Not who you are, not what you do. One hard-hitting benefit that makes people say "take my money". Build in beats: outcome, then credibility, then fear removal. Quantify value and timeline. Mix emotional and functional benefit.
2. **Subheadline:** explains how you deliver the headline. Contrast format "Stop doing X / no more Y [pain]. Our solution does X, Y, Z." Lead with pain, then the unique or proprietary way, listing the unique selling points as X, Y, Z. Add an effort or risk clause ("zero effort required from you").
3. **Social proof:** 2 forms (for example stats plus review count).
4. **CTA:** benefit-driven, large, stands out (padding, size). Name the outcome ("See how to end your dizziness"; "Get instant quote" not "Get a free quote", because "instant" implies speed). Card adds for stores: keep "Add to cart" where the platform expects it and make the copy around it benefit-led. [6dbf3e919d60]
5. **Image or video:** must reinforce the core value prop and show what you do. No images unrelated to the offer. Midjourney or stock (Pexels) works, but the presenter says custom graphics set a brand apart (this is also his agency's service).
6. **Form-above-the-fold variant** (high-awareness search traffic only): 1 benefit headline, 3 to 4 bullets of what they get by filling the form plus extra benefits, 1 large CTA button with a risk reducer or trust element under it. Do not use for Meta traffic; asking before educating is "premature". [0c22726bae24] [af3eaeba1a44]
7. **Progressive disclosure** for forms: ask 1 simple field first (email); reveal more fields only after they fill it. Once one field is filled, people are "way more likely" to finish. No data given. [50c99cc00c00]
8. **Tighten the fold:** no empty white blocks; fit headline, body, stats, form, proof, phone and CTA in the first screen without overwhelming. [98544575e3db]
9. **Visual hierarchy:** use font size, weight, colour and boldness to set reading order. His example: headline about 32 to 33 px, body about 16 px in a different colour (black versus blue headline), CTA in brand blue, large, all caps. [2877b10aa93f]

### 3.9 Build the closer section
[e296505d973c] [d436d80a4ccb]
1. **Lead gen or service:** no generic headline ("Contact us", "Get a quote"). Use 1 large benefit headline that recaps the dream outcome. Subheadline gives 1 final reason to act. 2 forms of social proof, as in the fold. 3 to 5 bullets of what they get by filling the form. Then the form.
2. **Form fields:** keep simple. One "Full name" field instead of first and last, unless you need them split. Separate placeholders from labels; on mobile people forget what a field was for when the label vanishes.
3. **Course, program or certificate:** use a value stack. Overview of what they get (number of videos, hours of content, perks), then module 1 with what you get plus a testimonial, module 2 plus testimonial, module 3 plus testimonial.
4. **Ecommerce:** product selection is the main action and sits near the top of the page. Make selection seamless. For bundles, show exactly what they save; "don't make people do math" (presenter says A/B tested "a lot", no numbers). Put social proof and FUD reducers near the add-to-cart point. Make add to cart seamless: no full-page refresh to confirm; use a sliding cart that lets you cross-sell and upsell, which he says raises conversion and AOV. Example: furniture site redesign making "laborious" product selection seamless (no numbers). [d436d80a4ccb] [verify UI: theme cart drawer support] [needs brand data: bundle prices and savings]

### 3.12 Build on WordPress plus Elementor [verify UI]

1. Hosting: Flywheel (or WP Engine). He avoids budget shared hosts (GoDaddy, Bluehost) because shared resources slow the site. Flywheel "Tiny" plan if under 5,000 visitors a month; "Starter" or "Freelance" above that [web check: Flywheel plans and prices].

10. Publish on a subdomain, for example `lp.yourdomain.com`.
11. Card adds: keep pages light (remove unused plugins, compress images, avoid heavy widgets); page builders add weight, so watch Core Web Vitals on mobile. See 08-SPD-speed. [64e822fe1ad0]

### 3.13 Decide whether to A/B test
[5b56dd4acd81] [fe0b0cc42e76]
1. Count monthly visitors to the single landing page (not the whole site). [needs brand data: monthly sessions per landing page]
2. Under 25,000 to 30,000 a month: do not A/B test; "it's going to take forever to reach a conclusive result". Use Clarity and UserBrain and make clear fixes.
3. Above 25,000 to 30,000 a month on one page: set up A/B tests.
4. Loop: run test, measure, find why it won or lost (Clarity, Google Analytics), repeat.

### 3.14 Set up a test in FigPii [verify UI]
[de130801d763]
1. Create a free account. Install the tracking code in the site's head (search for a tutorial or ask FigPii support).
2. A/B testing > Create new experiment > name it.
3. Type: "A/B test" (visual editor, for simple changes: headline, image, swap the fold) or "Split URL" (2 different page URLs, for example LP1 versus LP2).
4. Devices and visitors: all, mobile or desktop; visitor type all, new or returning. He uses "all" in most cases.
5. Targeting: the landing page URL.
6. Goals: goal type "page view", "contains", the thank-you URL (counts form fills).
7. Variations, split URL: enter the second URL; FigPii redirects and splits 50/50 (custom weights possible; with 2 or 3 variations it splits evenly).
8. Variations, A/B test: Add variation > front-end editor opens > left-click the element > Edit element > type the new text > OK > Save and continue.
9. Sample size: leave defaults. Save and launch.
10. Read results under "All": win or loss, "chance to win", and revenue per visitor impact.
11. Cards add (not in source): commit to a primary metric and decision date first, do not stop early, keep visitor assignment consistent, freeze page changes during a visual test. [de130801d763]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Headline explains who you are and what you do instead of why the visitor should care. [338245ec724b]
2. No clear offer and no difference from competitors. [338245ec724b]
3. Believing "ugly pages convert" and skipping design polish. [338245ec724b]
4. Bare-bones pages on paid traffic with little information. [338245ec724b]
5. Random sections, generic text and images unrelated to the offer; no thought about traffic source. [338245ec724b]
6. Page does not match the ad. [0c22726bae24]
7. Same layout for every traffic source; putting a form above the fold for problem-aware Meta traffic ("premature"). [0441a8cce669] [af3eaeba1a44]
8. Obsessing over traffic while ignoring conversion; or getting lots of converting traffic and running no A/B tests. [8cf491c30c46]
9. Designing without looking at data first. [19ceeeff7acf]
10. Large empty white blocks, above all on mobile, pushing proof below the fold. [98544575e3db]
11. Putting the "golden" sections (case studies, reviews) so low that most visitors never reach them. [98544575e3db]
12. Navigation menus and external links (LinkedIn, customer websites, featured logos) that pull clicks off the page. [89e1eb35d667] [e225b45e3d43]
13. CTAs that look like plain text; small CTAs that do not stand out. [e225b45e3d43]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Built for lead gen, not ecommerce.** The template, closer, forms, phone, team and case-study advice is for services and B2B. Ecommerce gets 1 closer paragraph and user-test tasks. The ecommerce section order in e296505d973c and the product-page cards are adaptations by the card writer. Pass 2 should weigh them below ecommerce-native sources.
- **Unsourced stats:** 60% never scroll past the fold, people read 20%, carousels have low engagement, 5x more ad agencies, 5 seconds to exit. [web check: scroll-depth and reading-behaviour research]
- **Lift numbers bundle many changes** (132%, 108%, 187%, 64%) and come from the seller's own clients. The free-audit "average 64% lift" is a sales claim.
- **Conflict with common ecommerce advice: price placement.** His ecommerce test moved the price lower on the page and trust higher, and won (32%, then 88% cumulative). Common PDP advice keeps price visible in the buy box near the top. Treat this as 1 test for 1 brand with a price objection, not a rule. [needs brand data: price objection evidence]
- **Conflict: removing navigation.** Standard for dedicated lead-gen pages; common ecommerce advice keeps navigation on PDPs and collection pages. Cards limit it to campaign pages. [89e1eb35d667] [e95750b86b05]
- **Conflict: 25,000 to 30,000 visitor threshold.** A flat rule of thumb. Required sample size depends on baseline conversion rate and the smallest lift you want to detect; the cards say so too. [web check: sample-size calculator for the brand's baseline] [5b56dd4acd81]
- **Internal contradiction on carousels.** He says avoid carousels (low engagement), yet shows an HVAC rotating carousel (install photo plus review) as a "very creative" proof format. Card 85e78b3e4eb7 separates a deliberate proof unit from hiding a whole review section.
- **Internal contradiction on awareness wording.** He calls the laser eye pain section shorter "because the audience is more solution aware", then says the coaching page was "a lot more direct response because the audience is more aware", although the coaching audience was described as problem-aware with longer copy. Follow the stated rule: problem-aware gets longer pain copy near the top.
- **Section order is loose.** The template puts wall of love after pain; the narration also says value props come "right after the pain point area". Card 0441a8cce669 treats order as awareness-driven.
- **CTA "3 to 5% click-through" is an unusual metric.** In context it is share of page clicks in the Clarity area map, not ad CTR or add-to-cart rate. No basis given. The "41%" CTA figure is likely 0.41% (caption error).
- **FUD reducers:** card e6a8d5dff241 lists FUD reducers (guarantees, returns, security, stated price) as a fold element. The transcript's fold has headline, subheadline, 2 proof, CTA and image; FUD reduction appears only as a risk reducer under a form CTA and near add to cart. The "clearly stated price rather than contact us" point is the card's addition.
- **Missing how:** the headline prompt framework, the Figma template, the Clarity guide and the UserBrain templates are all behind his lead magnet and not in the text. No guidance on mobile speed beyond hosting (see 08-SPD-speed). No guidance on FigPii sample size, test duration or significance beyond "leave defaults". No data on progressive disclosure. No ecommerce section order.
- **AI example headlines contain invented numbers** (97% success rate, 7 days). Our rules forbid invented claims; use only claims the brand can back. [needs brand data: substantiated claims, guarantees, review counts]
- **Hyperbole:** "10 times better design, 10 times higher conversion", "I can guarantee you" (plain-text CTA, full-page refresh). Treat as opinion.
- **Commercial bias:** he sells CRO and design services and argues CRO beats buying traffic. The cards flag this. [8cf491c30c46]
- **Tools and prices will drift:** UserBrain, Elementor, Flywheel, Clarity, FigPii. [verify UI] [web check]
