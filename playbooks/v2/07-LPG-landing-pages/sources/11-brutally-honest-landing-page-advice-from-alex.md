## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator 11 page changes to raise conversion rate and AOV on ecommerce product pages and landing pages: objection-matched social proof, images that sell, an above-the-fold formula, visual hierarchy, pop-up timing, fewer choices, bundles with price anchoring and middle option bias, brand humanizing, FUD reduction, scannable copy, and an A/B testing routine. Use it to audit a PDP or landing page and to write test hypotheses. It is strong on examples and before-and-after numbers from 1 or 2 client stores. It is thin on how to run the research, how to size tests, and on mobile.

## 2. Principles

1. **Social proof works when it answers the buyer's actual objection.** The speaker calls this "the single most important and needle moving" change. Match specific testimonials to your customers' exact pain points, found through surveys and user testing. Condition: you have research on why people almost did not buy. Evidence: his gas mask client (Parcel Safety) and SolaWave examples; no isolated lift number for this change. [f85b8a87c75d]
2. **Generic proof no longer stands out.** "Slapping a bunch of testimonials on your page about places you've been featured in just doesn't cut it anymore because everyone is already doing it." Better: pull quotes from the publication that speak to a pain point (SolaWave example). Opinion from his audits. [f85b8a87c75d]
3. **Hand-pick a few relevant testimonials over showing a thin review count.** If you only have 30 reviews, he says you may not want to feature them, and instead hand-pick 3 to 4 relevant testimonials. If price is a major objection, pick testimonials about ROI or value for money. Single-presenter opinion. See section 9 for the conflict with common advice. [f85b8a87c75d]
4. **Put social proof near the critical point of action.** Next to bundles and the Add to Cart button, under "choose your bundle", or above the fold where most people never scroll past. [f85b8a87c75d]
5. **Images do the selling because most visitors skim text.** He says most users do not read a single word, but most view the images and decide on images alone. Evidence: Microsoft Clarity recordings on client sites show users scrolling past text blocks to images, with "super high engagement and click through on the images". He also cites "60,000 times faster than text" with no source [web check: origin of the 60,000x claim]. [8c97c1a87218]
6. **Images must communicate the value prop, win emotional buy-in and motivate action.** Place text with images (benefit icons and text on the image) since people look at images first. [8c97c1a87218]
7. **People buy in 3 dimensions: emotional, social, functional.** Emotional is buy-in. Social is how people perceive the product in social settings or how others will perceive them. Functional is the benefit (his examples: better sleep, less stress). The hero image should carry all 3. "The emotional part is the most important part. People ultimately buy with emotion not logic." Opinion. [8805ac6febed]
8. **Above the fold is the only section 100% of visitors see**, so it can have more impact than every other section. Even big brands (he names Gymshark) get it wrong. [8805ac6febed]
9. **Visual hierarchy guides the eye; its absence makes a page chaotic.** Signs of no hierarchy: everything capitalized, everything bold, too many colors, no clear action. Good pages segment content so information flows top to bottom. He says this is "really hard to crack" unless someone on the team is good at design. [e64076c81e2b]
10. **Pop-up timing is everything.** Immediate pop-ups create friction because the visitor does not yet know who you are. Behavior-triggered pop-ups get fewer views but convert far better because they reach people who care and are ready to act. Evidence: "conversion increases by up to 125% just by tweaking the timing" in his tests. [82c9c48592fe]
11. **Paradox of choice: too much choice equals no choice.** Cluttered navigation, too many elements and too much text overwhelm people. Removing elements can raise sales. He uses a restaurant menu analogy. [be1f562f3042]
12. **Selling a single product alone leaves money on the table.** Bundle products at a bulk discount or bundle related items to raise perceived value. He says strategic bundling and price anchoring "can single-handedly boost conversions by upwards of 90% in some cases" (no test named for the 90%). [5eabdacef800]
13. **Do not make users do math.** Spell out bundle savings clearly, in the headline, not in body text. Evidence: Wildman Drinking test (section 7). [5eabdacef800]
14. **Price anchoring: a higher reference price raises perceived value.** Raise the compare-at (original) price so the deal looks bigger while the selling price stays the same. See section 9 for legal risk. [5eabdacef800]
15. **Middle option bias: people drift to the middle option because it feels safe or balanced.** Shape the savings so the middle tier looks like the best deal. Condition: the middle tier matches real use (in his case people bought 3-packs to share with 2 friends; 5-packs were rare because people did not want to overcommit). [14136b82590a]
16. **People buy from those they know, like and trust**, so humanize the brand. He admits this is "a lot harder to track" than a pop-up trigger; no number given. [5f5d2323e7dc]
17. **FUD (fear, uncertainty, doubt) blocks purchases.** Causes: being burned before, and a "trust economy" where shoppers are careful about where they spend. Reduce it systematically and at the point of action. No number given. [8a28322b508a]
18. **Most people scan, they do not read**, unless they have been pulled into a long, engaging sales letter. Make product descriptions and every part of the product page scannable. He says bullets and icons have been A/B tested "so many times" with "huge increases"; no number given. [312f58b6a046]
19. **Landing pages are never one and done.** Keep A/B testing even when converting well (clients at 5 to 6% still test). Losing tests give insight that seeds later wins ("snowball effect"). [bc795ed5fe19]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Objection-matched social proof
1. Collect customer data with surveys and user testing [needs brand data: post-purchase survey and user test results].
2. Ask buyers 2 questions: "What stopped you from almost buying today?" and "What motivated you to buy today?"
3. List the objections and motivations. (The card adds mining support tickets and chat logs; that is the card and curator header, not the speaker.) [needs brand data: support tickets, chat logs] [f85b8a87c75d]
4. Pick 3 to 4 testimonials that each answer 1 objection. If price is a major objection, pick value-for-money testimonials.
5. If you have press mentions, use pulled quotes that address a pain point instead of bare logos.
6. Place them on key product pages near bundles and the Add to Cart button, or above the fold.
The source does not say how many survey responses are enough or how to rank objections. [f85b8a87c75d]

### 3.2 Image set that sells
1. Watch session recordings (he uses Microsoft Clarity) to see how visitors treat text versus images [needs brand data: session recordings] [verify UI].
2. Build the gallery so each image shows a selling point. From his Wildman Drinking example: happy people using the product, how it is used, different use cases, ease of use, portability, use with other people (social proof), different occasions.
3. From the SolaWave example: a person happy and smiling while using it, high-level benefits as icons and text on the image, before-and-after transformations, videos of the product in use, benefits and statistics.
4. Test: if a shopper looked only at the images, would they have enough to buy?
(Card 8c97c1a87218 adds alt text and captions; the speaker did not say this.) [8c97c1a87218]

### 3.3 Above-the-fold formula (4 parts)
1. Clear value proposition: who you are, what you do, and "what's in it for me", readable in 5 seconds or less. Lead with the tangible benefit and how it helps daily life. Before: "gas mask respirator and fire safety". After: "protection you can rely on to stay safe".
2. Supporting body text that reinforces the value prop with selling points. After example: seals to any face size, filters out toxins, fog-free visibility, comfortable.
3. A supporting visual that shows the product and carries emotional, social and functional benefit.
4. A single obvious CTA button. Blur test: blur the whole page; the button should still stand out as the clear action. Make the label benefit-oriented, not "shop now" or "buy now" (he gives no example label).
5. Solid social proof in the fold. After example: "400,000+ customers protected".
6. Remove items that compete with the CTA. In the before version the CTA "competes with all of these items". [8805ac6febed]

### 3.4 Visual hierarchy
1. Size contrast: make what matters bigger. Headline 3 to 4x body text. Body is normally 16px on desktop; headline 48 to 57px.
2. Color contrast: give the CTA a distinct color and separate it from other elements (example: pink CTA and pink announcement bar; brownish headline; grayish body text).
3. Choose the 1 or 2 elements to prioritize (in his example, the CTA and the announcement bar) and let them stand out.
4. Segment content into blocks so the eye flows top to bottom.
5. Remove all-caps, all-bold and extra colors.
6. Check mobile on its own. His before example on mobile: huge logo, huge announcement bar, nothing stands out. After: big headline, lighter body text, orange CTA, good contrast against the image. [e64076c81e2b]

### 3.5 Smart pop-up timing (3 triggers)
1. Stop firing pop-ups in the first 3 to 5 seconds of a visit.
2. Exit intent: for first-time visitors who browse but are not sold. When the mouse moves toward the close (X) button, show an offer ("get 10% or whatever percentage off your next order"), aim for newsletter or email sign-up, and retarget them later.
3. Post add to cart: fire as soon as someone adds to cart but has not checked out (they may be price-conscious or second-guessing). Offer "here's X% off to seal the deal". He says "we've tested this a ton" but gives no number.
4. Scroll depth: use Clarity to see how far people scroll and where they lose interest on product pages, category pages or the product description [needs brand data: scroll-depth heatmaps]. If 50% drop off while reading the description, fire there: "need help deciding? here's 10% off". [verify UI: pop-up tool trigger settings]
5. Expect fewer pop-up views and more conversions; judge on sales, not views. [82c9c48592fe]

### 3.6 Cut choices and clutter
1. On product pages, list the options and bundles shown. Look for ones to consolidate.
2. Watch recordings of the bundle section. Warning sign from his case: users picked only the cheapest options (1 or 2 pack) or left the site.
3. Hypothesis: too many options. Test fewer (his case: 5 to 3).
4. Count navigation items. Keep them under 7; fewer is better.
5. Use Clarity click heatmaps. Remove navigation items and page elements people do not click [needs brand data: click heatmaps] [verify UI]. [be1f562f3042]

### 3.7 Bundles and anchoring
1. Build strategic bundles. Skincare example: cleanser, moisturizer and serum as 1 package at a higher price. iRestore example: a 2-step hair regrowth kit with the saving shown.
2. Add add-on options near the bundle ("complete your hair growth kit": warranty and other options).
3. Put the saving in the bundle headline as a dollar figure ("3 packs, save $10"), not a percentage in body text.
4. Show a compare-at price next to each bundle price. His test raised compare-at prices and so the shown savings, with the selling price unchanged. Check the law first (section 9) [web check: reference price rules in each selling market].
5. For 3 tiers, set incremental savings so the middle tier looks best (3.8).
6. A/B test it. "Don't be one of those people who underestimates the amount of testing you can do with this." [5eabdacef800]

### 3.8 Middle option savings ladder
1. Check which tier people buy and why [needs brand data: bundle sales mix, order data]. In his case the 3-pack was common (bought for 2 friends), the 5-pack rare.
2. Before: 3-pack saves $10, 5-pack saves $20.
3. After: the 3-pack saves $20 more than the 1-pack, and the 5-pack saves $20 more than the 3-pack. So the step up to the middle gives the same saving as the step to the top, and buyers feel the 3-pack is a steal without overcommitting.
4. Run it as an A/B test. [14136b82590a]

### 3.9 Humanize the brand
1. Add team photos and stories: who the brand is, its mission, its story.
2. Add behind-the-scenes content: how the brand started.
3. Add customer success stories, ideally videos of people using the product, saying why they like it and how it helps their day.
4. Write conversational copy, as if talking to a friend.
No placement, format or test method is given. [5f5d2323e7dc]

### 3.10 FUD reduction
1. Walk your funnel and landing page and ask: "What would I want to know before I would buy this product?"
2. Add a money-back guarantee and highlight the return policy (what happens if someone is not happy).
3. Add security badges (data and payment are safe).
4. Add clear shipping information: what to expect and when it arrives [needs brand data: real delivery times and shipping costs].
5. Show pricing upfront. Remove hidden costs.
6. Place these at the critical point of action: typically the Add to Cart button and homepage above the fold. (The card adds checkout steps and payment entry.) [8a28322b508a]

### 3.11 Scannable content
1. Replace large text blocks with bullet points and icons.
2. Break long text into clear subheadings and subcategories so users get an overview and find what they need.
3. Bold or highlight specific elements you want to emphasize.
Keep long-form reading only where the visitor is in a long, engaging sales letter. [312f58b6a046]

### 3.12 A/B testing routine
1. List hypotheses. Prioritize with the PXL framework: highest impact, lowest effort first. (He calls it "a framework at our agency"; PXL may also be a published CXL framework [web check: PXL origin and scoring criteria].)
2. Start with the most important elements.
3. Test 1 element at a time so you know what caused the change.
4. Run each test for a full cycle, typically a minimum of 2 weeks, and until the result is statistically significant [needs brand data: traffic and baseline conversion rate to size tests].
5. Document every test, winners and losers, with what drove the result. Use it for the next test. [bc795ed5fe19]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Social proof check.** Look at reviews near Add to Cart and bundles. Good: 3 to 4 quotes, each answering a known objection (e.g. "doesn't fog", "protects eyes and lungs"). Bad: featured-in logos only, generic praise, or proof far from the buy button. Fix: 3.1. [f85b8a87c75d]
- **Image-only test.** Hide all text. Good: the images alone show benefit, use, ease, social use and results, enough to buy. Bad: plain product shots on white only. Fix: 3.2. [8c97c1a87218]
- **5-second test.** Show the hero for 5 seconds. Good: a viewer can say who you are, what you do and what they gain. Bad: a category label ("gas mask respirator and fire safety") with no benefit, no body text, no proof. Fix: 3.3. [8805ac6febed]
- **Blur test.** Blur the page. Good: 1 button clearly stands out. Bad: the CTA competes with other items. [8805ac6febed]
- **Hierarchy check.** Measure headline and body size. Good: headline 3 to 4x body (48 to 57px vs 16px desktop). Bad: headline near body size; all caps; all bold; many colors; huge logo or announcement bar on mobile. Fix: 3.4. [e64076c81e2b]
- **Pop-up timing check.** Load the site fresh. Bad: pop-up within 3 to 5 seconds. Good: exit intent, post add to cart, or scroll-depth trigger. Measure conversions and sales, not pop-up views. [82c9c48592fe]
- **Choice check.** Count bundle options and nav items. Bad: 5 bundle options with shoppers picking the cheapest or leaving; 7 or more nav items; elements with under 1% of clicks. Fix: 3.6. [be1f562f3042]
- **Savings clarity check.** Good: dollar saving in each bundle headline. Bad: percentages in small body text that make the shopper do math. [5eabdacef800]
- **Tier mix check.** Good: most buyers pick the middle tier and it matches real use. Bad: most pick the cheapest tier [needs brand data: bundle mix]. Fix: 3.8. [14136b82590a]
- **FUD check.** Ask "what would I want to know before buying?" Good: guarantee, returns, security badges, shipping time and cost, full price visible at Add to Cart and above the fold. Bad: surprise costs later. [8a28322b508a]
- **Scan check.** Good: bullets, icons, subheadings, selective bold. Bad: dense text blocks. [312f58b6a046]
- **Test hygiene check.** Good: 1 variable, 2+ weeks, significant, documented. Bad: many changes at once, short runs, no log of losers. [bc795ed5fe19]

- **Price anchoring conflicts with pricing law.** The speaker raised compare-at prices that were never charged, only to make savings look bigger. In many markets a reference price must be a real former price (for example US FTC guides on former price comparisons, EU Omnibus rule on the lowest price in the prior 30 days, and consumer protection rules in India) [web check: reference pricing law per selling market].
