## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator build the angle and first draft of a direct-response advertorial: the page that sits between the ad and the product page and teaches a new root cause. It covers picking the pain point, finding the big idea, naming the mechanism, finding "why now", building proof from studies, picking an editorial frame, writing the lead and headline, and editing out academic and AI-sounding copy. It also gives a review checklist for someone else's advertorial draft. It is strongest on angle and copy. It is thin on page layout, CTAs, offer, traffic setup and measurement.

## 2. Principles

1. **Direct response needs urgent pain.** Condition: when the goal is to get someone to buy right away, not branded or slow-cycle selling. Urgent means big, embarrassing or fear-laden, with "a lot of energy and momentum" behind it. Nice-to-haves (such as general energy) are "way" harder to sell this way. Evidence: presenter's opinion. [8a3302c0a9fb] [37c5cec3fd92]
2. **Rank pain points on 3 axes, not 1.** Urgency (emotional energy), scale (how many people you can reach with ads that keep scaling) and relevance (how directly it ties to what the product does). Condition: the product could plausibly lead with several problems. Note: card [8a3302c0a9fb] says "urgency, not market size"; the source itself weighs all 3 and ranked odor last because its market is small despite high urgency. Follow the 3-axis version. [37c5cec3fd92] [8a3302c0a9fb]
3. **Separate the intermediary from the mechanism.** The mechanism is your product-specific explanation of how it works and must be unique to your product. The intermediary is a familiar step on the road to it (detox, circulation, blood flow, lymph flow) and is deliberately not unique. Condition: when you want new pain points or new markets. Once you name the intermediary, you can ask "what problems relate to it?" and open new angles. [c258a64d3d8a] [786f882a8dc0] [de4fde69775a]
4. **The big idea sits where product, market and prospect overlap.** Product: what is unique, or a unique combination, or a common ingredient renamed, or the origin story. Market: live debates, leading experts, where beliefs are now, competing products to position against. Prospect: which segment you write to. If one of the 3 is missing, the angle is generic or ungrounded. The source calls it "a decently complicated problem that requires some intuition" and says it may need multiple mechanisms for different segments. [a7a83f2d6fb7] [5737588f8a37]
5. **Give every product component a reason.** Condition: most products were built around cost, convenience or "it seemed cool", not true innovation. Force yourself to argue why each part is exactly as it is (why this ingredient, this source, this form). Make the strongest case while "trying to be as truthful as possible". Do not invent features. [a7a83f2d6fb7]
6. **Pitch the insight just ahead of the market, not far ahead.** Too esoteric and the audience rejects it; too familiar and it bores them. Aim for "an insight or an epiphany for an emerging idea". [a7a83f2d6fb7] [9d0386d60cd1]
7. **Ride trends in the early-majority window.** Use the adoption curve (innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards). The source placed microplastics and seed oils around the early majority in 2025: "tons of momentum, tons of proof". Condition: the product can credibly connect to the trend. Marketers see trends early, so check real public awareness first. Evidence: the presenter's judgment, plus keto and carnivore as past examples of ideas that went from "crazy" to accepted. [1e01d636796d] [9d0386d60cd1] [web check: current awareness stage of any trend before building on it]
8. **Answer "why now".** From "The 16-Word Sales Letter" (the presenter recalls "10 questions"): what is happening in the world right now that makes this problem or solution relevant today. The source's pattern: something rose (more toxins, microplastics, seed oils) and something fell (less green, chlorophyll-dense food), so people need the fix "more than ever". A trend-based cause also shifts blame off the reader ("not your fault"), which lowers defensiveness. [e7f60c4824a7] [9d0386d60cd1]
9. **Write for a distracted, concrete thinker.** Readers are not studying for a test; they are scrolling for fun, news or opinions and do not want to think hard. Most are "concrete visual thinkers", not abstract or math thinkers. The presenter's blunt reasoning: average IQ is 100, so half the audience is below it. Condition: consumer audiences; the card notes expert or B2B audiences may want more detail. Simplify without cutting intensity. [1a914d4178f2]
10. **Use the reader's existing mental sketch.** People hold rough "sketches" of how things work, not accurate models (liver = filter). Build the metaphor on that sketch. Every extra layer of abstraction (liver = factory) loses "selling power", immediacy and visceral feel. Use abstract metaphors only as secondary support. [66470c34199d] [8930f6aa03e2]
11. **Metaphors must match the mechanism's actual action.** A filter clogs and needs flushing or unclogging; a mop polishes a floor. "Molecular mop" for a clogged filter makes the reader reconcile the mismatch. Each mismatch is a "paper cut" of cognitive load; enough of them and the reader ignores you. The presenter admits "a mop could work" and he is "100% open to be wrong"; this is a strong preference, not a tested law. [66470c34199d] [8930f6aa03e2]
12. **Make the causal chain from mechanism to symptom crystal clear.** If the angle is clogged lymph, show how clogged lymph makes the reader feel bloated or puffy. Use proof plus a "mechanical visual metaphor". Then argue why this product form is "the best and only solution" and why past fixes failed. Condition: the argument must stay truthful and defensible. [45720eb59c92]
13. **Fewer steps beat more accurate steps.** A mechanism with too many links (toxins, shifted microbiome, bad bacteria, gas) confuses people even if it is closer to right. Prefer a mechanism that already feels intuitive (lymph, detox), since it needs less proof. [45720eb59c92] [66470c34199d]
14. **An advertorial needs an editorial identity.** It comes from "editorial" and must read as a piece of news content with a known theme (warning, breaking news, discovery). Without it, it is just a sales letter or VSL. The presenter calls missing identity "probably the number one issue" he sees in advertorials. Commit to 1 frame for the whole piece. [44bf08d46ea9] [5c8995960ecb]
15. **Do not lead with pain.** 2 reasons: AI and most writers default to it, so readers tune it out; and pain points are "for the street", the same for every product in the category. Lead with what only you have: curiosity about your mechanism, and proof. Pain can come later, once the reader is warm. Condition: the presenter says "you can lead with pain but it needs to be sticky". This is his strong default for direct response, not a proven rule. [046994a8d7d8] [65c1d8805a3b]
16. **Earn permission before naming pain.** Asserting "you diet every day" feels like a stranger telling you you're fat. Hedge with "maybe" and use softeners: a first-person story ("I don't know if you can relate, but I was...") or third person ("the patients I talk to, so many of them feel like this"). Readers it does not fit then do not feel accused. [046994a8d7d8] [2475094b3f6b]
17. **The best leads are a "symphony" of copy blocks.** Weave pain, curiosity, promise, proof, constraints and benefits together instead of stacking 1 block type. Otherwise there is no incentive or promise to keep reading. Condition: the presenter frames this as VSL lead practice that is "true for advertorials as well". [046994a8d7d8] [65c1d8805a3b]
18. **Headlines carry 1 curiosity element.** 2 at most, usually as problem in the headline and solution in the subhead. Each added variable costs comprehension. [9bd853e225c5] [046994a8d7d8]
19. **Headlines must be universal.** Drop research details that narrow the audience ("after your shift") if they add no selling power. [9bd853e225c5]
20. **Pass the 1-second "mom test".** A reader should know what the piece is about at once. [9bd853e225c5]
21. **Keep reading level near grade 5.** Advertorials may run slightly higher because they mimic editorials, but still aim for about 5. Simplify with Germanic-rooted, concrete, "earthy" words and simpler sentences, not by cutting ideas or intensity. Evidence: presenter's preference; he measured Israel's draft at grade 7 (he first said 8) and still called it too high. [d36b6dd3162b]
22. **Rhythm and flow beat grammar, but default to full sentences.** He does not care about grammar rules in copy; he cares about flow. Too many fragments kill flow. 1 fragment beat as an effect is fine; repeated fragments are the fault. [eb01995ac888] [2475094b3f6b]
23. **Subheadlines tell the story on their own.** Each subhead must be a coherent copy block alone, and the stack must read as 1 consistent story that pulls readers back into the body. Missing subheads are a named fault. [9bd853e225c5] [48b68b595955]
24. **The characterization deserves the most wording time.** A characterization is the catchy, specific name for your mechanism or product. The presenter says most wording "doesn't matter that much", but this 1 name "can do so much work". [10605f2c40fb]
25. **Proof must feel big, clear and credible.** Borrow credibility from the study's authors, journal and institution; check study design; turn raw numbers into plain percentages or multiples. Condition: keep it faithful to the data (see Caveats: the source's own wording on this is risky). [ae370217e964] [7b87cbeac602]
26. **Test a portfolio of awareness levels.** Unaware angles often fail but can open the market "really really wide" if cracked. Immediate pain angles are more reliable. Split tests across levels. [85a5623c70bf] [de4fde69775a] [1bc5a164d457]

## 3. Procedures

The source gives a working process, told in order, while reviewing 1 draft and rebuilding it. Steps below follow that order. Where a card adds a step the source did not say, it is marked.

### 3.1 Research inputs to gather first
The viewer's research, which the presenter called "all pretty good", had these sections. Use them as the research brief. [a7a83f2d6fb7] [needs brand data: reviews, testimonials, customer verbatims, personas]
1. Core problem.
2. Target audience and personas.
3. Proof elements.
4. Messaging and tone.
5. Market verbatims: fears (bloating, chronic digestive issues, detox), beliefs.
6. Psychographic mapping.
7. Skim the product's testimonials for the most repeated benefits. For Juna these were bloat, energy and gut.
8. Note where the brand's current ads sit in the funnel. Juna ran "only bottom-of-funnel", product-aware ads, which left angles unexplored. [needs brand data: current ad mix by awareness level]

### 3.2 Rank the pain points
1. List every problem the product's reviews, testimonials and research genuinely point to. Juna: bloating and weight loss (treated as 1), gut and digestion, energy, internal odor (breath). [37c5cec3fd92]
2. Score each on urgency, scale and relevance. [37c5cec3fd92] [8a3302c0a9fb]
3. Rank. Juna example: bloating/weight loss first (vanity-driven but high energy, huge market, direct link), gut second (similar, good energy), energy third (abstract, "nice to have"), odor last (strong urgency, much smaller market). [37c5cec3fd92]
4. Lead with the top pain. Fold small high-urgency pains in as secondary benefits. Small dedicated funnels for a niche pain are possible but not the default. [37c5cec3fd92] [a95896b77075]
5. Check that no big idea you later pick serves only a low-ranked pain. The source rejected a "you're not tired from lack of sleep" idea because it only served the 3rd-ranked segment. [37c5cec3fd92] [a7a83f2d6fb7]

### 3.3 Find the intermediary and expand pain points
1. Name the familiar concept your product works through. Juna: detox. Other candidates the source listed: circulation, blood flow, lymph. [c258a64d3d8a]
2. Ask what urgent, relevant, scalable problems relate to that intermediary. Detox produced: fatty liver, blood sugar, heart and cardio problems, inflammation, hormone disruption (menopause, testosterone). [c258a64d3d8a] [de4fde69775a]
3. Rank those the same way (3.2). [c258a64d3d8a]
4. Use the immediate pains (bloating, heaviness, weight, digestion, gut) for reliable everyday funnels. Use the broad swings (fatty liver, heart) as expansion bets. [de4fde69775a] [c258a64d3d8a]
5. For every derived pain, write the causal bridge back to the mechanism, or the copy reads as a non sequitur. [c258a64d3d8a] [786f882a8dc0]
6. Compliance check before writing any disease-adjacent angle (see section 9). [786f882a8dc0]

### 3.4 Build the big idea
1. Stack the product's unique parts. Juna: chlorophyll, specifically sodium copper chlorophyllin, from white mulberry leaf, in a liquid dropper, with vegetable glycerin, purified water, organic lemon and mint essential oils. [a7a83f2d6fb7] [needs brand data: full ingredient list, sourcing, form, origin story]
2. If nothing is unique, look for a unique combination, rename a common ingredient, or use the backstory, origin or creator. [a7a83f2d6fb7]
3. For each part, write the strongest truthful reason it has to be that way ("if it's a capsule it doesn't work as well"). [a7a83f2d6fb7] [45720eb59c92]
4. Scan the market: the live debate, the leading experts, where the majority's beliefs sit, and competing products to position against. [a7a83f2d6fb7] [web check: current category debate and competitor claims]
5. Pick the prospect segment (from 3.2 and 3.3). [a7a83f2d6fb7]
6. Write candidate ideas. Test each against all 3 parts (product, market, prospect). [a7a83f2d6fb7] [5737588f8a37]
7. Card addition, not in the source: test whether the 1-line angle could apply to a competitor; if yes, it is not unique enough. [5737588f8a37]

### 3.5 Research and picture the mechanism
1. Ask an AI tool how the hero ingredient actually works, then ask for the visual of how it works. [e30258576d36]
2. Look for a physical shape or action you can show. Juna example: chlorophyll(in) is flat and net-like, sticks to flat toxins "like one of these sticky hands"; the presenter would make ads with sticky-hand toys. [e30258576d36]
3. Look for a story in the ingredient's nature. Example: plants cannot run from predators or toxins, so they evolved defenses; chlorophyll called "life's original mediator" between sunlight, toxins and survival. [e30258576d36]
4. Build the "why now" layer: what rose (toxins, microplastics, seed oils), what fell (dietary green density). [e7f60c4824a7]
5. Build an "inside vector" reframe if the category is crowded. Name the usual target (liver), call it the wrong fix ("security guards inside the bank while the front door is wide open"), move the fight to the real entry point (gut wall), and show the product working there (a net across the border; toxins stick and get "escorted out"). [43788d1e878e]
6. Try a second picture. Example: "pancake stack": flat chlorophyll meets a flat toxin, they stick, and the pair becomes "too big to absorb". The presenter says this still has complexity he would remove. [e30258576d36] [43788d1e878e]
7. Justify the format. Juna: a dropper because it is taken with food and coats the food, while a capsule takes "35 to 40 minutes to digest", by which time "toxins are already in your system". He adds "it doesn't really matter how strong it is"; it only needs to answer the "why this form" question. [45720eb59c92] [web check: capsule dissolution time claim]
8. Research the ingredient's history for curiosity hooks. Mulberry: use in Japan, World War II trenches, silk and silkworms. [a9c5386954e0] [web check: every historical claim]
9. Verify every mechanism claim before publishing; the AI output is idea generation, not fact. [e30258576d36]

### 3.6 Name the mechanism (characterization)
1. Generate names from real attributes: form (dropper, liquid, tea, morning tea, syrup, tonic, elixir), ingredient chemistry (glycerin is a sugar, so "green sugar drops"; "two drops of this"), source (mulberry, berry, silk), visual (ring molecule, plant blood). [10605f2c40fb]
2. Drop overused names. The presenter has used and seen "plant blood" before and avoids it. [10605f2c40fb]
3. Shortlist. He settled on "silk syrup", "mulberry drops" or "silk nectar" because silk feels luxurious. [10605f2c40fb]
4. Use the chosen name the same way in headline, subheads and body. [10605f2c40fb]
5. Do not name the product as a form it is not in a way that misleads (calling a dropper a "tea"; the presenter says "even though it's not"). [10605f2c40fb]

### 3.7 Build "massive proof"
The presenter says massive proof is "a whole video and a whole training"; this is the short version.
1. Find studies that show the mechanism works, and that it works for each target symptom. [ae370217e964] [web check: each study, its population, dose and result]
2. Rate each study's credibility on 3 points: the authors (where they trained), the journal (his example: The Lancet as "the most prestigious medical journal in the world"), and the publishing institution. [ae370217e964] [7b87cbeac602]
3. Check design: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled. He asks ChatGPT for this. [ae370217e964] [web check: read the paper itself, not an AI summary]
4. Translate raw numbers into plain proportions: percentages, "5 times", or "for every 10 toxins, cut down to about 4 or 5". His example: "dropped from 0.45 to 0.24" becomes "slashed by nearly 50%". Do the arithmetic in code: 0.45 to 0.24 is a 46.7% drop, so "nearly half" is fair. [7b87cbeac602] [ae370217e964]
5. Add time where the study supports it ("in just a couple of weeks"). [7b87cbeac602]
6. Pair each translated claim with its source name so the reader senses it is real. [7b87cbeac602]
7. Put the proof near the top so readers register its weight early. [ae370217e964] [48b68b595955]

### 3.8 Pick the frame, pain point and assets, then draft
1. Choose 1 editorial frame. Full list the source gives (he says "10 core types", and there may be others): breaking news and discovery; authority or expert breaking down an issue; David vs Goliath; warning or fear; scandal or whistleblower; social proof ("why everyone is going crazy about this"); origin discovery; comparison ("I tested this, I tried that"); local or relatable ("Ohio mom discovers..."); scarcity or exclusivity ("why this is about to sell out"). [44bf08d46ea9] [2920ab7bf3d2]
2. Match frame to angle. The source's shortlist for Juna: warning/fear for microplastics and seed oils; breaking news around chlorophyllin or fatty liver; whistleblower for a conspiracy angle; origin discovery for World War II, Japan, silk. For a new solution, the whole piece is a discovery promo; for a warning, the whole piece is a warning promo. [2920ab7bf3d2] [44bf08d46ea9]
3. Pick 1 lead pain point. He chose fatty liver as a big, urgent market, tied to microplastics. [a95896b77075]
4. Gather the pieces: the proof stat (his 55% figure), the characterization, the study, the insights and metaphors. [a95896b77075] [ae370217e964]
5. Draft the whole piece. He "one-shotted" it with his bots, then built the page in Lovable with images from Nano Banana. [verify UI: Lovable and image tools] See 3.10 for the draft's structure.
6. Run the review (section 5) and the tightening pass (3.9).

### 3.9 Rewrite and tighten with AI (the source's 3 prompts)
The presenter shows rough prompts and says they are messy but work. Paraphrased faithfully:
1. **Flow and anti-AI rewrite.** "Rewrite this, same idea and structure and similar length, but better. Focus on copy block density without making it much longer. Use natural language that flows. Do not use staccato sentences like this [example]. Focus on natural conversational flow and musicality. Always use full grammatical sentences." [eb01995ac888] [2475094b3f6b]
2. **Simple language.** "Simplify the reading level. This does not mean shorter. Use more base Germanic-rooted words instead of high-flown Latin ones. Swap vocabulary for concrete, earthy words; sometimes shorten or simplify sentence structure. Do not make it dumber or cut intensity. Aim for grade 3 to 5; it is at grade 8 now." [d36b6dd3162b]
3. **Subheadlines.** "Make all the subheadlines stronger. Give 3 to 5 options for each. Use copy blocks. Each must be a coherent headline on its own, and together they must tell a story when stacked." Expect about 1 in 5 to be good. [9bd853e225c5]
4. Keep a saved file of anti-AI instructions and reuse it across drafts. [eb01995ac888]
5. Re-read after every AI pass; staccato text comes back "no matter what", even with his tuned bots. [eb01995ac888]
6. After the first draft, strengthen urgency in the headline (he wanted more microplastic framing and fear), simplify subheads that are clever but unclear, add earlier CTAs, and add comparison blocks and short videos where they sharpen proof. [9beeec9cb1de]

### 3.10 Section order of the presenter's rebuilt advertorial (example)

6. Reveal: "Your liver isn't the problem. Your gut wall is letting through a credit card's worth of plastic every week."
7. Analogy: body as a house; liver is security inside, gut wall is the front door.
8. Timeline contrast: for "99.9% of human evolution" we did not have microplastics in every meal; the liver evolved for the occasional bad mushroom.
9. Simple flow diagram: toxins reach the liver, the liver wraps them in fat, fat reduces function, less detox, it gets worse.
10. Category-enemy subhead: "Why the $8 billion liver detox industry is mopping the floor while the faucet runs full blast." He calls this subhead "kind of dumb" and would fix it.
11. Proof block with the 55% reduction.
12. Comparison: capsules vs liquid.
13. Short videos.
14. CTA. He would add earlier CTAs.

### 3.11 Awareness portfolio for tests
1. Map the 5 awareness levels by starting point and goal. Unaware: notices symptoms but does not see them as a problem, or does not know the hidden root; goal is to make them believe they have a specific problem with a different cause. Problem-aware: wants to solve a known problem; goal is to make them believe your mechanism (for Juna: overloaded liver, toxins, detox chlorophyll) is the cause and best fix. Solution-, product- and most-aware are named but not worked through. [213677507084] [85a5623c70bf]
2. Allocate tests, for example about 30% unaware (broad pains or curiosity), about 30% problem-aware (bloating, heaviness, weight, digestion, gut), and the rest across solution-, product- and most-aware. He says "you can adjust these". [85a5623c70bf] [needs brand data: current spend and results by awareness level]
3. Card additions, not in the source: rebalance toward the level that wins, let data decide rather than preference, and change few variables between variants so you learn what drove the result. [85a5623c70bf] [1bc5a164d457]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Leading with generic pain, which AI and most writers default to and readers tune out. [046994a8d7d8]
2. Naming the reader's pain without permission, or assuming habits they may not have. [046994a8d7d8] [2475094b3f6b]
3. No editorial identity: an advertorial that reads like a sales letter or VSL. [44bf08d46ea9]
4. A frame in name only: a "Warning" that warns about nothing specific. [44bf08d46ea9]
5. Bolting on copy blocks that do not serve the frame. [44bf08d46ea9]
6. Too many variables in the headline. [9bd853e225c5]
7. Research details that shrink the audience ("after your shift"). [9bd853e225c5]
8. Missing proof in the opening. [48b68b595955]
9. No subheadlines. [9bd853e225c5]
10. Academic copywriting: correct technique, no voice. [d36b6dd3162b]
11. Post-graduate vocabulary; the presenter calls it "unacceptable" and "one of the most egregious" and easiest faults to fix. [d36b6dd3162b]
12. Staccato, "film noir" AI fragments with no rhythm. [eb01995ac888]
13. Sloppy metaphors that are "in the right direction" but mismatch the mechanism. [66470c34199d]
14. Abstract math or timelines the reader must picture. [1a914d4178f2]
15. Insights that are clever but "don't matter" to how the reader feels. [a7a83f2d6fb7]
16. Mechanisms with too many steps. [45720eb59c92]
17. Big ideas that serve only a small segment. [37c5cec3fd92]
18. Being too far ahead of the market with "weird complicated esoteric stuff", or too boring. [a7a83f2d6fb7]
19. Assuming your early view of a trend matches the public's. [9d0386d60cd1]
20. Reusing overused characterizations ("plant blood"). [10605f2c40fb]
21. Reporting proof as dry numbers ("improved inflammation markers") with no story. [7b87cbeac602]
22. Only 1 CTA at the end of a long page. [9beeec9cb1de]
23. Trusting 1 draft; the presenter treats every angle as unproven. [1bc5a164d457]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Legal, ad policy and consumer law risks (flag before any use)**
- **Disease claims for a supplement.** The expansion angles (fatty liver, blood sugar, heart disease, inflammation, hormone disruption, menopause, testosterone) are disease or condition claims. For a supplement these can breach food, drug and advertising rules. [web check: rules in the brand's market, for example FTC and FDA in the US, and ASCI, FSSAI and the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act in India] [786f882a8dc0] [c258a64d3d8a]
- **"Only solution" and "nothing else works".** The source says to "pretend it's the best and only solution and make an argument". That is an absolute claim needing substantiation. Treat it as "make the strongest truthful case" only. [45720eb59c92]
- **Proof framing.** The source says to "manipulate the proof in the results to be as strong as possible" and to call a study author "Harvard trained" when the study came from another university. Both can mislead. Its own example is also off: it calls 0.42 to 0.25 "slashed in half", but that is a 40.5% drop. Rule for ecomOS: compute every figure in code, keep the comparison group and time frame, name the real institution, do not upgrade a small or animal study into a human result. [7b87cbeac602] [ae370217e964]
- **Unverified draft facts.** "Johns Hopkins study", "55%", "60% damage before symptoms", "credit card's worth of plastic every week", "first time" microplastics in liver tissue, "99.9% of human evolution", "$8 billion detox industry", "86,000 man-made chemicals", capsule "35 to 40 minutes", chlorophyll "bio-identical to blood except for one little molecule", "most powerful molecule in existence". All are unsourced in the video. [web check: each before any reuse] [720bfa12f751]
- **Expert persona.** The "17 years treating liver disease" specialist is a demo voice with no named person. Using an invented expert, or implying unaffiliated news, is deceptive. Use only a real, consenting, verifiable expert, and label the page as an ad where rules require it. [720bfa12f751] [5c8995960ecb] [web check: advertorial disclosure rules per platform and market]
- **Meta personal attributes and health.** Lines like "you're fat" or "your liver is clogged" address the reader's health directly. Meta restricts ad text that asserts or implies personal attributes, including health and weight. [web check: current Meta advertising standards on personal attributes, health and weight loss] The "maybe" softener helps tone but may not make an ad compliant. [046994a8d7d8]
- **Fear angles.** Warning/fear and whistleblower frames raise the risk of misleading claims; the cards flag this. [2920ab7bf3d2] [9beeec9cb1de]
- **Characterization.** Calling a dropper a "tea" or "syrup" when it is not may mislead. [10605f2c40fb]
- **Historical hooks** (World War II, Japan) are story, not efficacy proof; keep them apart from proof claims. [a9c5386954e0]

**Card drift from the source**
- [8a3302c0a9fb] says rank by urgency "not by how many people have it". The source weighs urgency, scale and relevance together. Use [37c5cec3fd92].
- [65c1d8805a3b] defines "constraints" as scarcity or "why this isn't for everyone". In the source, constraints are the obstacles and other solutions the reader wants to avoid ("even if you don't want to do these constraints", "why fiber, detox teas and water won't fix it"). Use the source meaning.
- [5c8995960ecb] adds "dateline, reporting voice" and "delay the pitch"; the source does not say these.
- [2920ab7bf3d2] says "four archetypes"; those are only the 4 the presenter shortlisted for Juna. The full list is 10 [44bf08d46ea9].
- [1bc5a164d457] and [85a5623c70bf] add "let data decide", rebalancing, and "change few variables". Reasonable, but not in the source.
- [d36b6dd3162b] says grade 8 is "the outer limit"; the source only says grade 7 to 8 is too high and aims for about 5.

**Conflicts with common ecommerce and copy advice**
- Pain-first frameworks (such as problem, agitate, solve) are common. This source says do not lead with pain in direct response. Keep both; test.
- Many guides aim for grade 6 to 8. This source aims for grade 5 even on editorial-style pages.
- The source's "readers are kind of stupid" framing conflicts with the common "your customer is not stupid, she is your wife" advice, which he quotes and rejects. His practical output (simple, concrete) matches common advice; only the framing differs.
- ecomOS writing rules ban invented claims. The source's proof and "only solution" tactics must be cut back to what is true and substantiated.

**What is missing**
- Page layout, above-the-fold design, mobile, speed, images, social proof placement, guarantee, offer and pricing: not covered.
- CTAs: only "add earlier CTAs". No wording, count, button design or link destination.
- Traffic matching: only the placement (ad, then advertorial, then product page) and the awareness levels. No guidance on matching the ad's hook to the advertorial headline, on which campaigns send traffic, or on bridge-page vs direct-to-PDP tests. [needs brand data: which ads send to which pages]
- Measurement: no metrics, no benchmarks, no test design, no stop rules. The presenter does not know whether any of his work converted.
- Solution-, product- and most-aware levels are named but not worked through.
- "Massive proof" is admitted to be a separate full training; only the outline is here.
