## 1. What this source is for

Build or audit a single landing page that turns paid or organic traffic into a lead, a booked call or a sale. It gives a 4-step build order: (1) the above the fold section, (2) benefit-driven sections, (3) a low-friction conversion event, (4) social proof and trust sections placed through the page. It is strongest on hero copy, hero proof and form friction. It is written for B2B and lead-gen, so every ecommerce use is a translation the operator must check.

## 2. Principles

1. **Raise conversion before buying more traffic.** When you already pay for traffic, doubling conversion also raises the return on every visit you buy after that. The presenter says he "will take doubling your conversions any day of the week". Condition: this is his preference. It holds when traffic exists and the page is the bottleneck. It does not hold when traffic is the real constraint or traffic quality is poor. Evidence: argument only, plus the Influence Hunter case (40+ booked calls per week, $3M revenue from 1 page). [ff47a06fdb3a]

2. **The top section decides most of the result.** 100% of visitors see it. The presenter says 60% "statistically" never scroll past it, and people form an opinion of a site in 50 milliseconds. So spend about 80% of your page effort on the above the fold section. Condition: applies to any entry page. The 60%, 50 ms and 80% figures are his heuristics with no source. [706a0be05ca5] [web check: current scroll depth research; the 60% figure is unsourced]

3. **The hero has 5 pillars.** In his order: (a) a clear value proposition and benefit-led headline, (b) social proof above the fold, (c) clear visual hierarchy, (d) FUD reduction at the point of action, (e) an image or video that reinforces the value proposition. [706a0be05ca5]

4. **In 5 seconds or less, the visitor must know who you are, what you do and what is in it for them.** The third part is the one he says "99% of brands" get wrong. Body text under the headline reinforces the core value proposition. Test: put yourself in the buyer's shoes. Would this headline and body text really move someone to buy or reach out? If not, rewrite. [706a0be05ca5]

5. **Lead the headline with the derived benefit, not the product name.** Use the "so that" chain to find it. Example from his client: the product is an "email deliverability and warm-up tool", the headline became "Land emails in the inbox effortlessly so that you can close more deals". The chain continues: so you can generate more revenue, so you can scale your business. Condition: the source does not say where to stop the chain. The card adds: stop at a concrete outcome the buyer cares about, not a vague slogan. [05cef19e3b81]

6. **The headline must match the ad, search result or link that earned the click.** Whatever offer or promise was made there must also appear in the headline and body text. Applies to Facebook ads, Google ads and organic SEO traffic. [706a0be05ca5]

7. **Pick the headline frame by awareness level.** He quotes a CXL table that maps awareness level and value proposition type to headline templates. Low awareness, desire-led: "Achieve [specific desire] with the only / largest / most [product type] that does [valuable thing]". Low awareness, pain-led: "Eliminate [problem] with the only / largest / most [product type] that [valuable unique thing]". Only these 2 rows are read out. He uses the table as an idea generator, not a rule. [5014418361eb] [web check: the full CXL value proposition table, since only 2 rows are given here]

8. **Put proof at the very top, and stack it.** People do not scroll out of curiosity; you give them a reason to scroll, and trust at the top is one. "Stacking" means using 2 or more different kinds of proof above the fold, for example review badge plus user count plus client logos plus quantified results. Condition: the proof must be relevant to the buyer and impactful. Thin proof should not be featured as a count. [7f28a425cebc]

9. **Choose proof metrics your buyer cares about.** For a sales audience he showed average email open rate, average response rate and spam rate, plus "trusted by" logos. [7f28a425cebc] [needs brand data: which proof metrics matter to this brand's buyers]

10. **Visual hierarchy guides the eye to the CTA.** Vary font size, font weight and colour together so the reader goes headline, then subhead, then body, then button. The main CTA must stand out by colour, weight and size. Blur test: if you blur the page, the main action should still be clear. He calls hierarchy an advanced technique that people "underestimate". [4e7bcbfc5e4b]

11. **Reduce FUD (fear, uncertainty, doubt) at the critical point of action.** Points of action he names: the add to cart button for ecommerce, the book a demo CTA, and the hero section. FUD reducers he names: money-back guarantee, free trial, return policy, no credit card required, GDPR compliant. The "Look for" note in the source places them directly under the CTA button. Combining FUD reduction with social proof in the hero is what he calls "the winning combination". [0d11fac972bd]

12. **The hero visual must reinforce the copy.** It should show who you are and what you do, tell a story and connect emotionally, "especially for e-commerce". He quotes "the human brain processes images 60,000 times faster than text" and "90% of information transmitted to the brain is visual". Both numbers are unsourced. [ecf232bf372c] [web check: both visual statistics; do not repeat them on client pages without a source]

13. **Give benefits 4 to 5 or more whole sections.** Each section explains the value proposition and what makes you different, and answers "what is in it for me". Length is fine as long as every section is valuable. Condition: his own observation from pages he says convert "well above industry average"; no number is given for that average. [145380876d79]

14. **Separate benefits from features, and quantify when you can.** Examples: "Campaigns not performing? Generate more leads with peace of mind." and "Better open rates, response rates and booked meetings in 14 days". [145380876d79]

15. **Contrast principle: lead with the pain, then the solution.** Use a pain the audience says they dislike. Examples: "We reach out to thousands of influencers so you don't have to" (the client said their buyers hate mass outreach). "Denied again? We can help." (mortgage buyers often get denied by banks). [e5afd40ade58] [needs brand data: pains customers repeat in reviews, tickets and surveys]

16. **Write to "you".** Every time you write copy, focus on the reader and what they care about. Example: "We grow your e-commerce brand like it's our own. We act as an extension of your team to help you scale your Amazon business. We only win if you do." Note that the example still uses "we" for what the brand does for the reader. [4b1eaf817c12]

17. **Let people convert at any point on the page.** Interest can peak in any section. Put CTAs through the whole page, not only at the top. Capture interest "while you have it". [f7356787e0a5]

18. **Make the conversion event low friction.** Include only relevant information. Fewer fields, clear labels, a reason to submit and clear next steps. [f391cf14459a]

19. **Balance lead quality against lead quantity.** Aim for 4 form fields or fewer, but add friction when a traffic source brings many unqualified leads (his example: Facebook ads) and when your team has limited capacity. [f391cf14459a] [needs brand data: lead quality by source, sales capacity]

20. **Progressive disclosure.** Make the first step simple, then reveal more fields after the user starts. Once people finish step 1, they feel they "might as well" finish. Analogy: Google Maps shows more detail as you zoom in. He calls it an advanced technique; no test result is given. [78edd0e14e2f]

21. **Scatter hand-picked proof through the page.** Put proof and objection-handling copy at points where people drop off. Match each testimonial to the pain point, motivation or outcome of that section. On his own services page every selling point has a testimonial under it. His rule: people care about what customers say, not what you say. [bece1235ee24]

22. **Collect proof on purpose.** Generic reviews are unfocused. Ask customers leading questions so the answers map to the page's claims. Prefer video testimonials. [04866413d08b]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Build order (the 4 steps)

1. Step 1, above the fold. Build the 5 pillars in section 3.2. Spend about 80% of total page effort here. [706a0be05ca5]
2. Step 2, benefit-driven sections. Write 4 to 5 or more sections, each led by a benefit (section 3.4). [145380876d79]
3. Step 3, low-friction conversion event. Repeat CTAs through the page, simplify the form, and consider progressive disclosure or a same-page form (sections 3.5 to 3.7). [f7356787e0a5] [f391cf14459a]
4. Step 4, social proof and trust sections through the page. Place targeted proof at drop-off points and under each claim (section 3.8). [bece1235ee24]

### 3.2 Build the above the fold section

1. Write the plain product or feature line (for example "email deliverability and warm-up tool").
2. Run the "so that" chain (section 3.3) to find the derived benefit.
3. Classify the traffic by awareness level and pick a headline frame from the CXL table (principle 7). [5014418361eb] [needs brand data: traffic sources and their awareness level]
4. Write a headline that leads with the benefit and matches the promise in the ad or search result. [706a0be05ca5] [needs brand data: live ad copy and offers pointing at this page]
5. Write body text that reinforces the value proposition. Add more benefits, and quantify the offer where true (his example: results within 14 days of signing up).
6. Write the CTA button in terms of the outcome. His example: "Improve your deliverability".
7. Stack 2 or more proof types above the fold: rating badge and review count, user or customer count, client or press logos with what the press said, quantified results. [7f28a425cebc] [needs brand data: review count, rating, customer count, press quotes, results]
8. Add FUD reducers directly under the CTA (guarantee, free trial, returns policy, no card required, compliance). Only use ones the brand actually offers. [0d11fac972bd] [needs brand data: real guarantee, returns and shipping policy]
9. Add a hero image or video that shows what you do or the result. Prefer real output over decoration. [ecf232bf372c]
10. Set hierarchy: biggest and boldest headline, lighter and smaller body, high-contrast CTA. [4e7bcbfc5e4b]
11. Run the 5-second check and the blur check (section 5).

### 3.3 "So that" chain for headlines

1. State the feature or product.
2. Ask "so that...?" and write the next outcome.
3. Repeat until you reach an outcome the buyer cares about, such as more revenue or time saved.
4. Lead the headline with that outcome. Keep the feature in the body text.
The source gives 1 worked chain: land emails in the inbox, so that you can close more deals, so that you can generate more revenue, so that you can scale and grow your business. The source does not say which level to pick. [05cef19e3b81]

### 3.4 Write benefit-driven sections

1. List every feature. Translate each into a reader benefit.
2. Give each major benefit its own section. Target 4 to 5 or more sections.
3. Lead each section headline with the benefit, or with pain then solution (contrast principle).
4. Quantify where you can (numbers, time frames).
5. Write in "you" language.
6. Cut any section that does not answer "what is in it for me". [145380876d79] [e5afd40ade58] [4b1eaf817c12]

### 3.5 Repeat CTAs

1. Keep the hero CTA.
2. Add a CTA after each benefit or proof section so a reader can act when a section lands.
3. Check session recordings and heatmaps to confirm people click the mid-page buttons. In his Influence Hunter case, recordings in Microsoft Clarity showed clicks on the "Learn more" buttons all down the page. [f7356787e0a5] [verify UI]

### 3.6 Fix the contact or lead form

1. Merge First name and Last name into Full name.
2. Keep the label separate from the placeholder, so the label stays visible after typing. Reason: on mobile, a user who is interrupted may forget what the field asked.
3. Replace a redundant heading like "Contact us" (or a vague one like "Always here for you") with a benefit-led headline on what the user gets by submitting (grow pipeline, grow revenue, save time, save money).
4. Add 3 to 4 bullet points on what to expect after submitting.
5. Add social proof at the point of form entry.
6. Add a short blurb on next steps.
7. Aim for 4 fields or fewer. Add fields back only if lead quality from the traffic source or team capacity calls for it. [f391cf14459a]

### 3.7 Progressive disclosure and same-page form

Progressive disclosure:
1. Show 1 field first (Segment's example: work email next to the demo CTA).
2. After the user types in it, reveal the rest. In Segment's case the form went from 1 field to 3.
3. Keep revealed fields relevant. [78edd0e14e2f] [verify UI: how the form tool supports conditional fields]

Same-page form (from 1 A/B test):
1. If the hero CTA sends users to a separate contact or opt-in page, change it to scroll down to a form on the same page.
2. A/B test the scroll version against the link-away version.
3. Watch recordings. In his test, people read the value proposition, image and stats, clicked "Learn more" within the first 6 to 7 seconds, reached the form and filled it in straight away. [45c2e59e672b]

### 3.8 Place and collect social proof

1. At the top, stack several proof types (section 3.2, step 7).
2. Through the page, add proof at points where people drop off, to give them a reason to keep scrolling. [bece1235ee24] [needs brand data: scroll depth and drop-off points from recordings or heatmaps]
3. Under each selling point, place a testimonial that backs that specific claim.
4. Pick each piece of proof for the pain point, motivation or desired outcome of that section.
5. To collect proof, ask customers: How did you discover us? What problem were you trying to solve? How did we help you solve it? What were your goals? What was your desired outcome? [04866413d08b]
6. Prefer video testimonials. Focus them on the ideal customer's pain points, how the brand helped overcome them, and tie the goal back to what the ideal customer faces. [04866413d08b]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Headline explains who you are and what you do but not what is in it for the visitor. [706a0be05ca5]
2. Headline does not match the ad or search promise that earned the click. [706a0be05ca5]
3. Only 1 form of proof at the top, or proof placed mid-page or at the bottom only. [7f28a425cebc]
4. Featuring a small review count (for example 3 reviews) at the top. [7f28a425cebc]
5. "Featured in" logos with no quote from the outlet. [7f28a425cebc]
6. Weak visual hierarchy: when blurred, you cannot tell the main CTA. [4e7bcbfc5e4b]
7. Hero image or video that does not reinforce the value proposition or show what makes the brand unique. [ecf232bf372c]
8. Only 1 to 2 sections on benefits because of worry about page length. [145380876d79]
9. CTA only at the top of the page. [f7356787e0a5]
10. First and last name as 2 fields. [f391cf14459a]
11. Using the label as the placeholder, so it disappears after typing. [f391cf14459a]
12. "Contact us" heading on a contact page (redundant) or a vague line with no incentive. [f391cf14459a]
13. Hero CTA that sends people to a separate contact page instead of an on-page form (based on 1 test). [45c2e59e672b]
14. Generic customer reviews collected with no goal, which do not map to page claims. [04866413d08b]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **B2B and lead-gen source.** The conversion event is a form or booked call. Ecommerce translations (PDP, add to cart, free returns under the button, product in use as hero) come from the card writers, not the presenter. The cards also add invented ecommerce examples (wool socks chain, "Sizes run out? We restock or refund", "a jacket that survives ten winters"). Treat those as illustrations, not source content. [05cef19e3b81] [e5afd40ade58] [4b1eaf817c12]
- **Unsourced statistics.** 60% never scroll, 50 ms first impression, 60,000 times faster, 90% visual. Do not put these on client pages. [web check: origin of each statistic]
- **Conflict with common ecommerce advice on scrolling.** Much ecommerce CRO advice says mobile shoppers scroll a lot and that long PDPs are read below the fold. The "60% never scroll" claim and "80% of effort on the hero" may overweight the hero for product pages. The source itself also asks for 4 to 5+ benefit sections below the fold, which assumes people scroll. [needs brand data: scroll depth by device from heatmaps]
- **Dual CTAs.** The source praises Chargebee's dual CTAs. Card 4e7bcbfc5e4b says to avoid dual CTAs; that caution is the card's addition, not the source's. Keep both views; test.
- **FUD placement detail.** The transcript says reduce FUD "around the critical point of action" and in the hero. The "directly under the CTA" placement comes from the source's summary header, and fits the Snov.io example. [0d11fac972bd]
- **No frequency limit on repeated CTAs.** The source gives no rule for how many or how far apart. [f7356787e0a5]
- **Case results are thin.** 220% is 1 A/B test on 1 client. 142% is a bundle of changes. The $3M and 40 calls per week are not tied to a baseline. The carousel test gives no number. None states sample size, duration or significance.
- **Awareness framework is partial.** Only 2 low-awareness rows of the CXL table are given. High-awareness headline frames are not stated in the source (the card adds "lead with product name for high awareness").
- **"So that" chain has no stopping rule** in the source.
- **Progressive disclosure has no test result**, only the Segment example and a psychological argument. Multi-step forms can still lose people at each step.
- **Missing how-to**: no guidance on mobile layout, page speed, pricing display, offer structure, form tools, analytics setup or how to run the A/B tests.
