## 1. What this source is for

- Explains what an advertorial is (paid ad written to look like the host's editorial content) and why it works (readers carry the publication's trust over to the brand).
- Gives one writing rule: about 70% useful or fun content, about 30% or less pitch.
- Gives 2 quality tests for a draft: would a reader tear it out and keep it (Ogilvy), and would a reader share it.
- Helps an operator choose between a plain product ad and a sponsored content piece for cold readers.

## 2. Principles

1. **An advertorial borrows the host's trust.** Most people distrust plain ads but trust editorial articles that mention a company well. Putting the message inside a trusted publication makes readers think "it's in the Wall Street Journal, so it must be legit." Applies when the host is trusted by the target reader. Evidence: the speaker's reasoning plus screenshots of sponsored posts. [e8a2b0ea5103]
2. **Plain ads get skipped.** "Simply slapping some of these advertisements onto a publication isn't good enough. People tend to gloss over blatant advertisements." Applies to banner or display style ads placed next to content. Evidence: opinion. [e8a2b0ea5103] [94b27707001f]
3. **Write with a giving mindset (70/30).** Make the piece about 70% good content and about 30% promotion. Paid placement only pays off if people like, engage with and share the piece, and people do not share plain ads. The speaker's contrast: "spammy Sarah" throws in a blatant ad; "giving Gary" blends the ad into content and tries to give better information than the publication itself. Evidence: opinion plus AppSumo practice. The 70/30 split is a working rule of thumb, not a measured result. [94b27707001f]
4. **In deal emails, the 70% can be about the product itself.** At AppSumo, the 70% was a story or how-to around the product: its benefits, how to use it, its features. Less than 30% pitched the deal. So the "content" part does not have to be off topic. It has to be worth reading. Applies to owned email and deal copy. Evidence: the speaker's AppSumo account, no numbers beyond list size. [687f3ff8d7fe]
5. **The content angle can be loose or even negative.** Sponsored pieces do not need to praise the product or even be about it. Examples: Starbucks sponsoring an Onion satire piece on productivity; TGI Fridays sponsoring a Thrillist "worst office lunches ever" list. People will not share "how great Fridays is," but they will share "13 worst office lunches ever" with coworkers. Applies when the goal is reach and shares. Evidence: examples only. [155f2bc2faf1]
6. **Usefulness makes the piece last (tear-out test).** Ogilvy knew that if an ad benefited the reader, they would pay special attention and even rip it out of the magazine to keep. Applies to reference-style content. Evidence: retold history, no data. [343044bc4a83]
7. **A plain product ad converts poorly unless the context already matches.** A "Donnie loves Purina Puppy Chow" ad with a Buy button "could work" but "generally will have a very low conversion rate unless the article someone is reading is directly along the lines of what's the best dog food." Applies to paid display placements. Evidence: opinion, no numbers. [63b55235ae47]
8. **Indirect mentions still build the name.** An article saying Purina was named the number 1 brand, or that its stock is growing, still gets the name out and can lead to purchases, even though it is not about the product. Evidence: hypothetical. [63b55235ae47]
9. **Advertorials carry an ethics risk.** The speaker calls them "semi sneaky" and "kind of sneaky," and says they cause controversy in journalism because readers treat a credible publication's content as automatically credible. The source does not say how to handle disclosure. [e8a2b0ea5103]

## 3. Procedures

The source gives one worked exercise. It is a teaching example, not a real campaign. Steps as the source gives them: [ffa8114ae927] [63b55235ae47] [155f2bc2faf1]

1. Start from the baseline: a standard web ad with the product claim and a Buy button (the source's "Donnie loves Purina Puppy Chow" ad). Accept it will convert poorly unless the page around it is already about the product's category.
2. Pick where the piece would run. The source gives 2 options:
   - A business outlet (Wall Street Journal): a news-style angle, such as the brand named number 1 or its stock growing.
   - A fun listicle site (BuzzFeed): a light, shareable list, such as "21 dogs that have been working out way too hard," with the product dog saying "gotta get my protein bro."
3. Write most of the piece (about 70%) as content people would want on its own. Work the brand in lightly. Keep the pitch to about 30% or less.
4. Check the draft: is it fun or useful enough that people would share it or keep it?

For a deal or product email, the AppSumo pattern: [687f3ff8d7fe]

1. Do not write "buy this, buy this" for the whole email.
2. Build a story or how-to around the product.
3. Spend most of the email on benefits, how to use it and its features.
4. Keep the deal pitch under about 30% of the email.

What the source does not give: how to find or buy a placement, prices, how to brief a publisher, headline formulas, page layout, where the link goes, or how to measure results. Card bullets that say "route readers to a PDP or landing page," "apply 70/30 to the landing page," "measure reads to clicks to purchases," "disclose sponsorship" and "turn the tear-out test into buying guides or size charts" are the card writer's ecommerce adaptations, not the speaker's words. Treat them as suggestions to test, not source rules. [e8a2b0ea5103] [94b27707001f] [343044bc4a83] [155f2bc2faf1] [ffa8114ae927]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Running a blatant ad inside a publication and expecting it to be read. People gloss over it. [e8a2b0ea5103]
- Writing like "spammy Sarah": all pitch, nothing people would like, engage with or share. [94b27707001f]
- Sending emails that only say "buy this." People will not open daily blatant promotions and will unsubscribe. [687f3ff8d7fe]
- Picking an angle that only praises the brand ("how great Fridays is"). No one shares it. [155f2bc2faf1]
- Expecting a plain product ad with a Buy button to convert well next to unrelated content. [63b55235ae47]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Different meaning of "advertorial."** This source means sponsored content on a publisher's site. Most sources in this folder mean a brand-hosted presell page sent paid social traffic. Rules here (70/30, share test, credibility transfer from the host) do not carry over to a brand-hosted page automatically, because a brand's own page has no host publication to borrow trust from. Pass 2 should keep this separate.
- **Possible conflict on pitch share.** This source keeps the pitch to about 30% or less and favours loose or fun angles. Presell sources in this folder may push a stronger, earlier product and offer. Pass 2 should compare, not average.
- **70/30 is not measured.** No method to count the split and no test behind it. Treat it as an editing guide. [94b27707001f]
- **Internal tension on what the 70% is.** In the AppSumo example the 70% is product benefits and how-to. In the listicle examples the content is only loosely tied to the product. The source does not say when to use which. A reasonable reading is: owned email to buyers leans product how-to; paid reach on publisher sites leans fun or useful off-product content. This is an inference, not the source's rule.
- **No conversion or cost data.** Reach claims ("millions of views") are unnamed. Loose angles mean weak buying intent; the source never measures sales. `[needs brand data: click, add-to-cart and purchase rates from any past sponsored or content placements]`
