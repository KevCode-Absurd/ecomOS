## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a checklist of 11 changes to test on a product page, bundle page or landing page: targeted social proof, image-led selling, the above-the-fold formula, visual hierarchy, pop-up timing, cutting options, bundles and price psychology, humanizing the brand, FUD reduction, scannable copy, and an A/B testing routine. Its strongest part is the bundle work (3 client test results with numbers). It is thin on implementation steps inside any tool and gives no test design detail.

## 2. Principles

1. **Match testimonials to the buyer's actual objections.** Condition: when you can collect objection data from customers. Ask 2 survey or user-test questions: "What almost stopped you from buying today?" and "What motivated you to buy today?" Feed the answers back into which testimonials you show. If price is the main objection, show testimonials about value for money or ROI. Evidence: Parcel Safety (gas mask respirators) research found 2 objections: "will this actually protect me from harmful chemicals" and "will the mask fog up"; the chosen testimonial named eye and lung protection, sparks, metal dust and "doesn't fog over". Card adds mining support tickets and "top 3 objections"; those come from the curator header, not the presenter's speech. [83fd5d9ddc1a] [needs brand data: post-purchase survey answers, support tickets, user-test notes]
2. **Relevant proof beats volume or press logos.** Condition: all pages; strongest when review count is low. Source says press-logo strips "don't cut it anymore because everyone is already doing it". If you have only about 30 reviews, source suggests you may not want to feature the count; handpick 3 to 4 relevant testimonials instead. If you use press, pull a quote tied to a pain point (Solo Wave example: "my tried and true trick for reducing morning puffiness"). [83fd5d9ddc1a] [needs brand data: review count and review text]
3. **Put social proof at the point of action.** Condition: PDPs and bundle pages. Place it next to bundle selectors, under Add to Cart, or above the fold. Parcel Safety placed it near bundles and Add to Cart on all key PDPs; another example sits right under "choose your bundle"; Solo Wave puts it above the fold, which the source says most people never scroll past. Card caveat: placement heuristic, not a verified universal rule. [83fd5d9ddc1a]
4. **Let images do the selling.** Condition: when recordings show visitors skim text. Source says most users read little or nothing and decide from images; he bases this on Clarity recordings of client sites showing skipped text blocks and high engagement and clicks on images. Each image should carry the value prop, win emotional buy-in and motivate action. Pair short benefit text and icons with the images. The "60,000 times faster" claim is cited without a source. [b721b5ef1086] [web check: 60,000x image processing claim] [needs brand data: Clarity recordings or heatmaps of image vs text engagement]
5. **People buy in 3 dimensions: emotional, social, functional.** Condition: hero and gallery images. Emotional = buy-in feeling; social = how others see them or use with others; functional = the practical benefit (source examples: better sleep, less stress). Source says the image "should have all 3" and emotional matters most because "people buy with emotion, not logic" (opinion). [b721b5ef1086] [2070e6c0b648]
6. **The above-the-fold section matters more than any other section.** Condition: all landing pages and PDPs. Source says it is the only section 100% of visitors see. It needs 4 parts: clear value prop, supporting visual, 1 obvious CTA, social proof. [2070e6c0b648]
7. **Visual hierarchy tells the eye where to go.** Condition: any page that looks busy, or where headline, body and CTA compete. Tools: size contrast, color contrast, separation. Bad example: everything capitalized, everything bold, many colors. Source admits this is "really hard to crack" without a design-minded person on the team. [5d7260153d8b]
8. **Pop-up timing beats pop-up reach.** Condition: discount or email pop-ups. Source says a pop-up in the first 3 to 5 seconds creates friction because visitors do not yet know who you are, and leads to exits or no opt-in. Trigger on intent instead. Fewer people see it, but conversions rise. Source reports "up to 125%" conversion increases from timing changes alone (client experience, no detail). [396f2ef27e9e]
9. **Fewer choices, more sales (paradox of choice).** Condition: cluttered navigation, many page elements, or many bundle tiers. "Too much choice equals no choice at all." Remove elements recordings and heatmaps show are unused. Source calls this counterintuitive and applies it to bundles, navigation and PDP elements. Card caveat: removing a tier can hurt if it served a real segment. [d0db4bcd8f2f]
10. **Sell bundles, not just a single product.** Condition: when the page sells 1 product only. Source says single-product pages "leave a ton of money on the table" and that bundling "can single-handedly boost conversions by upwards of 90% in some cases" (no case given for the 90%). Two bundle types: same product in bulk at a discount, or related products together to raise perceived value. [fa82d767b952]
11. **Do not make the shopper do math.** Condition: any bundle or discount display. State savings plainly and prominently, in the tier headline, not in body text. [fa82d767b952]
12. **Price anchoring: a higher reference price makes the same price feel like a steal.** Condition: bundle tiers with compare-at prices. Source raised compare-at prices while keeping sale prices the same, so displayed savings grew. See section 9 for the legal risk. [e8c3a0cb2c42]
13. **Middle option bias: people pick the middle tier because it feels safe.** Condition: 3-tier bundles where you know which tier fits most buyers. Structure savings so the middle tier looks like the best deal. [e8c3a0cb2c42] [needs brand data: tier sales mix and typical units per order]
14. **People buy from brands they know, like and trust.** Condition: trust-driven categories and About or PDP story sections. Humanize with team photos and stories, behind-the-scenes content, customer success videos and conversational copy. Source says this is "a lot harder to track" than a pop-up trigger; no test result given. [e56b14bccb8c]
15. **Reduce FUD (fear, uncertainty, doubt) at the point of action.** Condition: Add to Cart, homepage above the fold, and other critical action points. Shoppers are cautious ("trust economy"), some have been burned before. Four reducers: money-back guarantee and return policy, security badges, clear shipping info (what and when), transparent pricing with no hidden costs. No isolated test numbers. [9f5465768bf6]
16. **People scan, they do not read.** Condition: all pages, except when a reader has been pulled into a long, engaging sales letter. Use bullets, icons, subheadings and selective bolding. Source says this has been A/B tested "so many times" with "huge increases" (no numbers). Source calls this "a cousin" of visual hierarchy but distinct. [4ef5d75f8104]
17. **A landing page is never done.** Condition: always, even for strong pages. Source's clients converting at 5 to 6% ("well above average") still test. Amazon cited at 12,000+ experiments a year. [51c0c6b48e3a] [web check: Amazon experiment count] [needs brand data: current conversion rate]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Targeted social proof [83fd5d9ddc1a]
1. Survey buyers and run user tests with 2 questions: what almost stopped you, what motivated you. The card adds: mine support tickets.
2. List the main objections (curator header says top 3).
3. From your reviews, handpick 3 to 4 testimonials that answer those exact objections. For price objections, pick value-for-money quotes.
4. For press mentions, replace logo-only strips with a quote relevant to a pain point.
5. Place them near bundle options, directly under Add to Cart, and above the fold.
6. If you have few reviews (source example: about 30), do not lead with the count.

### 3.2 Image audit [b721b5ef1086]
1. Watch session recordings (source uses Microsoft Clarity) to confirm visitors skip text and engage with images. [verify UI]
2. For each gallery image, check it shows at least one of: happy people using it, how it is used, use cases or occasions, ease of use, portability, use with others, before and after, benefit stats.
3. Add icons plus short benefit text on or beside images so text and image carry the same value prop.
4. Include video of the product in use where you can (Solo Wave example).
5. Target: a visitor who only looks at the images has enough to buy.

### 3.3 Above-the-fold formula [2070e6c0b648]
1. Headline value prop: who you are, what you do, what's in it for me. Readable in 5 seconds or less. The "what's in it for me" part is the most important: the tangible day-to-day benefit.
2. Body text under the headline reinforcing the top selling points.
3. A supporting visual that demonstrates the product and covers emotional, social and functional benefits.
4. One obvious CTA. Blur test: blur the whole page; the button must still stand out as the clear action. Make its text benefit-oriented, not just "Shop now" or "Buy now". The source does not give example button wording.
5. Social proof above the fold (example: "400,000+ customers protected").

### 3.4 Visual hierarchy [5d7260153d8b]
1. Size: headline 3 to 4x body text. Desktop body is typically 16px, so headline about 48 to 57px (source's numbers; see section 9 on the math).
2. Color: separate headline color from body color (example: brown headline, gray body). Give the CTA a distinct accent color and separate it from nearby elements (examples: pink Add to Bag and a matching pink announcement bar; orange button in a client redesign).
3. Strip competing emphasis: no all-caps everywhere, no bolding everywhere, fewer colors.
4. Check mobile separately. The client "before" on mobile had a huge logo, a huge announcement bar and a button lost in the layout.
5. Check the eye moves top to bottom: headline, body, button, with the image contrasting well behind.

### 3.5 Pop-up triggers [396f2ef27e9e]
1. Turn off pop-ups that fire on arrival (first 3 to 5 seconds).
2. Exit intent: fire when the mouse moves toward closing. Offer a discount (example: 10% off next order) for an email signup, then retarget. Source says this works especially well for first-time visitors. Mouse-based exit intent is a desktop behaviour; the source does not cover mobile.
3. Post add to cart: fire right after Add to Cart for shoppers who have not checked out, with "here's X% off to seal the deal". Source says "we've tested this a ton" (no numbers).
4. Scroll depth: in Clarity, find where people lose interest (PDP, category page or product description). If data shows, for example, 50% drop off while reading the description, fire at that point with an offer of help plus a discount (example copy: "Need help deciding? Here's 10% off"). [verify UI] [needs brand data: scroll-depth heatmap per page]
5. Judge by sales and conversions, not pop-up views.
The source names no pop-up tool and gives no setup steps. [verify UI]

### 3.6 Cut options [d0db4bcd8f2f]
1. Watch recordings of the bundle section. Warning sign: shoppers pick only the cheapest tiers or leave.
2. Hypothesis: too many tiers. Cut to 3 (Wildman: 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 pack cut to 1, 3, 5 pack). A/B test it.
3. Count navigation items. Keep under 7; fewer is better.
4. In heatmaps, list nav items and PDP elements with near-zero clicks. Remove them. [needs brand data: click heatmap per page]

### 3.7 Bundles and price display [fa82d767b952] [e8c3a0cb2c42]
1. Create bundles: bulk packs at a discount, or complementary products as a kit (example: cleanser + moisturizer + serum). Name the kit by outcome (example: "2-step hair regrowth kit") and show the saving.
2. Add optional add-ons near the order point (example: "complete your hair growth kit" add-ons and a warranty).
3. Put savings in each tier headline as a money amount (example: "3 pack: save $10", "5 pack: save $20") rather than a % in small body text.
4. Price anchoring (test): raise compare-at prices, keep sale prices, so shown savings grow. Check legal rules first (section 9).
5. Middle option bias (test): decide which tier most buyers realistically need (Wildman: 3-pack, often bought for 2 friends; 5-pack rarely bought). Set savings so the step from tier 1 to tier 2 gives the same money saving as the step from tier 2 to tier 3 (Wildman after: $20 and $20).
6. A/B test each change. Source urges playing with these levers rather than assuming.

### 3.8 Humanize the brand [e56b14bccb8c]
1. Add team photos and the founding story and mission.
2. Add behind-the-scenes content on how the brand started and works.
3. Add customer success videos: real users, why they like it, how it fits daily life.
4. Rewrite copy to sound like talking to a friend.
No placement or test guidance given.

### 3.9 FUD pass [9f5465768bf6]
1. Walk your funnel and landing page. At each step ask: "What would I want to know before I buy this?"
2. Add or surface: money-back guarantee and what happens if unhappy; security badges; shipping cost and delivery timing; full price up front with no hidden costs.
3. Place them at the critical action points: under Add to Cart, homepage above the fold.
[needs brand data: return policy, shipping times and costs, payment providers]

### 3.10 Scannable content [4ef5d75f8104]
1. Replace large text blocks with bullets and icon lines.
2. Split what remains under clear subheadings so users get an overview and jump to what they need.
3. Bold or highlight only the specific phrases you want noticed.

### 3.11 Testing routine [51c0c6b48e3a]
1. Put every hypothesis in a list and prioritize by highest impact and lowest effort (source calls this the PXL framework; see section 9).
2. Test 1 element at a time so you know what caused the change.
3. Run at least 2 weeks to cover a full cycle, and require statistical significance. [needs brand data: traffic and conversions per variant to judge if 2 weeks is enough]
4. Document winners and losers and why. Source says losing tests produced insights that led to a run of wins.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Generic social proof: press-logo strips only, or showing a small review count (about 30). [83fd5d9ddc1a]
2. Social proof far from the action point. [83fd5d9ddc1a]
3. Relying on text when most visitors only look at images. [b721b5ef1086]
4. Above the fold that says what you are but not what's in it for me; no body text; no social proof; a CTA that competes with other elements. Even big brands (Gymshark) do this. [2070e6c0b648]
5. Generic CTA text like "Shop now" or "Buy now". [2070e6c0b648]
6. Headline nearly the same size as body text ("the biggest mistake I see when auditing"). [5d7260153d8b]
7. All-caps, bolding everything, too many colors. [5d7260153d8b]
8. Oversized logo and announcement bar on mobile that bury the button. [5d7260153d8b]
9. Pop-up on arrival (3 to 5 seconds). [396f2ef27e9e]
10. Too many bundle tiers; too many nav items; PDP elements nobody clicks. [d0db4bcd8f2f]
11. Selling a single product with no bundle. [fa82d767b952]
12. Making shoppers calculate savings. [fa82d767b952]
13. Hidden costs revealed late. [9f5465768bf6]
14. Dense text blocks. [4ef5d75f8104]
15. Testing many elements at once; stopping tests too soon; not documenting losers. [51c0c6b48e3a]
16. Treating a page as done. [51c0c6b48e3a]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Compare-at inflation is a legal risk.** Raising a "was" price the product was never sold at can count as deceptive reference pricing in many markets. The source presents it purely as psychology. Only use a compare-at price the brand can defend. [e8c3a0cb2c42] [web check: reference-price rules for the brand's market, for example FTC pricing guides (US), EU Omnibus 30-day prior price rule, India consumer protection and dark pattern guidelines]
- **The source breaks its own 1-variable rule.** The anchoring plus middle option test changed 2 things at once, so the +11.74% cannot be split between them. The 5-to-3 tier test and the savings-in-headline test also changed tier count or both wording and placement. [e8c3a0cb2c42] [51c0c6b48e3a]
- **Numbers lack test detail.** No traffic, sample size, duration or significance for any result. The Sumach +60% came with "several other changes". The 80%, 90% and 125% figures are "up to" or "upwards of" claims.
- **Headline math does not match.** 3 to 4x of 16px is 48 to 64px, not 48 to 57px (57px is about 3.6x). Numbers are desktop only; the source gives no mobile sizes. Do not apply 48px+ headlines to mobile without checking layout. [5d7260153d8b]
- **PXL described loosely.** The source calls PXL its agency's framework and describes it as impact vs effort. PXL is commonly credited to CXL and uses a yes/no scored checklist, which is different from simple impact/effort scoring. [web check: PXL framework definition] [51c0c6b48e3a]
- **2 weeks is a rule of thumb.** It does not replace a sample size estimate. Low-traffic stores may need longer. [needs brand data: sessions and orders per week per tested page] [51c0c6b48e3a]
- **Discount pop-ups have costs the source skips.** It gives no guidance on margin, discount conditioning, or stacking a post-ATC discount on top of bundle savings. [needs brand data: gross margin per SKU and bundle]
- **Exit intent on mobile is not covered.** The trigger is described with a mouse moving to the X. [verify UI: how the chosen pop-up tool handles mobile exit intent]
- **Borrowed claims.** "60,000 times faster" and "Amazon 12,000+ experiments a year" are unsourced. [web check: both claims]
- **Review count advice may conflict with common practice.** Many stores show star rating and count whatever the number; the source says a low count (about 30) may be better left out. Opinion only, no test.
- **Curator header vs speech.** "Top 3 objections" and "mine support tickets" appear in the header and the card, not in the presenter's speech (he says surveys and user testing). Header also says "run tests for a minimum of 2 weeks to achieve statistical significance"; the presenter says 2 weeks minimum and significance as separate conditions.
- **Humanizing, FUD and scannable tricks have no numbers.** Treat them as hygiene and trust builders, not proven lifts.
- **Missing how.** No tool steps for pop-ups or compare-at prices, no example benefit CTA wording, no guidance on how many images or which order, no guidance on where on the page the humanizing content goes.
- **Scope notes in the header point to old folder names** ("06-MEA", "09-EML"). Paid media and email flows live in neighbour folders; this mini playbook covers the page only.
