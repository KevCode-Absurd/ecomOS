## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator design a long, personalised quiz funnel for cold paid traffic. It walks through a live 30-plus step quiz question by question. It names 4 working parts: data-gathering questions, "micro-commitment" info screens every 3 to 5 questions, varied input types, and a results summary that shows the gap between the shopper's current and goal state before the offer. It also argues that quiz answers are first-party customer research for ad hooks and avatar work. It is strong on structure and psychology. It is thin on how to build (no tool steps, no logic setup), has no numbers on results, and its example sells an app, not a product.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 Why a quiz

1. **A quiz has 2 jobs: front-end personalisation and back-end data.** On the front end, it makes the shopper feel the plan or product is made for them. On the back end, the brand collects every answer and learns, straight from the avatar, what they need, what outcome they want and what they want from the product. The presenter says this is "really really important" to understand. Condition: his view, no data. The card turns this into a product-quiz rule: each question must either change the recommendation or give useful research. [58789a695fea] [needs brand data: quiz answer exports]

2. **Some brands send almost all cold traffic to quizzes, not PDPs.** The app sends traffic from YouTube, Instagram and Meta "straight through" several quiz funnels, and "not any traffic to PDPs or any other type of landing pages". He read this from "their traffic data" but does not say which tool. Condition: 1 app brand. It shows what one advertiser does, not that it works better for a product store. [web check: the app's current traffic split and landing pages] [a7e987adb7a8]

3. **A long quiz builds commitment before the offer, so the offer matters a bit less.** He says if someone goes through "20, 30, 40 steps", they are "very committed" by the end and "trust you even before they reach this point", which is what you want with cold traffic. So the offer "falls a little bit in second place", though it is "still extremely important", and it is "even more important" in funnels that show the offer quickly. Condition: his opinion. The card calls long quizzes and commitment steps "hypotheses, not guaranteed retail improvements". [a7e987adb7a8]

4. **You control the narrative through the answers.** A quiz is not just "click on a few buttons and get something". The brand steers what the shopper sees next from what they chose. He calls this "using their answers against them, quote unquote". Condition: his framing. The card limits it to real branching that the team can test and maintain. [4a5a01ce29d7]

### 2.2 Question design

5. **Open by sorting who is in front of you.** The first questions (age range, gender, body type) "categorise" the person so the rest of the quiz can speak to them. Later copy and graphs change by gender (a testosterone graph appears for a male path). Condition: the example is a fitness app where age and sex change the plan. The card disagrees for product quizzes: "Skip demographic or health questions that do not affect a valid product choice." See section 9. [58789a695fea] [4a5a01ce29d7]

6. **Make the shopper picture the outcome early.** The "body you want" question shows images, and the options change with the goal chosen before (lose weight shows "slim body" and "slim and shredded"; gain muscle shows other builds such as "hero build"). He says this ties the shopper's feelings to the outcome, and "you need to really tie the goal or the outcome that the person wants back to your product". Condition: his view. The same chosen image comes back at the top of the results page. [4a5a01ce29d7] [d20c2b44905c]

7. **Ask data-gathering questions even when they do not change the offer.** Diet (keto, vegetarian), sugar intake and water intake are asked to learn the avatar. "It doesn't really matter at the end because you're still going to sell them your product." You may find the people taking the quiz "are not who you expect it to be". Condition: his view, and he says "depending on the product, it's not like this in every case". The card is stricter: each field must improve selection or give useful research, and do not add questions just to make the quiz longer. [58789a695fea] [b73a7db33bae]

8. **Use frequent answers to write hooks and headlines.** If many takers say they like jogging or martial arts, use that angle "to generate hooks or headlines" for funnels and ads. Common setbacks and goals are the same kind of input. Condition: his suggestion, no example of it done. The card adds: compare frequent needs with the assumed avatar, look for gaps in the range, and remember respondents are only the traffic you sent. [58789a695fea] [needs brand data: answer distributions by question and traffic source]

9. **Tie the goal to a date with an event question.** "Do you have an important event coming up?" (he picks a vacation, then a date). He says it adds a deadline: "I need to get to this result fast", which raises motivation. Condition: common in fitness and weight-loss funnels, per the presenter. The card turns it into a delivery check for products: ask the date only if timing changes the recommendation. [5f24ab9d9d72]

10. **Ask lifestyle and "whole life" questions to widen the outcome.** "Not only a better body, but improve your lifestyle too" (reduce stress, improve sleep), and "What challenge would you like to join?" (21 days no sugar). He says this frames the product as making you "a better person all around", not just a body change. These goals come back in the offer section. Condition: his reading of the example. [a7e987adb7a8]

11. **Ask about past failures and current habits.** "Have you experienced any of these issues in your previous fitness attempts?" and "How many times per week have you trained in the last 3 months?" He says this reads the person's "mind space" and where they are in life, not only fitness level. Condition: his view. The card applies it to products: ask what failed before and what routine is realistic, then map that to a starter configuration. [6bd33483c267] [4a5a01ce29d7]

12. **Ask what they like to make it feel like you care.** "Choose the products you like" (foods such as broccoli, rice, eggs, chicken). He says this "makes it feel like they really care about who you are as a person". Condition: his view. The card adds: respect exclusions and dislikes in the main recommendation and in cross-sells. [6bd33483c267]

### 2.3 Input types

13. **Mix input types. Do not run 20 steps of buttons.** He says many quizzes are "buttons on buttons on buttons ... for like 20 steps, which is not ideal". Different inputs "stimulate the brain" to think about the answer. The example uses image choices, single choice, multi-select, numeric fields (height, weight, target weight), a date picker, and a like/dislike scale. Condition: his view, no test. [b73a7db33bae]

14. **A like/dislike scale feels gamified.** The example rates cardio, yoga and stretching, lifting weights and pull-ups on a scale from dislike to "like a lot". He likes it because it "feels kind of gamified". He has **not** used or built it himself yet and plans to start. The card says: test a slider only if it is easier than buttons for this choice. [b73a7db33bae]

### 2.4 Info screens (micro-commitments)

15. **Put an info or "micro-commitment" screen between questions to stop question fatigue.** Question fatigue is when you "bombard the person with questions" and they struggle to answer after a few. Put an info screen "around every 3 to 4 questions or 5 questions". Basis: "what we've seen from the quizzes that we've run". The card calls 3 to 5 "a source heuristic, not an optimum". [2e104f23d8b2]

16. **Each info screen should give value, so the shopper trusts the quiz.** Screens tell the shopper what to do based on their answers ("based on this you need to do this"). He says the shopper feels they are "getting something out of it", here education, and repeated value builds trust. [2e104f23d8b2]

17. **Branch info screen copy on the answers.** Example: the nutrition screen says "to make the most gains in muscle mass ... you need enough total calories each day and adequate protein" for a muscle goal, and different copy for a weight-loss goal. On the "why people give up" screen, he says copy could reflect the setback they picked (poor coaching, poor plan). Condition: needs branching logic. The card: branch on use, format and prior frustrations; limit branches to what the team can test and maintain. [2e104f23d8b2] [4a5a01ce29d7]

18. **Use "feel-good" screens to normalise setbacks and praise the shopper.** After the past-failures question: "Thanks for sharing. We've learned that a lot of people have faced the same setbacks." He reads it as "you're not alone ... you're not a failure ... we are here for you". After training frequency: "You've worked out more than 62% of users ... it'll be easier for you to maintain a workout plan." Condition: example copy. The card warns: a percentage comparison needs a real measured population. [2e104f23d8b2] [needs brand data: real answer distributions before using any "more than X% of users" line]

19. **Show the shopper's own numbers back in a graph mid-quiz.** After height, weight, target weight and event date, a screen says "the last plan you'll ever need" and "we believe you may hit your goal weight of 80 kg by March 12th, 2026", with a line from today to the goal date. He says showing today's date and the goal date "makes it feel more real ... makes it feel achievable", and that this is why such quizzes "perform really well" if done right. Condition: no data given. The card forbids simulated computation and invented future-body projections or guaranteed result dates for merchants. [d20c2b44905c] [4a5a01ce29d7]

20. **Colour can matter on info screens.** The "you're not alone" screen is blue. He says he used to think colour did not matter, but "if you want to be really really optimised, in some cases they do", and this blue is "soothing" and fits the message. Condition: his opinion from 1 screen. No card covers it.

21. **Add a safety screen where the product has health risk.** A step lists medical conditions that need a physician. He reads it as a way to be safe and not be liable. Condition: health-related offers. No card covers it. [web check: disclaimer rules for health, fitness and supplement claims in the target market]

### 2.5 Results summary and offer

22. **Split the funnel into 2 parts: the quiz, then the summary.** After contact capture, the "second part" uses all answers to summarise where the shopper is and what they need to reach their goal. [d20c2b44905c]

23. **Use a loading screen to signal a custom build.** He says his agency is "heavy on" loading steps; they make it "feel more customised" and like the system is "going through the data to build a plan made for you". Condition: his practice. The card warns: do not simulate computation. See section 9. [d20c2b44905c]

24. **Reinforce commitment right before the offer.** A pop-up asks the shopper to commit (the answer he picks: "Yes, I'll do my first workout today"). He says after about 30 steps, brands want the commitment "solid" so the shopper "convinces themselves" before they reach the product. [a7e987adb7a8]

25. **The results page shows current state versus goal, using the shopper's own inputs.** At the top: the body image they chose at the start, shown as "now" versus "you in 6 months". Then a personal summary (current BMI with a plain explanation, daily calorie intake, daily water intake). [d20c2b44905c]

26. **Put dates on the goal, not a vague time span.** He says "6 months" feels "a little bit too far in the future"; he would put the real dates on the results page, as the mid-quiz graph did. Condition: his suggestion. The card forbids guaranteed result dates for merchants. [d20c2b44905c]

27. **Then the offer: what you get, goals, social proof, timer, buy box, guarantee.** The order in the example is in section 3.3. For ecommerce he says to swap app-store-style reviews for a carousel of customer photos, reviews and videos of people using the product, and to put the product and offer in the buy box. [d20c2b44905c] [a7e987adb7a8]

28. **Remove exits at the buy box.** There is an element below the buy box that takes people out of the funnel "at the most important step". He would remove it. The card adds: keep access to delivery, returns, product details and support. [a7e987adb7a8]

29. **Ecommerce can learn from subscription-app funnels, and the other way round.** He says ecommerce brands miss things SaaS apps do in direct response, and SaaS misses things ecommerce does right. Condition: his opinion. [a7e987adb7a8]

### 3.3 Results and offer page order (from the example)

1. Top: the goal image the shopper chose at the start, "now" versus "you in 6 months". Presenter would add real dates here.
2. "Different data between versions of you" (a comparison block).
3. Personal summary from their answers: current BMI ("slightly overweight") with a plain explanation of BMI, daily calorie intake, daily water intake.
4. What you get: for the app, a structured workout plan, a daily plan, a step-by-step approach, for beginners and advanced users.
5. Goals, repeated from the quiz: reduce stress, improve sleep, feel healthier.
6. Social proof: app reviews. For ecommerce, a carousel of customer photos, reviews and videos of people using the product.
7. Discount expiry timer, very visible and easy to read (6:30 shown). See section 9.
8. Buy box with the product and offer.
9. Money-back guarantee.
10. More reviews.
11. An element that sends people out of the funnel. Presenter would remove it.

Card version for a product store: summarise the answers that decide the recommendation, show the SKU, size or bundle with the rule that links it to those answers, show any consumption assumption and let the shopper correct it, then product details, price, real reviews, delivery and returns, and a buy button. Keep summary, product and price consistent. [d20c2b44905c] [a7e987adb7a8] [6bd33483c267]
