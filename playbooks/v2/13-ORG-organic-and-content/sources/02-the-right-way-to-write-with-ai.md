## 1. What this source is for

It gives a philosophy and operating framework for writing with AI, not a platform or tactics guide: why AI output reads as generic "slop," a three-tier model of content (commodity, personality, original), the "I" vs "You" copy heuristic, a strict division of labor between human and AI (human originates, AI only repeats and remixes "approved language"), how to build a personal "digital brain" or personal language model (PLM) as the raw material AI works from, how voice breaks down into a few identifiable variables, and how the same idea scales to a company ("company brain"). Use it to diagnose why AI drafts feel generic, to decide what a human must write versus what AI can safely remix, to set up a content library/company-brain structure, and to build or maintain a founder or brand voice. The source explicitly places paid media setup, direct-response sales-letter or advertorial copywriting, and email/SMS automation outside its scope (pointing to 10-MCR, 07-LPG and 14-EML respectively) [88b7d114b8b5]. Skip the sponsor read for Brex, personal banter, and the agency/product name-drops (LCA, TypeShare, Ship 30 for 30) — none of it is part of the transferable framework [88b7d114b8b5].

## 2. Principles

1. **Language is mostly "open source"; ownership of writing is really association, not authorship.** No one owns individual words or most word groupings — "you don't own the word tomato... I don't own the word catastrophe." What people call "AI slop" when they read it is usually just commodity content: writing not attributable to any one person because it repeats a shared idea. The easiest way to build "ownership" (association) is publishing on a topic repeatedly. [712445a708f1] [cbbb787360b8]
2. **Content splits into three tiers: commodity, personality and original.** Commodity: universal, shared facts anyone could write ("eat healthy to lose weight," "save your coffee money and invest in the S&P 500"). Personality: content anchored in a person's specific, unshared biography and stated positions. Original: rare, category-defining insight reached only after years of "crystallizing" a point of view — the source's example is Pythagoras's six words, "the earth is round, not flat." **Gap:** the cards cover commodity and personality content in depth; no card distills the "original" tier or the Pythagoras example — see section 9. [cbbb787360b8] [49c888bb326e]
3. **AI writes slop because it doesn't know which side of a settled debate you're on, not because it lacks skill.** Two commodity positions on the same topic (save your coffee money and invest vs. saving coffee money won't make you rich) are both "correct" as writing; AI can't guess which one is yours unless you tell it. The fix is recording your stance, not re-prompting. [cbbb787360b8]
4. **Write from "I," not "You," to escape commodity phrasing.** Talking to the reader as "you should" leans commodity and can read as "I'm better than you, therefore you should." Writing "I did this / I think this" is personality content — what this specific person thinks, not what the category typically says. [2b0c662e2dd0]
5. **AI's job is to repeat you, not write for you.** The ghostwriter analogy: a ghostwriter never invents a client's ideas; they stitch sentences the client already said (chapter 1, chapter 2, a blog post, a recorded meeting) into something new. AI should do the mechanical version of the same thing — recombine your own "approved language" — not generate first-time thinking. Skipping the writing step and asking the LLM to "just do it" is, per Cole, where slop comes from. [ad4c55b5d306] [4392aa839fe7]
6. **Commodity content still has real value — it's just the lowest-leverage content to keep writing yourself.** For beginners in a topic, the most basic commodity advice is often the most useful thing you can give them. The problem is repeating it "for the 999th time" yourself is low-leverage; the source's hope is that AI takes over that repetition so the human's time goes to higher-tier content. [bdf52e0f0da8] [cbbb787360b8]
7. **Build the "unmade data set": your own unwritten stories, mistakes and stated positions.** Because everyone uses the same LLMs trained on the same public corpus, your only real moat is content that has literally never been articulated before — your formative experiences, specific failures, and the positions you hold on commodity debates. Even a "boring" fact (Cole's own example: he moved across the country twice in four years) resonates because other people have lived a version of it and no AI can invent it for you. [33bc4c19a527]
8. **The unmade data set needs a home: a personal language model / "digital brain."** The source's claim is that the storage tool doesn't matter (a doc, a spreadsheet, Obsidian) — what matters is that it's structured enough for AI to read from it. This is the input layer that a volume floor (principle 15) depends on. [0d61d18719cc]
9. **Personality content generally outperforms pure credibility.** Illustration, not measurement: the source claims there are less-credentialed personal-finance creators with strong personal narrative who reach bigger audiences than Ray Dalio, a multi-billionaire. Credibility still matters ("you want to learn from someone who's done the thing"), but per the source it isn't "the winning card" — personality and how much the person lets you into their life is. [49c888bb326e]
10. **Association builds in two layers.** Layer 1 (base): write about a topic a lot so people connect the topic with you — the source's example is that constant AI-startup-idea content built that association for the podcast, independent of any single piece. Layer 2 (stronger): the audience knows specific personal facts about you — where you're from, your path, your failures — the way people learn about a new acquaintance at a first coffee meeting. Writing online is "the scalable version" of that same conversation. [712445a708f1] [49c888bb326e]
11. **Voice is not mystique — it's a small bundle of identifiable variables.** Word choice (a neutral, an elevated, or a blunt/edgy word for the same idea reads as three different personalities), sentence length and structure (long semicolon-heavy vs. short and terse), and stance (opinion-led vs. citation-led) are the levers. Voice is "intentionally created," not something that just happens. [612587c6befe]
12. **When an AI or ghostwritten draft "doesn't sound like you," locate the one wrong variable — don't discard the draft.** Cole's own anecdote: a furious ghostwriting client said a piece "doesn't sound like me." Cole hadn't misjudged the whole piece — the client happened to speak by referencing studies and facts, and Cole's draft leaned on opinion instead. Adding 2–3 phrases ("according to the New York Times," "according to Harvard Business Review") — about 1% of the piece — fixed it completely. [612587c6befe]
13. **You discover your voice by writing and publishing, not by figuring it out first.** Most people who say "I don't like my voice" can't actually name what they don't like about it, and per the source that's usually a sign the real issue is fear of putting themselves out there, not a voice problem. The fix is shipping small pieces and comparing variants, the same way you already know whether you're "a pizza or a pasta person" without having thought hard about it. [8b3238183f67] [910b7d45cd6e]
14. **Most feared "commodity" opinions are low-risk to publish.** The source's own example: a ghostwriting client was scared to publish "the key to marketing is knowing your customer" — a statement most of the market already agrees with, so there's little exposure in saying it. [910b7d45cd6e]
15. **Every piece of content sits on a timely–timeless spectrum, and the two have opposite value curves.** Timely content (reacting to a launch, holiday, trend) spikes hard for about 24 hours and then loses nearly all value; you can't reuse it later. Timeless content (an essay, a durable how-to, a personal story) pays little immediately but compounds for years. The source's own worked example: "here's how to start a Substack" is timeless; adding "Substack just launched this feature... in 2026" makes the exact same idea timely and un-reusable. Decide which you're making, and don't expect timely payoff from a timeless piece. [3014faaee9da]
16. **Before creating anything, weigh its short-, medium- and long-term value.** A 20-minute post repeating a known idea adds a little "approved language" to your library; a two-week essay that crystallizes a genuinely new point of view adds far more, and the source's own example essay on AI is one he expects to keep referencing for roughly a decade. Not every piece should be judged as if it returns equally. [1193451bea64]
17. **Set an explicit minimum volume floor before judging what content "works."** The source states his own agency's floor as five pieces of content per day — his stated bar for giving content "a fair shot." This is his own agency's number, not a universal rule; calibrate it to your team's capacity. [7d684acdc24b]
18. **Separate two distinct problems: infrastructure (can you ship the volume) and input (do you have anything original to feed it).** Fixing the systems to post more often without fixing the quality of ideas feeding them just produces more generic content, faster. Solve infra first, then ask "where are the ideas coming from?" [ed26cf9e8b40] [4392aa839fe7]
19. **Deprioritize virality micro-tactics until the library and infrastructure exist.** Exact posting time, chasing virality, and paying for retweets are, per the source, irrelevant if there's no library and no system behind them — "none of it matters" at that stage. Redirect that effort into building the reusable library first. [3335c7ab8443]
20. **A company can build a "company brain": a compounding library of original long-form thinking that feeds every downstream format.** The source's opinion is that this is the single best marketing spend available to a company with budget — better than another round of promotional posts — because short-form, video, sales enablement and product messaging can all be mined from it later. This only pays off if the company also builds the machinery to remix and distribute the library; buying or producing content without that machinery is, per the source, "a horrible decision." [bda11c7340cf] [320db8651bf5]
21. **Treat your accumulated writing (and any content asset you consider buying) as data whose value depends entirely on your ability to leverage it.** The source's illustration: Micro One reportedly bought Spirit Airlines data for what looked cheap to the seller and valuable to a buyer who could mine it [web check]. The lesson is to write down the specific channels and repurposing workflow you'd use before acquiring or investing in any content asset. [4a3f81e0a69c] [7b9b8ce18fd2]
22. **A distinct point of view is a moat for a product as much as for a content program.** The source's own example: his SaaS product, TypeShare, isn't sold as a generic scheduling-and-writing tool (commodity); it's sold on a specific methodology (the personal-language-model framework). A competitor copying a feature has to first reconstruct the reasoning behind it, which keeps them permanently a step behind. This is Cole's own product and a self-interested claim, not a measured outcome. [687b8507e264]
23. **Branded (company) accounts should repeat commodity positions; personality content belongs to a named human.** "Nobody wants to hear from the branded Twitter page. They want to hear from the CEO" — branded content, per the source, "always underperforms" content from a real person. A brand account can still repeat the commodity ideas its founder has already decided to hold; it just shouldn't carry personal biography. [49c888bb326e]
24. **Expect large companies to build owned media, not just buy ads.** The source cites OpenAI acquiring the podcast TBPN as an example of this shift [web check]; for a smaller brand the equivalent is owning the category's narrative through an original content library rather than only running paid campaigns. [264a1b8b2dd8]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Diagnose why an AI draft reads as "slop"
1. Identify the commodity claim or example the AI used (e.g., a generic PDP or blog line). [cbbb787360b8]
2. Ask: is this a settled debate in the category where two credible positions exist (e.g., "buy heavy pans" vs. "light pans done right work fine")? If yes, the draft failed because AI doesn't know which side you hold, not because it can't write. [cbbb787360b8]
3. Record your position explicitly in the brief or a running "positions" log so editors and AI reuse the same stance instead of defaulting to category consensus. [needs brand data: the category's recurring commodity claims and which side the brand takes] [cbbb787360b8]
4. If you genuinely have no strong opinion on a specific claim, mark it "deliberately neutral" rather than letting the model default silently. [cbbb787360b8]

### 3.2 Convert "You" copy to "I" copy
1. Highlight every sentence in a draft that starts with or implies "you should," "you need to," or "you must." [2b0c662e2dd0]
2. Rewrite as first-person experience or stated opinion: "You should test your products before launch" → "I tested this for a month before ordering." [2b0c662e2dd0]
3. Read the rewritten line next to a competitor's page. If it would still fit there unchanged, it's still commodity — add a specific, personally-true detail. [2b0c662e2dd0]
4. Where a brand (not founder) account is posting, keep it on agreed commodity positions and route "I" content to the founder's or a named person's account instead. [2b0c662e2dd0] [49c888bb326e]

### 3.3 Build the unmade data set / personal language model
1. Set aside recurring time to write about how you (or the brand's founder) became who you are: mistakes, a failed product line, a bad sourcing trip, the reason you started. [33bc4c19a527]
2. Write these into whatever tool the team and AI tools read from — a doc, spreadsheet, or second-brain tool like Obsidian; the source says the storage location doesn't matter, only that AI can access it. [0d61d18719cc] [33bc4c19a527]
3. Label each entry so it can be reused rather than re-told from scratch; once you've said a story well once or twice, treat it as "approved" and let AI redeploy it across channels. [33bc4c19a527] [0d61d18719cc]
4. Where the standard category claim is generic ("we care about quality"), drop the specific story into that exact slot in the copy instead. [33bc4c19a527] [needs brand data: the founder's/team's actual formative stories, failures, sourcing history]

### 3.4 Decide the human/AI division of labor before automating any content workflow
1. Draw the line explicitly: human work = net-new thinking, first articulation of an idea, taking a stance on a commodity debate, specific customer or founder stories. AI work = remixing sentences/passages you already wrote, cross-posting across channels, templating. [ad4c55b5d306] [4392aa839fe7]
2. Before automating anything, produce the source material first (the library from 3.3). [ad4c55b5d306]
3. Assign AI the repeating: slicing a long piece into shorts, generating automated video cuts, producing volume across formats from existing approved language. [4392aa839fe7]
4. Review AI-remixed output for voice and factual accuracy before it reaches customers — AI is repeating, not verifying. [4392aa839fe7]
5. As the library grows, ask of every AI-drafted piece: did this add net-new thinking, or just another arrangement of what already exists? Track this so the library keeps growing rather than just cycling. [ad4c55b5d306]

### 3.5 Fix a draft that "doesn't sound like me" (or the brand)
1. Do not discard the whole draft. Isolate which single variable is off: a word choice, sentence length/rhythm, or an opinion-vs-citation stance mismatch. [612587c6befe]
2. Check whether the person or brand actually favors facts/citations or personal opinion as their natural mode, and whether the draft matches that. [612587c6befe]
3. Make the smallest edit that restores the mismatched variable (Cole's example: adding 2–3 citation phrases, about 1% of the piece) rather than rewriting from scratch. [612587c6befe]
4. Record the confirmed variables (preferred adjectives, sentence rhythm, citation habit) in a short brand voice guide so any writer or AI tool can match them without re-deriving them each time. [612587c6befe]

### 3.6 Label content timely vs. timeless before publishing, and plan accordingly
1. Before making a piece, decide: does it react to a launch, holiday, or trend (timely), or is it a durable how-to/story/essay (timeless)? [3014faaee9da]
2. If timely, expect payoff in hours to days and plan to retire/archive it after the window rather than resurfacing it later (e.g., a Black Friday countdown post). [3014faaee9da]
3. If timeless, don't measure it against a timely piece's short-term spike; expect it to be reused and repurposed for years (e.g., a "how to choose your first [product]" piece). [3014faaee9da]
4. Watch for a single word or date reference (e.g., "in 2026," "just launched") turning an otherwise-timeless piece timely — remove it if you want the piece to compound. [3014faaee9da]

### 3.7 Weigh short/medium/long-term value before deciding what to spend time on
1. Before creating a piece, ask: what does this get me in the next hour? The next month? The next years? [1193451bea64]
2. If the honest answer is "a slightly different phrasing of something I've already said," treat it as a fast, low-effort commodity piece (still useful "approved language," but not worth a large time investment). [1193451bea64]
3. If the piece crystallizes a genuinely new point of view you haven't published before, budget real time for it (the source's own example: about two weeks for an essay he expects to reuse for roughly a decade). [1193451bea64]
4. Use these three questions to justify time investment to whoever controls the content budget, instead of assuming every piece should return equally. [1193451bea64]

### 3.8 Set and test a volume floor
1. Pick an explicit output floor calibrated to your team size and channel capacity — the source's own stated floor for his agency is a minimum of five pieces per day, described as the level needed to give content "a fair shot." [7d684acdc24b]
2. Build the systems (scheduling, repurposing, AI-assisted production) to hit that floor reliably before optimizing anything else. [7d684acdc24b] [ed26cf9e8b40]
3. Once the floor is reliably hit, shift attention to the harder question: where are the ideas (input) coming from? Route back to 3.3/3.4 rather than tactics. [ed26cf9e8b40] [3335c7ab8443]
4. Deprioritize posting-time optimization, retweet-buying, or virality-chasing until the library and the volume floor are both in place. [3335c7ab8443]

### 3.9 Set up a company brain
1. Identify one or two people (in-house or contracted) whose only job is to think deeply about the category and produce original long-form content — not promotional copy. [bda11c7340cf] [320db8651bf5]
2. Position that long-form output as the input layer that feeds everything downstream: short-form social, automated video cuts, sales-enablement material, product-education content. [bda11c7340cf]
3. Do not judge this library by a single post's direct sales attribution; the source's stated payoff is long-horizon brand association with original thinking. [bda11c7340cf] [320db8651bf5]
4. Before committing budget, confirm the operational infrastructure exists to remix and distribute the library (see 3.8) — the source says content without that machinery is a wasted investment. [bda11c7340cf] [4a3f81e0a69c]
5. For ecommerce specifically: route the library's output into product education, buying-guide copy, email streams, PDP nuance and acquisition creative so one deep piece spawns many store assets. [bda11c7340cf]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Treating "AI slop" as a technology failure when it's really a missing-input (missing stated perspective) failure. [cbbb787360b8]
- Writing "you should" copy from a place of "I know everything, therefore you should" — Cole says he actively tries to avoid this frame. [2b0c662e2dd0]
- Letting AI do net-new thinking or first articulation instead of only repeating already-approved language. [ad4c55b5d306] [4392aa839fe7]
- Repeating the same commodity idea "for the 999th time" without noticing you've stopped thinking and are only repeating. [bdf52e0f0da8]
- Believing "I lead an uninteresting life" as a reason not to build the unmade data set — Cole calls this simply incorrect. [33bc4c19a527]
- Assuming credibility alone wins distribution and skipping personal detail. [49c888bb326e]
- Spreading across too many topics ("writing about everything") instead of a narrow, repeated set — you never go deep enough to build association. [712445a708f1]
- Discarding an entire AI or ghostwritten draft because one variable (a word choice, a missing citation) is off, instead of fixing just that variable. [612587c6befe]
- Confusing "I don't like my voice" (a practice problem) with "I'm afraid to publish" (a fear problem) and using the first as an excuse to stall. [8b3238183f67] [910b7d45cd6e]
- Adding a dated or "just happened" reference to content meant to be timeless, which kills its long-term reuse value. [3014faaee9da]
- Treating every piece of content as equally valuable instead of weighing its short/medium/long-term contribution to the library. [1193451bea64]
- Chasing micro-tactics (exact posting time, buying retweets, virality-chasing) before the content library and production infrastructure exist. [3335c7ab8443] [7d684acdc24b]
- Building volume/posting infrastructure without fixing the quality of the input feeding it — this only produces more generic content, faster. [ed26cf9e8b40] [4392aa839fe7]
- Acquiring a content asset (a writer's catalog, a data set) without the operational infrastructure to mine it — the source calls this "a horrible decision." [4a3f81e0a69c] [7b9b8ce18fd2]
- Making a branded/company account personal (posting a specific personal detail from a faceless handle) instead of keeping personality content on a named human's account. [49c888bb326e]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Internal tensions**
- Cole says AI should "never" do net-new writing or thinking [ad4c55b5d306], but he also says he's "not against" a non-writer who brain-dumps into an LLM and lets it draft in its own voice, calling that "no different" from hiring a human ghostwriter [8b3238183f67]. The source does not say when the strict repeat-only rule applies versus when full LLM drafting from a brain-dump is acceptable.
- Voice is framed as a small, almost incidental part of a piece ("roughly 80%" commodity word-groupings with small swaps) [612587c6befe], yet Cole also reports using essentially one authoritative voice successfully across "north of 300" ghostwriting clients — which cuts against treating voice differentiation as the make-or-break variable the chapter otherwise implies.
- The "you should" framing is criticized as commodity and mildly patronizing [2b0c662e2dd0], but the source does not distinguish this from direct, reader-benefit "you" copy ("you'll get X"), which is standard and effective in ecommerce writing; a team could over-apply this rule and strip out clear benefit-focused copy that isn't actually the "prescriptive you" Cole means.

**Gaps versus the card set**

**Missing from the source entirely**

**Conflicts with common ecommerce advice**
- Common ecommerce copy guidance leans on direct, reader-benefit "you" statements ("you'll love how this fits") for clarity and conversion; this source frames most "you" phrasing as commodity and mildly patronizing and pushes toward first-person founder narrative instead. The two aren't fully opposed (prescriptive "you should" differs from benefit-focused "you"), but applying the rule literally risks stripping out clear, converting benefit copy.
- The source treats credibility as secondary to personality and story [49c888bb326e], which sits against typical ecommerce trust-building advice (reviews, certifications, guarantees, expert endorsement) that leans on credibility signals to reduce purchase risk — the source doesn't address regulated or high-consideration categories where credibility likely carries more weight.
