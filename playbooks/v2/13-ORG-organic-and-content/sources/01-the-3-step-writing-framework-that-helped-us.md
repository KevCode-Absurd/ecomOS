## 1. What this source is for

It gives a personal, repeatable writing process (warm-up, draft, incubation, edit) plus a set of tactical rules for making any piece of copy, story or content more persuasive and easier to finish: what to decide before writing, how to open, how to write plain and rhythmic sentences, and how to choose what to write about. It is a craft and process framework for a solo writer or a small content team, not a testing or measurement framework, and it says nothing about SEO, paid distribution mechanics, or data-driven iteration.

## 2. Principles

1. **Writing is persuasion at scale, not self-expression.** Condition: any copy meant to move a reader to act (product page, email, ad, blog post). Evidence: the presenter's own framing, illustrated by his personal shift from one-to-one hot-dog sales to writing that "scales to an infinite amount of people." [1629164d5fc0]
2. **Decide the reaction, the action and the one-line takeaway before writing a word.** Condition: any substantial piece (product page, landing page, welcome email, campaign). Evidence: borrowed from Chris Quigley's viral-video agency, which picked an emotion before writing a script; the source's own three questions are "what's the reaction," "what's the action," "what's the one line they'd remember." [5286ee7f0f32]
3. **Writing forces clear thinking; bad writing usually means unclear thinking, not bad style.** Condition: diagnosing why a page, email or pitch is not landing. Evidence: presenter's claim that "I'm not a good writer" is usually code for "I'm not a good thinker," reinforced by Amazon's Jeff Bezos banning PowerPoint in favor of long-form memos because bad thinking has nowhere to hide in prose. Also cites Paul Graham's idea that writing generates new ideas, not just communicates existing ones. [4f38d9d987ed]
4. **Unlearn school writing habits for internet writing.** Condition: any consumer-facing internet copy. Rule: drop minimum word counts, drop fancy vocabulary, drop the formal outline. Evidence: presenter's direct contrast between what school rewards (length, complex vocabulary, five-paragraph structure) and what performs online (concision, plain words, a one-line thesis in place of an outline). A social post's ~180-character subheadline can serve as that thesis. [797d8a7279aa]
5. **Warm up with "copywork" before drafting your own copy.** Condition: any high-stakes piece where you feel blocked or stale. Method: hand-copy (or type, as a fallback) a piece of writing you admire, word for word, without improving it, for about 10-15 minutes as a warm-up; keep different admired pieces for different tones (persuasive vs. serious business, etc.). Evidence: presenter's own multi-year habit, originally practiced 2 hours a day for 8 months to learn to write; still used today in short form before important pieces. [c807997f7db2] [ea8652fd50e8]
6. **Draft fast and badly on purpose.** Condition: the drafting stage of any piece. Rule: bang out a "dump draft" without self-censorship, accept that it will sound bad, and don't show anyone. The presenter states this is where most people get stuck because they are afraid to look stupid; the fix is to expect and accept the bad draft, because editing (not drafting) is where quality is made. [8c85d056f475]
7. **Give the draft an incubation gap before editing.** Condition: after any first draft, before editing. Rule: step away and do something passive (walk, shower, cook, sit) for at least about 90 minutes; the source says this sometimes stretches to 6 hours, a day or two, or even a week. Evidence: framed as "passive thinking" or "shower thoughts," attributed loosely to psychological research the presenter does not name precisely. [8c85d056f475]
8. **Editing is where the value is created, so shift time toward it.** Condition: after incubation. Rule: come back and edit hard rather than trying to perfect the draft up front; a short bad draft followed by high-energy editing beats a long, fatiguing "good" draft. Evidence: quotes David Ogilvy ("I'm a lousy writer but a good editor") and Stephen King's phrase "killing your darlings." [8c85d056f475]
9. **Target a 5th-8th grade reading level; you can go as low as 4th.** Condition: consumer-facing copy (product pages, emails, landing pages). Method: check with an automated readability tool; the source names the Hemingway app [verify UI]. Cut adverbs, jargon and thesaurus words; vary sentence length for rhythm. Evidence: reference points given are USA Today at about a 4th grade level and the New York Times at about a 7th grade level; the source's own analysis of Warren Buffett's shareholder letters (1974-2013) found words-per-sentence dropping from 17 to 13 and reading level dropping from 10th grade to 4th grade as his writing improved despite the subject (insurance) getting more complex. [d8fbcddae70a] [a3cac5e01c5a]
10. **Don't bury the lead; punch with the first sentence.** Condition: any opener (landing page, founder story, email) where the reader decides in seconds whether to continue. Rule: cut intro, context and background; move the thesis, promise or most provocative statement to the very top. Evidence: the Soylent blog opener ("I spent the last 30 days eating nothing but Soylent... why would I do something so stupid? I'll explain.") is given as a model; the source also claims 80-90% of readers only ever read the headline, stated as the presenters' estimate, not measured data. [26bc815a7b84]
11. **Use curiosity gaps, but always pay them off soon and honestly.** Condition: subject lines, hooks, page openers. Rule: withhold just enough to make the reader need the next sentence, then deliver the payoff soon, not much later in the piece. The source's own line for the ethics of this: "it ain't bait if you deliver on the promise." [0980397e62b4]
12. **Test copy by speaking it out loud first.** Condition: reviewing a team member's draft, or writing your own product explanation. Method: ask the writer to explain the idea out loud in plain language instead of reading their draft; the resulting one-sentence spoken version often becomes the first line, or all the copy that's needed. If you can't say it plainly, the underlying thinking isn't clear yet, so clarify that before rewriting the copy. The source explicitly argues informal language is not the opposite of professional. [4f3116aa30c6]
13. **Build categorized swipe files before you need them.** Condition: everyday content production. Method: capture any phrase, opener, closer or headline that lands emotionally the moment you notice it (notes app, file); keep separate files by use case (the source keeps separate opener and closer files because those were his hardest sections to write); pull from diverse sources (podcasters, YouTubers, competitor and non-competitor site copy); before writing, pull 2-3 relevant entries and riff on their structure. [6ff9c1f9fc32]
14. **Filter topics with a 60-second voice-memo test.** Condition: choosing what to write about (product stories, campaign angles, blog topics). Method: record yourself telling a friend about the topic informally ("hey, did you hear what happened..."); if it wouldn't naturally come up in that conversation, don't write about it; the natural spoken structure can become the first draft, transcribed and lightly edited. Evidence: used at Milk Road to fix new writers who were producing boring, over-fluffed drafts. [16e4989b065a]
15. **Write to one person, not a crowd.** Condition: drafting newsletters, product descriptions or landing pages meant for a broad audience, when the writing starts to sound corporate. Method: pick one specific real or composite person and write only to them; let it be a coincidence that many others read it. Evidence: at The Hustle (sold with close to 2 million subscribers), instructing new writers to "write to Sam" (one person) immediately improved their output versus writing "to millions." [9783e7f32959]
16. **Vary sentence length for rhythm.** Condition: any copy that reads flat or monotonous on review. Rule: default to short sentences; the source's own line is "anytime you want to use a comma, use a period" (then start the next sentence with "and" or "but" if needed); after a run of short sentences, insert a medium one, then occasionally a long one once the reader is "rested," to build energy. Use imagined reader-internal-dialogue lines ("you're asking yourself right now...") as pacing devices. [056882fe4e22]
17. **Frame brand and founder stories with intention and obstacle.** Condition: About pages, founder origin stories, narrative product pages, story-driven ad creative. Rule: name what the character (founder, customer, brand) wants (intention) and what stands in the way (obstacle); attributed to screenwriter Aaron Sorkin ("I worship at the altar of intention and obstacle"). Test: you should be able to pause at any point in the story and state both; if you can't, the reader has already lost the thread. Reveal both fast; don't bury them. [b5c0734ef3bc] [b372918f62e6]
18. **High stakes can come from a low-stakes setting.** Condition: brand or founder content when the product or event itself lacks obvious drama. Rule: pick a genuinely small moment (an HOA dispute, a shipping mishap, a packaging argument) and render the narrator's emotional stake honestly; the goal is to get the reader to believe the stakes felt real to the character, not that they were objectively large. Cites a driveway-workout HOA dispute and a Larry David/"Curb Your Enthusiasm" example (wearing a MAGA hat specifically so strangers won't talk to him) as models. [9affec3cf159]
19. **A first-person "letter from the CEO" can work as a sales-page format.** Condition: top-of-page copy for a landing page, especially subscription or considered-purchase products. Method: write as a direct first-person letter from the founder, open with a contrarian reframing of the category (not the product) - the source's example is Hey.com: "Email gets a bad rap, but it shouldn't. Email is a treasure." - then explain the problem and product in plain, short sentences. Argued rationale: a real person's claim raises personal stakes and reads as more credible than generic marketing copy; this is presented as a format to test, not a proven lift, and it must be genuinely attributable to the named person. [32e6539348a0]
20. **Stories with a strong emotional hook (especially outrage/WTF) can earn free distribution.** Condition: sourcing or writing brand editorial content you want a third party to amplify. Method: write the story as genuine editorial, not as an ad, driven by the emotion first rather than a product feature; let the visible reason the story matters to you show. Evidence: the Hint Water founder story (headline paraphrased by the source as "sweetie, help this entrepreneur create a multi-million dollar business," from a Coca-Cola executive dismissing her); Hint later discovered the piece and spent millions promoting it. [0dcc74caac04]
21. **Study comedians for timing, tension and self-aware crisis response.** Condition: brand storytelling, apology or crisis statements, and acquisition creative. Method: study pacing and callbacks (source names Dave Chappelle and Shane Gillis) for holding attention; study self-deprecating ownership of a mistake instead of denial or spin (source's example: Louis CK's post-cancellation return, "everybody's got a thing"); study story structure built so the audience reaches one conclusion on its own (source's example: Chappelle's "Unforgiven" segment, described as "a maze that led to one door"). [2a2b22a57381]
22. **Mine niche, obsessive practitioner writing (e.g., Hacker News) for content angles.** Condition: your category's normal marketing content is saturated. Method: monitor aggregator sites for high-ranking posts by non-marketers writing in obsessive detail about a narrow topic; use the "I know nothing about X, but I do know about Y" opening pattern to frame an outsider's angle as an asset. Source's example: a roughly 10,000-word teardown of the Kroger grocery app's UX ranked #15 on Hacker News, opening with "I know nothing about the grocery industry, but what I do know about is tech and UX." [d6daae75f48f]
23. **Write from genuine personal interest to build a "personal monopoly."** Condition: defining a store's or founder's content voice and picking which topics to own. Rule: write about what you are most genuinely interested in or knowledgeable about, even if you suspect few people care; consistency finds the audience that does care, and nobody can compete with you on the thing you are most specifically obsessed with. Evidence: the podcast itself only worked, per the source, once it stopped emulating other formats (Tim Ferriss, How I Built This) and let the host talk about whatever he genuinely found interesting, including niche topics. [4adfe40984ba]
24. **Copywork is a structured practice loop, not a one-off trick.** Condition: training yourself or a team before a batch of store content or ad copy. Method: study a principle, see an example done well, try it yourself, repeat with a new example; the source argues repeated practice, not more theory, is what builds skill. [ea8652fd50e8]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Before-writing setup (do this before typing a word)
1. Decide the reaction you want the reader to feel - an emotion (the source lists examples such as awe, outrage/WTF, heartwarming, curiosity). [5286ee7f0f32]
2. Decide the action you want the reader to take (click buy, share, subscribe, forward, sign up). [5286ee7f0f32]
3. Write down the single sentence or line you want remembered if the reader forgets everything else. Use this sentence in place of a formal outline. [5286ee7f0f32] [797d8a7279aa]
4. If working from a headline/subheadline format (e.g., a social post), draft the subheadline (roughly 180 characters) as the stable thesis; the headline itself may be rewritten many times, but the subheadline should stay put and guide the draft. [797d8a7279aa]
5. Pull 2-3 relevant entries from your swipe file (openers, closers, phrases) that fit the tone of this piece. [6ff9c1f9fc32]
6. Warm up with 10-15 minutes of copywork: hand-copy (or type) a piece of admired writing that matches the tone you want (persuasive, serious/business, rhythmic, etc.), word for word, without improving it. [c807997f7db2] [ea8652fd50e8]

### 3.2 The four-step writing process
1. **Copywork** - warm up per 3.1 step 6. [8c85d056f475]
2. **Draft** - write a fast, bad "dump draft" with no self-censorship; do not show anyone yet. [8c85d056f475]
3. **Incubate** - step away and do something passive (walk, shower, cook) for at least about 90 minutes; longer gaps (up to 6 hours, a day or two, even a week) are acceptable and sometimes used by the source. [8c85d056f475]
4. **Edit** - return and edit hard; this is where quality is actually created, so budget more time here than in drafting. [8c85d056f475]

### 3.3 Opening-line / curiosity-gap test
1. Write the opener, aiming to state the thesis, promise or most provocative claim first, cutting intro and background. [26bc815a7b84]
2. Cover everything except the first two sentences. Ask: would an indifferent reader (who doesn't know or care about you) need to read the next sentence? [26bc815a7b84] [0980397e62b4]
3. If yes, reveal the next sentence and repeat the same question. Continue through the third or fourth sentence. [26bc815a7b84]
4. If no at any point, rewrite that sentence to create the gap. [26bc815a7b84]
5. Once the reader is invested (about 3-4 sentences in), you can let the piece breathe and add background/context. [26bc815a7b84]
6. Make sure whatever gap you opened gets paid off soon in the piece, and that the payoff is true, not exaggerated - the source's line: "it ain't bait if you deliver on the promise." [0980397e62b4]

### 3.4 Readability edit pass
1. Run the draft through a readability checker; the source names the Hemingway app [verify UI]. [d8fbcddae70a] [a3cac5e01c5a]
2. Target a 5th-8th grade reading level (as low as 4th grade is acceptable per the source). [a3cac5e01c5a] [needs brand data: whether the brand's audience or category needs more technical/precise language, e.g. regulated claims]
3. Remove adverbs and jargon; flag words a reader would only encounter on LinkedIn (source's example: "utilize") and replace with plain speech. [d8fbcddae70a]
4. Apply the thesaurus test: if you had to look up a word to use it, it is the wrong word (attributed to Stephen King). [d8fbcddae70a]
5. Read the sentence aloud; if you would not say it that way to a person, rewrite it in spoken form. [d8fbcddae70a] [4f3116aa30c6]
6. Vary sentence length: default short, insert a medium sentence, and occasionally a long one once a run of short sentences has "rested" the reader; use imagined reader-internal-dialogue lines to break up rhythm where useful. [056882fe4e22]

### 3.5 Topic-selection filter (what to write about)
1. Record a 60-second voice memo as if telling a friend about the potential topic informally. [16e4989b065a]
2. If the topic would not naturally come up in that conversation, drop it. [16e4989b065a]
3. If it passes, transcribe the memo and lightly edit it into the draft's structure and tone, rather than starting from a blank formal draft. [16e4989b065a]
4. Alternatively, or additionally, check the topic against your own genuine interest/expertise ("personal monopoly" filter): is this the thing you are most specifically informed or opinionated about in your category? [4adfe40984ba]

### 3.6 Building and using a swipe file
1. Whenever a phrase, opener, closer or headline lands emotionally for you, capture the exact wording immediately in a notes app or file. [6ff9c1f9fc32]
2. Sort entries into categories by use case: phrases, headlines, connecting words, openers, closers. [6ff9c1f9fc32]
3. Source from a wide range: podcasters, YouTubers, competitor and non-competitor site copy, comedians, engineering blog posts. [6ff9c1f9fc32] [d6daae75f48f]
4. Before writing, pull 2-3 applicable entries and riff on their structure - do not paste swiped competitor wording directly into customer-facing copy (legal/brand risk). [6ff9c1f9fc32]

### 3.7 Building a founder/brand story with intention and obstacle
1. Name the central character (founder, customer, brand) and their intention: what do they want. [b5c0734ef3bc] [b372918f62e6]
2. Name the specific obstacle in the way (a rejection, a competitor, a skeptical gatekeeper, a supply constraint). [b5c0734ef3bc] [b372918f62e6]
3. Decide the stakes: they do not need to be life-or-death; a low-stakes situation rendered with honest emotional weight works (source's HOA-dispute and Larry David examples). [9affec3cf159]
4. Open with the provocative moment or the intention/obstacle stated plainly - do not bury it in background (reuse procedure 3.3). [b5c0734ef3bc]
5. Test the draft: pause at any point and ask "what does this character want, and what's in their way?" If you can't answer immediately, rewrite so the answer is always available. [b372918f62e6]
6. Let the founder's own voice and actual language come through rather than a polished corporate register. [b5c0734ef3bc]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Readability check.** How: paste the draft into a readability tool (Hemingway app named) [verify UI]. Good: scores 5th-8th grade (4th acceptable). Bad: scores above 8th grade, or is full of adverbs/jargon. Fix: run procedure 3.4. [d8fbcddae70a] [a3cac5e01c5a]
- **Curiosity-gap / opener check.** How: cover all but the first two sentences and ask whether an indifferent reader needs to read on. Good: yes, and the gap pays off soon after. Bad: no, or the payoff never lands (bait without follow-through). Fix: rewrite the opener per procedure 3.3; recheck the promise is honestly kept. [26bc815a7b84] [0980397e62b4]
- **Speaking-it-out-loud check.** How: explain the idea to someone verbally instead of having them read the draft. Good: you can say it in one clear sentence; use that sentence as the copy's first line. Bad: you struggle to explain it plainly. Fix: clarify the underlying idea before rewriting the copy - the writing problem is a thinking problem. [4f3116aa30c6] [4f38d9d987ed]
- **Voice-memo topic check.** How: record a 60-second informal memo about the topic as if telling a friend. Good: it's something you'd naturally bring up unprompted. Bad: it feels forced or you wouldn't mention it socially. Fix: drop the topic or find the angle that would make you want to tell a friend. [16e4989b065a]
- **Intention-and-obstacle check.** How: pause at any point in a brand/founder story and ask what the character wants and what's in the way. Good: you can answer instantly. Bad: you can't name both. Fix: add or foreground the missing piece; move it earlier if it appears too late. [b372918f62e6] [b5c0734ef3bc]
- **Jargon/thesaurus check.** How: scan for words you had to look up, or words people don't say aloud (source's flagged example: "utilize," called out as a LinkedIn tell). Good: every word is one you'd use in conversation. Bad: any word needed a thesaurus or reads as corporate. Fix: replace with the plain-speech equivalent. [d8fbcddae70a]
- **Tossed-salad-style topic check (implicit in "one takeaway").** How: after drafting, ask what the one sentence a reader would remember is. Good: there's one clear answer. Bad: multiple competing takeaways. Fix: cut supporting material that doesn't serve the single takeaway. [5286ee7f0f32]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Starting with intro, context or background fluff instead of punching with the most important sentence first (burying the lead). [26bc815a7b84]
- Drafting and editing in the same session with no incubation gap, which the source says was its own earlier mistake and produced worse results. [8c85d056f475]
- Trying to write a "good" draft on the first pass instead of a fast, bad one - causes fatigue, self-doubt and avoidance ("I hate this, is this even good"). [8c85d056f475]
