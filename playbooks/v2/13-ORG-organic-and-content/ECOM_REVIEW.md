# Ecommerce review: 13-ORG-organic-and-content

## 1. Score and rule mix

Score: 2 out of 5.

The playbook is honest, well sourced and clearly organized, but almost all of it is generic short-form content and copywriting advice that would read the same for a coach, a SaaS founder or a solo creator. Store-specific framing shows up in a handful of rules (funnel stage into a PDP or post-purchase flow, price-ladder CTA routing, sales-tied measurement) but it is scattered, not the spine of the document, so an agent using this playbook would rank and phrase content almost the same way for a store as for a personal brand. The parts that are store-specific are good, which is why this is a 2 and not a 1.

Section 3 has 174 numbered decision rules (R1 to R174). Classification:

- (a) store-specific: 15 rules (9%). Examples: R65 (specs move to the PDP), R76 (sourcing trips), R78 (founder letter into post-purchase flow), R95 (sales plateau diagnosis), R124 (DM follow-up on clicked-not-bought), R126 (size guide as next step), R127 (low-ticket in-app vs high-ticket to a call), R130 (launch and seasonal-sale cadence), R131 (no-call ecommerce routes to email and SMS), R134 (PDP nuance and buying-guide copy), R150 (sales spikes vs post dates), R156 to R157 (value at the product layer, not sponsors), R161 (discount codes as attribution).
- (b) generic but correctly applied to a store: 21 rules (12%). Examples: R7, R9, R10, R24, R28, R36, R44, R62, R66, R69, R71, R73, R87, R93, R112, R118, R122, R128, R140, R151, R172.
- (c) generic with no store framing: 138 rules (79%). This is most of hook craft (3.3), writing process (3.4), AI voice (3.5), algorithm claims (3.8), platform setup (3.9), repurposing (3.11), teardown (3.12) and most of measurement (3.13).
- (d) out of scope for a store, as standalone rules: 0. No rule is written only for B2B, SaaS or a publisher. But 3 rules carry an out-of-scope example inside an otherwise generic rule: R82 (a B2B software company's lead case), R160 (OpenAI buying a podcast), R162 (a SaaS sold on its own methodology). These examples should not be handed to a store operator as evidence.

## 2. Prioritization check

No priority block exists. Section 0's routing table says which sections to load for which task, but nothing ranks levers by impact for a store, and nothing branches by store type (hero SKU vs catalogue, low vs high AOV, subscription vs one-off, luxury or considered purchase, mobile-first India vs US). Draft below.

### 3.0 Ecommerce priority order

1. Fix the funnel: every post maps to top, mid or bottom funnel and reuses across the PDP, email and DM (R10, R131, R134). A missing funnel tag is the most common way a store account gets views with no sales.
2. Set CTA and price-ladder routing before scaling reach: low-ticket in-app, high-ticket or considered purchase to a call, capacity confirmed first (R24, R127, R118, C4). Ranks above cadence for luxury or considered-purchase brands.
3. Get the hook and first frame right; it gates everything downstream (R25, R26, R29), so it outranks writing-process polish.
4. Move specs to the PDP, lead with story or demo (R65). For a wide catalogue, pick 1 hero SKU per post rather than a generic "our products" post. `[general practice]`
5. Set the floor and pillar mix by catalogue size and capacity: a hero-SKU brand sustains fewer, deeper pillars than a wide catalogue (R2, R3, R13, R14). The catalogue-size branch is `[general practice]`.
6. Pick the hero platform by where the store's actual customers are, checked with the sample in P22 step 2, not by presenter preference; a mobile-first India audience and a US audience can answer differently (R110, P22). `[general practice]` for the geography branch.
7. Build the comment-to-DM and lead-magnet layer once the account has traction, tied to a lead magnet the store can fulfil (R119, R121, R122, R24).
8. Run the compliance and claims check before publishing, especially for regulated or health-adjacent categories (R163, R167, P21).
9. Sell founder or sourcing story for considered or handmade products; use expert-breakdown or ratings pillars for commodity products (R4, R76, R156). `[general practice]` for the product-type branch.
10. Tie every month's read to sales, not views: spikes vs post dates, discount codes, week-on-week sales (R150, R95).
11. Repurpose winners into paid tests once they move sales; route catalogue-wide creative decisions to 24-DEP and 10-MCR (R132, R150).
12. Layer seasonal sales and launches on continuous lead-magnet content; timing matters more with real inventory and drop dates than for a media account (R130). `[general practice]` for the inventory-timing framing.

## 3. Rule rewrites (condition, sources and card ids unchanged)

1. **R1.** Fill the proof slot with something a stranger can verify, and check it does not contradict the PDP: if the statement says "fastest" or "best," the product page must say the same thing.
2. **R5.** Design every post for a stranger who has never seen the PDP: no product nicknames, no assumed familiarity with SKUs, sizes or the catalogue.
3. **R13.** Set the starting cadence by catalogue shape: a single hero SKU runs 2 pillars at the stated cadence; a wide catalogue needs a rotation so no SKU goes untouched for a month.
4. **R28.** Match the hook and caption reading grade to the PDP and checkout copy, so a viewer who clicks through meets the same voice and grade level.
5. **R30.** Open the gap with the product's before-state; name the problem's stakes, withhold the specific result until the payoff.
6. **R32.** For the outcome hook type, use a real before-and-after tied to the product, backed by a customer or test result from the brand record, never a generic "results" claim.
7. **R57.** Record the brand's side on debates that gate a purchase decision: material vs synthetic, price vs quality, in-house vs sourced, not abstract industry opinions.
8. **R58.** Rewrite prescriptive lines as a first-person product test: "I used this for 30 days before we shipped it," not a general category claim.
9. **R81.** New accounts split effort toward product demo and use-case Reels first; carousels carry spec comparisons and size or fit guides.
10. **R87.** Invest the signature prop or set around the product itself (a wall of stock, a fitting room, a workbench) so the set doubles as merchandising.
11. **R110.** Pick the hero platform by where the store's actual buyers spend time; a mobile-first India audience and a US audience can answer differently.
12. **R118.** Set the CTA-free window and view threshold by price point: a low-AOV store can add a soft CTA sooner than a high-AOV or considered-purchase store.
13. **R138.** Split swipe files by DTC competitor ads and non-competitor ads, so structure borrowed from a bank or a SaaS never applies unchanged to a physical product's PDP.
14. **R146.** Read retention and 3-second watch rate per SKU featured, not just per post, so the read shows which products the content builds demand for.
15. **R156.** Route attention to the product page and cart, not a vague "product layer": every high-performing post gets a matching PDP or collection link ready before it goes live.

## 6. Deliverables check (section 9)

Of the 11 deliverables, most are things a content operator would actually build (a monthly calendar, a hooks bank, scripts, a comment-to-DM funnel spec), which is good. But none is framed around the store's own catalogue, PDP or offer ladder. Proposed changes:

- **9.1 Monthly calendar**: add a "SKU or collection featured" column so the calendar ties to the catalogue, not just pillar and format.
- **9.9 Account foundations document**: add the price ladder and catalogue shape, since both drive the priority order in section 3.0.
- **9.8 Monthly performance read**: add sales by SKU or collection, not only total sales.
- No new deliverable for an "offer ladder" or "creative test plan": those belong to 03-OFF and 09-CRO, which this folder should reference, not duplicate.

## 7. The single highest-value change

Add section 3.0 as drafted above, and thread its catalogue-shape, price-ladder and geography conditions into P1, P3 and P22 as required inputs, not optional notes. The playbook's craft advice (hooks, writing, AI, algorithm claims) is strong, but an agent running it would build the same calendar and the same hooks for a mobile-first Indian jewellery brand with 1 hero collection and a US considered-purchase brand with 200 SKUs, because nothing forces a branch on catalogue size, price point or market before the craft rules apply. A short, mandatory priority and branching layer at the top of section 3 would turn a well-written general content course into a playbook that behaves differently for different kinds of stores, which a generic prompt cannot do.
