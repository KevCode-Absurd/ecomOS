## 1. What this source is for

An end-to-end operator workflow for planning and shipping AI-search-optimized pages using an AI assistant (ChatGPT or Claude) as the production engine: keyword research split into transactional and informational (plus GEO-specific "fan-out" queries), competitor analysis, page architecture (money pages vs. content pages), writing content built to be lifted and cited by AI engines (the "content capsule" technique), a fast on-page technical audit, site launch and indexing, internal linking, AI-visibility tracking, and a sequenced approach to backlinks and citations. Built for a local service business; several cards in this source already translate the structural principles to ecommerce category/product pages — this file flags where that translation is the card layer's addition rather than the presenter's own words.

## 2. Principles

1. GEO (generative engine optimization) sits on top of traditional SEO, not instead of it. The presenter's estimate: doing solid classic SEO already delivers roughly 80% of what GEO needs. This is his own heuristic, not a measured figure. Applies when deciding how much net-new "AI-specific" work is required versus fixing SEO fundamentals first. [3fc1358227bbe45b]
2. Two new KPIs sit alongside clicks and conversion: citations (the AI uses your content to build its answer) and mentions (the AI names your brand without necessarily citing you). Track both, and keep purchase conversion as the ultimate commercial measure. [707b3fae30adc4d8]
3. Google does not penalize AI-authored content; it penalizes thin, spammy, low-value content regardless of who or what wrote it. Applies whenever a stakeholder argues for or against AI-assisted copywriting on quality grounds alone. [3fc1358227bbe45b]
4. Which AI model you use (ChatGPT vs. Claude vs. others) is not and never was a ranking factor, per the presenter. Use whichever tool you already pay for. [3fc1358227bbe45b]
5. Keyword-research language should come from the customer, not the internal/expert vocabulary of the business. Example given: an installer calls it "EVSE installation," but the customer searches "Tesla charger installation" — the customer's phrasing is the one with demand. [04b6ed95b92bd4c7]
6. Split keywords into two buckets: transactional (ready-to-buy, e.g. "home EV charger installation") and informational (still researching, e.g. "EV charger installation cost"). Research and route them differently — transactional feeds money pages, informational feeds guides/FAQs. [04b6ed95b92bd4c7]
7. Fan-out queries are the internal sub-questions an AI engine generates and researches before it synthesizes an answer to a broader prompt. Answering those sub-questions inside your content raises the chance of being pulled into the AI's answer. Condition: most useful for broad category/informational prompts, not narrow transactional ones. At the time of recording this data was US/English-only in the tool demonstrated. [22b29e78f3c4a8e5]
8. Build one page per distinct service/offering. The presenter calls this "the number one mistake I see" — cramming every service onto one page "to be efficient" instead of ranking. Condition: only build a dedicated page where keyword research shows independent demand for that variant. [60eade3cd6956138]
9. The "2-second rule" for a commercial page's first fold: within 2 seconds, without scrolling, a visitor must know what you do, where you do it (for local/geo-relevant businesses), and what to do next. Plain, direct headlines beat clever taglines in this zone; save creative copy for further down the page. [71181984bc10c8ee]
10. The "content capsule technique": a short, self-contained answer sits directly under a question heading (H2), written so it makes sense with no surrounding context. Target 20-25 words per capsule (up to 50 words acceptable if the answer genuinely needs it), with roughly 60-70% of a piece written this way and the rest freer prose. Presented as backed by "a peer-reviewed study" (unnamed, unverifiable from this source) plus the presenter's own testing. [50fb70d1a21f5ee1]
11. Cite the source for any factual claim or number by linking a contextual keyword to its origin — this is framed as "just as important as writing good content" for building AI and reader trust. The presenter's counter to "I don't want to link out and lose the visitor": if a reader leaves the moment you cite a source, the content itself wasn't strong enough to hold them — that's a content problem, not a linking problem. [2b192a421dcf206a]
12. Add first-hand experience to content — described as the one thing AI engines can't yet replicate. This does not require years of credentials; a single documented result counts ("we tried X and got Y, so do/don't do Z"). The AI has to be told this detail explicitly in the prompt because it cannot invent it. [2b192a421dcf206a] [50fb70d1a21f5ee1]
13. Internal linking exists for three reasons: easier crawling (for AI and traditional search engines), easier navigation for users, and passing authority deeper into the site (authority dilutes with depth but linking still helps). Link supporting/informational content to the relevant money page wherever it genuinely fits — not on every mention. [bc9c4929973d0a86]
14. Backlinks are the last layer, not a launch requirement. Sequence: build the site correctly, publish content, get indexed, and only pursue links (starting with free local citations and expert-citation platforms, then paid placements) if you still can't outrank competitors. [d84b2be76cec2e08] [3514f3eb2279b461]
15. Link quality beats link quantity — the presenter's own framing: "the more roads pointing to your city... but trust me, it's about the quality of them," not raw count. [d84b2be76cec2e08]
16. There is no fixed target for how fast citations/mentions should grow. Judge success by month-over-month improvement in impressions, clicks, mentions and citations, not against an absolute benchmark. [4df3dc92ffaf5f8c] [311517f2c62e1cb9]
17. AI-generated research lists (keyword suggestions, PAA questions, fan-out queries, clarifying questions) are noisy and must be manually reviewed and trimmed, never accepted in bulk. Stated repeatedly across keyword research, content-brief and drafting steps. [9c1581aeb2a16016] [22b29e78f3c4a8e5] [847055f1a54743a6]
18. Plan the whole site structure before building it. The presenter's analogy: building without a plan is "like trying to make an incredible dish and not really getting your ingredients first." [77a7370ed12c5c7c]
19. A launched site that isn't submitted anywhere is "just wasted pixels" — deployment and Search Console submission are not optional finishing touches. [cb715d72eaf386ef]

## 3. Procedures

**A. Set up the AI workspace before starting any research** [852d326e7cca1b96]
1. Create a dedicated project/workspace in ChatGPT or Claude for the whole SEO effort, instead of one running chat thread. Rationale given: a single chat has a limited context window and will gradually "forget" earlier business detail; a project retains added files. [verify UI: context-window sizes and "project" features change by vendor and plan]
2. Generate a business brief: paste a prepared prompt or your store URL into the AI and answer its follow-up questions about what you sell, who you sell to, and where. Save the output as a text file.
3. Add that brief file as a source/knowledge file inside the project so every later prompt (keyword clustering, page copy, content drafts) inherits correct catalog and positioning context.
4. Add every new research artifact you produce (keyword exports, competitor sitemaps, clusters) into the same project as you go, instead of re-explaining it in chat.
5. [needs brand data: your own catalog, categories, target buyer and fulfillment areas] — the source's brief format is built for a service business; for ecommerce, adapt the brief to capture product catalog and categories, not just "services."

**B. Transactional keyword research and clustering** [d644b9efe0f5088d]
1. Enumerate every product/category/service-equivalent page you intend to have (start from your own catalog, not a generic seed list).
2. Pick a seed transactional term for one category/product (source example: "home EV charger installation") and pull its overview in an SEO tool: monthly search volume, difficulty score, cost-per-click. [verify UI: tool is Data Wise in the demo, presenter's own paid product — an Ahrefs/Semrush/similar equivalent workflow exists]
3. Save the seed term to a planner, then open the tool's "suggestions"/"related keywords" view to surface adjacent variants (secondary/LSI keywords).
4. While scanning, flag brand- or audience-specific variants (e.g. "Tesla charger installation," "EV charger for businesses") that may justify their own sub-page if they carry independent volume.
5. Save every relevant variant to the planner, then build a cluster around the seed term so the tool groups them and totals estimated cluster volume.
6. Export the cluster as CSV and load it into the AI project.
7. Repeat steps 2-6 for every other product/category.

**C. Informational keyword research: People-Also-Ask and fan-out queries** [9c1581aeb2a16016] [22b29e78f3c4a8e5]
1. Take a core transactional term and run a People-Also-Ask (PAA) search in the SEO tool with search depth set high (the demo uses depth 3, which surfaced 143 questions for one term).
2. Expand nested questions (clicking a PAA entry reveals further sub-questions) to build a fuller list.
3. Manually review and select only the questions a real buyer would ask (price, suitability, running cost, DIY-vs-professional, etc.) — do not trust the AI's filtering blindly.
4. Separately, open the tool's fan-out queries view seeded with the same core term (demo returned ~195 queries for one seed). This data was US/English-only at recording time. [web check: current language/region coverage]
5. Filter hard — many fan-out queries will be irrelevant (example given: "B2B electric services" surfaced for a residential EV install query) — keep only ones your products actually answer.
6. Export selected PAA and fan-out questions as CSV and add to the AI project with context.
7. Route each surviving question: short FAQ items (max ~5) onto the relevant commercial page, or full topics/subheadings onto a buying-guide/blog piece. [370b7ebbfabd33c6]
8. Repeat for every main category.

**D. Four-part competitor analysis** [0f0999fffc6578c3]
1. Identify real competitors: search your main commercial term (skip sponsored results), and separately ask an AI assistant who it recommends/cites for that query. Pick 2-5 root domains.
2. Run a domain comparison in an SEO tool for total ranking keywords, traffic estimate, and keyword value; identify the competitor with the most ranking keywords.
3. Open that competitor's ranked-keyword list and inspect which URL types win (demo finding: mostly blog posts plus dedicated location pages) — this tells you the page types you need to compete.
4. Fetch their sitemap at `domain/sitemap.xml`, paste it into the AI project, and ask the AI to categorize it into commercial pages, location/category pages, and blog posts as a CSV — this reveals the structural scale you need to match.
5. Check their backlinks tab: total backlinks, referring domains, link sources (directories, press, associations), and spam score (demo example: 1,100 backlinks, 373 referring domains).
6. Convert findings into your own page-count and content plan; treat backlinking as a later phase, not an immediate requirement.

**E. Build a commercial ("money") page** [71181984bc10c8ee] [ea6702f063e43c68] [24de1308655acd16]
1. Confirm the page matches search intent — a transactional query needs a page where the product can actually be bought or requested, not an article.
2. Assign exactly one primary keyword to the page; work secondary/related terms into subheadings and body copy.
3. Build the first fold to pass the 2-second rule: plain headline stating what you sell (+ where, if geo-relevant), primary CTA button placed above the fold.
4. Add appropriate structured data (service/product schema); validate the output rather than trusting a plugin blindly. [verify UI: current schema plugin behavior]
5. Use real photography over stock where possible, state specific prices, include reviews (ideally ones mentioning that specific product/service), and add a short "how it works" section.
6. Add a short FAQ (no more than ~5 questions) using the most-relevant PAA/fan-out questions from step C — keep informational content off the commercial page beyond this.
7. Check every page against a single H1 rule: one H1 stating the offering exactly; watch platform defaults (source flags Shopify's Dawn theme using the logo as homepage H1 by default — check and correct). [verify UI: theme defaults change by theme and version]
8. Score the page against the full checklist; the source's rule of thumb is that ~90% of checklist items checked means the page is ready.
9. Once one page performs structurally, treat it as a structural template (not a content template) and replicate the framework — never copy-paste the actual copy — across other offerings. [faf7cefdf38d7ad1]

**F. Write a content-capsule buying guide/blog post with AI** [100d6edb5ef37ec4] [c500104bcbec04b5] [847055f1a54743a6] [ba67965b333264e6]
1. Inside the project, paste a content-drafting prompt instructing the content-capsule technique, and supply topic, audience, and goal.
2. Pull the topic from the keyword planner/content map — a PAA or fan-out question can become the main topic, with related questions as subheadings.
3. Describe the audience specifically (demo: "homeowners in Dallas, Texas who just bought an EV") — more detail produces a more useful draft.
4. Let the AI run its research phase; it should surface top-ranking pages and candidate sources to cite (demo skewed to government sources for factual/regulatory claims). Review sources before accepting them.
5. Approve the proposed structure (headings, capsule placements, one or two tables, an FAQ at the end); if the AI proposes too many sub-questions, cap the list to what's actually useful. [847055f1a54743a6]
6. Before requesting the first draft, inject proprietary/first-hand detail the AI cannot know (e.g. a specific operational friction or result).
7. Generate the first draft in an editable canvas/document view if the tool offers one; edit it directly and manually rather than treating it as final. [verify UI: canvas/editing features change by vendor]
8. Check the draft actually used capsule formatting and that cited sources are real, relevant, and the links work.
9. Optionally illustrate a dense passage: copy the passage into a text-to-visual tool (demo uses Napkin, free tier), generate and pick a visual, export, then convert PNG to WEBP before placing it in the page (use once or twice per post, not throughout). [web check: Napkin.ai current features/pricing]
10. Paste into the CMS, fix title duplication/formatting, add internal links to real (not placeholder) pages the AI couldn't know about, and add a meta description under ~150 characters via a quick AI prompt. [a024bd850f132660]

**G. Plan and lock the site structure before building** [77a7370ed12c5c7c]
1. Use the keyword research, competitor analysis and page list already produced as inputs.
2. Run a "build your site structure" prompt inside the same project (works in ChatGPT or Claude) to output a structured sheet: URL, parent/service page, name, title tag, page type, focus.
3. Lay out parent and child pages following a consistent URL pattern (e.g. `services/ev-charger-installation`, then `services/ev-charger-installation/location-name`). Consistency matters more than which element (service or location) comes first in the slug.
4. Review the output with judgment and adjust — do not accept it uncritically.
5. Lock the structure as the build map.

**H. Launch and index** [cb715d72eaf386ef]
1. In Google Search Console, add the new domain as a property and verify ownership. [verify UI: GSC's onboarding flow]
2. Locate your sitemap URL (you can find a competitor's the same way to confirm the expected path/pattern).
3. In GSC's sitemap section, add and submit the sitemap URL.
4. Confirm status reads "success" and check the discovered-page count is proportionate to the number of pages you built (demo example: a throwaway test site discovered 8 pages).
5. Work through a launch checklist on staging before going live, then reuse the same checklist as an ongoing on-site SEO checker after launch.

**I. Quick on-page SEO audit** [1007ba5c92ec738c]
1. Install a free on-page browser extension (demo uses "SEO Wallet"). [web check: tool name/availability]
2. Check the title tag contains the main target keyword.
3. Check meta description length; tighten if slightly too long.
4. Open the headings view and confirm H1/H2/H3 reflect primary and secondary targets.
5. Open the structured-data view and confirm schema matches the page type (e.g. course, FAQPage, Organization in the demo; Product/Offer for ecommerce pages) — schema isn't required on every page but should be on money pages.
6. Check page speed via Chrome DevTools → More tools → Developer tools → Lighthouse tab → Analyze page load; push performance, accessibility, best practices and SEO scores as close to 100 as practical. [verify UI: DevTools menu path]

**J. Internal linking** [bc9c4929973d0a86]
1. Identify money pages and the informational content that supports each one.
2. Add internal links from supporting posts to the relevant money page wherever it genuinely fits (not on every mention).
3. Optionally automate with a plugin such as Link Whisper (WordPress; uses GSC data to suggest links on new posts) or an AI writer tool that handles internal linking when fed a knowledge base (pages, tone of voice, offer details). [web check: current plugin/tool capability]

**K. Track AI visibility** [4df3dc92ffaf5f8c] [311517f2c62e1cb9]
1. In GSC's Performance tab, track total clicks and impressions (impressions typically rise before clicks) and average position across ranking keywords.
2. Expect lower click-through generally as AI answers absorb some queries — treat this as the new baseline, not automatically a fault of your own SEO.
3. Separately check Bing Webmaster Tools' "AI performance" tab for total citations, average pages cited, and the queries you're cited for. [verify UI: Bing Webmaster Tools feature availability/name]
4. Optionally use a paid AI-visibility tool for a performance-by-engine tab, a brand tracker (instant check of whether/what you're cited for), and prompt/keyword tracking — avoid over-building this dashboard.
5. Treat mentions as at least as important as citations; investigate if mentions trend down (the presenter flags his own example of decreasing mentions as something to look into, not something he had already solved).

**L. Sequence backlinks and citations** [3514f3eb2279b461] [f6fee63ce69ebbc4] [d84b2be76cec2e08]
1. Start with free local citations: ask an AI for free business-listing directories in your target location, with business-type detail for a fuller list (demo turned up Next Door Business, Foursquare, Better Business Bureau — "always get on the BBB").
2. Create free accounts wherever allowed, keeping NAP (name, address, phone number) identical everywhere.
3. If managing more than ~3 sites, delegate or use a paid citation service (demo uses Bright Local, ~$3.20/citation, ~$2/citation at bulk). [web check: current pricing]
4. Avoid bulk cheap citation/backlink sellers (source's example red flag: "300 USA local citations... for $5" on Fiverr).
5. If you (or a founder) have subject-matter authority, create an account on a reporter-request platform (demo uses "Connectivity," formerly Featured.com, formerly a HARO-style service), answer questions the platform flags as a good fit, and expect a review → selection → publication pipeline before a citation/backlink appears. [web check: platform name/features — this space renames and consolidates often]
6. Only after the site is built correctly, content is published, and you're indexed and still not competitive: consider paid link placements. Require sponsored/nofollow attributes; get on a call with the provider to confirm what they actually do; assess publisher relevance and real audience rather than taking a vendor's word for it. Presenter names Get Me Links and Rhino Rank as agencies he's used/would consider; states he has not tried every backlink service. [web check: vendor names/pricing/practices]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Brand/competitor gap check.** What: your page/keyword/content coverage vs. 2-5 real competitors. How: domain comparison for ranking keywords → inspect the leader's winning URL types → pull and categorize their sitemap → check their backlink count/quality. Good: you can name the page types and rough page count you need to match. Bad: you have no view of what page types are winning for competitors, or you're guessing page count. Next: convert findings into a page/content plan; hold backlinks for later. [0f0999fffc6578c3]
- **Commercial page readiness check.** What: intent match, one primary keyword, schema present and validated, 2-second-rule first fold, FAQ ≤5 items, real imagery, specific pricing. How: run the checklist item by item. Good: ~90%+ of items checked. Bad: below that, or first fold fails the "what/where/next" test. Next: fix the first fold and CTA before anything else — that's where bounce decisions happen. [ea6702f063e43c68] [71181984bc10c8ee]
- **On-page technical check.** What: title tag, meta description, H1/H2 hierarchy, structured data, page speed. How: browser SEO extension for the first four; Chrome DevTools → Lighthouse for speed. Good: keyword-relevant title/H1, correct schema type validated (not just plugin-assumed), Lighthouse scores near 100. Bad: homepage H1 defaulting to a logo (flagged on Shopify's Dawn theme), unvalidated schema, low Lighthouse scores. Next: re-run this audit after any theme/template/plugin change. [1007ba5c92ec738c] [24de1308655acd16]
- **Indexing check.** What: whether the launched site is actually discoverable. How: submit sitemap in GSC, check status. Good: status "success" with a discovered-page count proportionate to your built page count. Bad: low or zero discovered pages relative to what you built. Next: re-check sitemap validity and internal linking. [cb715d72eaf386ef]
- **AI visibility check.** What: clicks, impressions, average position (GSC); citations, average cited pages, cited queries (Bing AI performance tab); mentions and citations by engine (optional paid tool). How: read these panels monthly. Good: impressions rising (usually first), citations/mentions trending up month over month. Bad: mentions trending down (presenter's own flagged example) — investigate rather than assume; also don't assume every GSC movement is AI-caused. Next: no fixed target exists — judge direction, not an absolute number. [4df3dc92ffaf5f8c] [311517f2c62e1cb9]
- **NAP consistency check.** What: name, address, phone number across every local directory listing. How: review each listing before and after adding new citations. Good: identical NAP everywhere. Bad: any variant — this is the main risk the source flags for local citation work. Next: correct before scaling to more directories. [3514f3eb2279b461]

## 7. Examples and case results

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Domain mismatch.** The entire demonstrated workflow uses a local service business (EV charger installation) as its running example, not an ecommerce store. The card layer explicitly adapts the page-architecture principle to "distinct products and useful collections" [60eade3cd6956138], but the source itself never discusses SKU-level catalogs, product variants, collection pagination, faceted-navigation SEO, or canonical handling for filtered URLs — these are gaps in this source, not something it addressed and got wrong.
- **Vendor self-promotion.** The presenter built and sells the primary tool used throughout the demo (Data Wise) and separately sells paid community access. Treat every claim about that tool's output (e.g. cluster volumes, fan-out counts) as a vendor demo, and verify workflow equivalents exist in tools you actually use (Ahrefs, Semrush, etc.) before committing to this exact toolchain.
- **Unverified study claim.** The content-capsule technique is said to be "part of a study, a peer-review study" — the study is not named, linked, or otherwise identifiable from this transcript. Its numeric parameters (20-25 words, 60-70% capsule share) should be treated as the presenter's working heuristics until that study can be located and checked. [50fb70d1a21f5ee1]
- **No fixed targets given.** Several rules explicitly have no numeric target: how many pages a catalog needs (demand-driven only), how fast AI mentions/citations should grow (judge trend, not a benchmark), and how many clarifying questions is "too many" from an AI draft. Do not manufacture a number where the source gave none.
- **Card addition beyond the transcript.** Card d84b2be76cec2e08 ("Evaluate sponsored placements by buyer relevance and referral value") instructs requiring "sponsored or nofollow link attributes" on paid links. The transcript itself does not say this — the presenter's actual words are that buying backlinks is an open secret in SEO and that "Google states they don't condone purchasing backlinks, but... it wouldn't be a multi-million dollar industry if it didn't work." The nofollow/sponsored-attribute requirement appears to be an editorial safety addition in the card layer, not the source's own instruction. Flag this gap between card and source when using that card as an authority.
- **Conflict with conservative ecommerce SEO guidance.** The source normalizes paying for backlinks ("everybody buys back links, especially in competitive niches") as a practical, if against-guidelines, tactic once organic and on-page work has been exhausted. This runs counter to more conservative playbooks (and to Google's own webmaster guidelines) that avoid paid link schemes entirely because of manual-action/de-indexing risk. An operator following this source should weigh that risk explicitly rather than treating "everybody does it" as a green light. [d84b2be76cec2e08]
- **Region/language limits.** Fan-out query data was US/English-only in the tool demonstrated at recording time; non-US or non-English stores could not get this specific data point from that tool at that time. [web check: current coverage] [22b29e78f3c4a8e5]
- **Pricing and platform names will drift fastest.** Bright Local's per-citation price, Connectivity's free-tier answer limit, the presenter's own community pricing, and even platform names (Connectivity was "Featured.com," itself descended from "HARO") have already changed once inside the source's own narration — expect further change and verify live before quoting any of these to a client.
