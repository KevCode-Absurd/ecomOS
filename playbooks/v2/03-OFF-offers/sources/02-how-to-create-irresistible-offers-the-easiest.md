## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator design the offer: what the buyer gets, what they must do to get it, and the terms around it. It covers 4 lead offer types, free information "bait pieces", premiums (free gifts), price presentation and payment terms, guarantees, deadlines and scarcity, and the reply or order form. Its core claim: offers and their wording move response more than most copy edits, and only split tests tell you which offer wins. Use it to build an offer menu and a test backlog, not as a source of ecommerce benchmarks.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 What an offer is and why it matters

1. **An offer is what the prospect gets plus what they must do to get it.** Example: "Call us toll-free for a free booklet on adjustable beds": the booklet is what they get, the call is what they do. Bly quotes G. Lynn Sumner: give the reader something to do, make it easy, say where to get it, how much it costs, and why to act now. [abaef45f549d]
2. **Sell the offer, not the brand, when the goal is orders.** Bly cites a Kern Organization study (Target Marketing, 4/08): marketing that sold the offer instead of the brand produced about 10 times more orders and sales. Condition: direct mail for high lifetime-value offers (cable TV, long distance, cell phone service). The card treats the 10x as direction, not an ecommerce forecast. [abaef45f549d]
3. **The 2 highest-leverage parts of a promotion are the opening and the close.** Opening = headline, lead, design. Close = the offer and how it is presented. [5bb68d5c21f5]
4. **Offer rules of thumb (Bly's opinion from experience):** the more valuable and risk-free the offer seems, the better the response; the more you stress the offer in copy and graphics, the higher the response; the clearer the offer, the better the response; the lack of a clear offer depresses response. [abaef45f549d] [5b5cd348dd0a]
5. **Direct marketers test; they do not trust opinion or focus groups.** Bly says many direct marketers skip market research in favour of live tests, since the only opinion that counts is the one voted with a credit card. The card flags this as a direct-response viewpoint, not proof that research has no role. [abaef45f549d] [3ab744029b93]
6. **Offer testing can move response by 10% to 900%.** This is Bly's claimed range across his experience, repeated in the conclusion. Not a promise. [c7ccc30477c8]

### 2.2 Lead generation offers

7. **2 kinds of direct marketing.** Order generation ("mail order", one step: the promotion asks for payment). Lead generation (two step: get an inquiry, then follow up and close). Card adaptation: sell simple products in one step; use a lead or quote step only when complexity, price or wholesale needs a conversation and a follow-up workflow exists. [286c40a28bfa]
8. **There are 4 lead offer types.** [1944526d2b8a] [207cd91b5fae]
   - Soft: the prospect requests printed material, a catalog or a PDF, with no personal contact. Highest response because there is no sales pressure. Suits "just looking" prospects and those who want facts before talking to a rep.
   - Hard: the reply leads to a meeting, call, demo, estimate, consultation or appointment. Suits prospects with serious interest and an immediate or near need.
   - Negative: the reply form offers "Not interested right now because: (Please give reason, thank you.)"
   - Deferred: "Not interested right now. Try us again in (month/year)."
9. **Soft leads are real leads.** Many marketers think soft responders are brochure collectors. Bly says soft leads can be very profitable and often convert as well as hard leads. He cites a survey where 57% of IT professionals said white papers influenced their buying decisions. In his own mailing, soft and hard responders converted at the same rate. [c2720146fa16]
10. **Use the negative offer when testing a new product, service or offer by mail.** Without it, a low response does not tell you whether the piece or the product is wrong. With it, people tell you why (price too high, not compatible, do not use the type). It can also lift total response slightly. People who write a reason have framed an objection a salesperson can answer, so they may be prime prospects. [1944526d2b8a] [b4af036c7d67]
11. **Use the deferred offer when many prospects will need you later, not now.** Some prospects will not tick "send me a brochure" because they fear sales calls. The deferred box lets them flag a future need without being pestered. [b4af036c7d67]
12. **Give 2 or 3 offer options, not 1.** Having only 1 option is a common mistake. Best results usually come from 3: soft + hard + either negative or deferred. Minimum: soft + hard, with one primary (stressed in copy and graphics) and one secondary (often in the P.S.). [1944526d2b8a] [c2720146fa16]
13. **Make the soft offer primary when:** a brochure or catalog request is the normal first step in the industry; prospects are reluctant to meet; the product is complex and best explained in writing; you have few phone staff; prospects are far away with no local reps; or you already have a proven fulfilment kit that screens out the unqualified and sells the qualified. Example: industrial equipment makers. [c2720146fa16]
14. **Make the hard offer primary when:** phone leads close better; printed material only slows the sale; the product can be explained in a 2-minute call; you have a free or low-cost trial best sold by phone or in person; the product is unusual and hard to explain in print; or the audience are not readers. Example: adult vocational training, which found brochures a waste and needed people at a free intro class. [c2720146fa16] [286c40a28bfa]
15. **Prospects are like submarines.** Most are submerged (not ready) and surface only now and then. The soft offer draws out submerged prospects; the hard offer catches the ones on the surface who are actively solving a problem. Build both paths. [207cd91b5fae]

### 2.3 Bait pieces (free information offers)

16. **Adding a free information "bait piece" can often double response** versus the same copy without it (Bly's claim from experience). He calls it one of the few direct-response rules that "works almost every time" and says leads rose and marketing costs fell in virtually every campaign where he added one. Card treats this as a testable hypothesis. [04eeb7082406]
17. **The bait piece must be relevant, valuable, desirable and specific** to the prospect's needs. [04eeb7082406]
18. **Where it sits in the sales cycle:** the promotion offers the bait piece; some readers request it; you send it with sales material (for example a brochure); then you follow up. Depending on product and price, 10% to 35% or more of requesters eventually buy. [04eeb7082406]
19. **Why free content works (Bly's reasons):** prospects are information seekers; a free-booklet offer with a coupon or a picture of the booklet makes skimmers stop; people love free things. Unless the prospect has an urgent need, a mailing is just another pitch; a free booklet gives them a reason to reply anyway. [04eeb7082406]
20. **Feature the free item, not the product, on the reply device.** His example reply card headline: "Send me your FREE White Paper", illustrated with the white paper, not the product. [04eeb7082406] [7e4c3385639c]
21. **The topic should help the reader and quietly sell your approach.** It predisposes the reader to accept your method as standard and positions you as the expert. The sell must be soft. Michael Stelzner's "silver bullet": include a "how to select / what to look for" section; the client's product meets every criterion. The guide must appear neutral. [04eeb7082406]
22. **Narrow beats broad for topic and title.** Example: Fala's "Should I Personalize? A Direct Marketer's Guide to Personalized Direct Mail" beats a general "Direct Mail" guide. "How to Choose the Right Software for Your Business" interests a small-business owner; "How IBM Designs and Codes Its Software Systems" does not. [04eeb7082406]
23. **Wording changes perceived value.** Bob Hemmings (Direct, 12/08, p. 34): "The same offer stated differently is perceived as being a uniquely different offer and can give a substantial boost to results." Examples: "consumer awareness guide" beats "brochure"; "executive briefing" beats "white paper"; "Inside the White House Report" for a bundle of reprinted back-issue articles. Cards add: the item must live up to the better name. [4cd653974efd]
24. **Content need not be new.** Many people read to confirm what they know; 1 or 2 new ideas make it better. It must deliver the title's promise, be useful and be accurate. [ef7f4d952153]
25. **Keep selling out of the body.** Put your firm as author on the front cover with address and phone; put a company description and next step on the back cover, like an "About the Author" bio; ads may go in back pages. A sales pitch in the body angers readers. [ef7f4d952153]
26. **Do not give away so much that the reader no longer needs you.** Tell enough to create interest in hiring or buying. The card flags this as a judgment call that may conflict with a generous-education strategy. [ef7f4d952153]
27. **Keep the bait piece free.** Unless your main business is selling books or reports by mail, charging "will dramatically decrease your response rate". Paying does not make a better prospect; the barrier is the trouble of paying, not the cost. Qualify with questions instead. Bly: "For 99.99 percent of marketers, the free booklet offer should be just that: entirely free." Exception: when the information product is what you sell. [dc0cb3cc7271]
28. **Competitors' bait pieces are not a reason to skip yours.** A bait piece is almost never a USP or branding strategy. Pick a different topic that still sells your product, or the same topic done better, with a clearly different title. [04eeb7082406]
29. **Free booklet offers do not wear out with overuse** (Bly's experience). Prospects in a niche get used to them and look for them; an ad without one is more likely to be passed by. [04eeb7082406]
30. **Even an unread bait piece did its job** by producing an inquiry. [04eeb7082406]
31. **Electronic bait pieces carry higher perceived value.** Audio, video, CD-ROM and software stand out, get past gatekeepers, are kept, and are passed on. Software has a very high perceived value to cost ratio ($49 to $499 retail vs about $1 per CD or near zero by download). Do not use disks or software for audiences who are not computer literate. [555e6c0f1878] [753260589d73]
32. **Web viewers accept low production values,** so marketers can shoot their own video instead of paying for studio production (Bly, 2009). Card caveat: low production is not right for every brand; test trust and comprehension. [555e6c0f1878]
33. **Generic reprinted material works for generic needs; specialised products need original content.** Bly often recommends custom premiums because they can be more relevant to the product. [b6c6fd19bccb]

### 2.4 Hard offers

34. **Do not say "salesperson".** "Have a salesperson call me" is the most used hard offer and few pick it, because it signals pressure. Use a helpful title instead (list in section 6). [1944526d2b8a] [207cd91b5fae]
35. **Do not offer a "sales presentation", "sales call" or "sales pitch".** Offer a named, useful meeting (list in section 6). Reason: the first meeting has value to the buyer whether or not they buy (a printer's quote is a benchmark; a financial planner answers questions). Framing it as a free expert consultation recognises that value. [1944526d2b8a]
36. **Do not charge for the first meeting** if you are prospecting. Marginal prospects can be met by phone. Say clearly that it is free, since competitors may charge. [9d8ec982e3cb]
37. **Add value to the first meeting.** People get excited when a free service seems exclusive and worth $100 or $1,000. [9d8ec982e3cb]
38. **State "no obligation" in the letter and again on the reply form.** Do not assume prospects know. [9d8ec982e3cb]

### 2.5 Payment-with-order vs bill-me

39. **In mail order, a hard offer is cash with order; a soft offer is bill-me.** Cash with order removes collections. [1470cceef4db] [48c07a67fbf9]
40. **The offer is not an afterthought.** Copying boilerplate offers leads to terms that are confusing, deceptive, unfair or illogical. [4e5ab6ba27a6]
41. **State the offer so it cannot be misunderstood** (Bly paraphrasing Emerson). "Nothing is worse for your business reputation than a customer who did not understand your offer and feels he is being cheated when you refuse him a refund." [4e5ab6ba27a6]
42. **Bill-me lifts front-end response but not always net revenue.** "The true measure of profitability is not how many orders you get, but how many of those buyers pay." After no-pays, gross revenue can be below cash-with-order. Test it. [48c07a67fbf9] [acb243537e19]
43. **Consumers: cash with order alone can work.** Small amounts are not worth chasing in court (a $12 balance), and consumers pay easily by card. Bly's own mail-order book business runs on payment with order without problems. [48c07a67fbf9]
44. **B2B: it is usually a mistake not to offer bill-me.** Companies buy by purchase order, receive, get an invoice and pay in 30, often 60 to 90, days. Forcing advance payment pushes them to a more flexible competitor. [1470cceef4db] [48c07a67fbf9]
45. **Do not let a generous premium be taken for free.** With bill-me, a buyer can order, take the gift, cancel and pay nothing. Tie the gift to payment. [48c07a67fbf9]
46. **Fewer choices, faster decisions.** Offering a choice of alternate premiums "seldom works": "a decision deferred is a decision not made." [48c07a67fbf9]
47. **Every extra step lowers response.** Never make prospects read many instructions or fill complex paperwork. [7fdcaf1b88c1]

### 2.6 Premiums (free gifts)

48. **2 kinds of premium: merchandise and information.** David Ogilvy sold stoves by offering 6 cooking lessons for 3 guineas, free with a stove. [f90dfb9c9072]
49. **6 traits of an irresistible offer.** The more of these an offer has, the higher the response: (1) different or unique, (2) desirable, (3) high perceived value relative to fulfilment cost, (4) dramatises the brand or USP, (5) easy to take up, (6) minimises the buyer's risk and obligation. [f90dfb9c9072] [e068cda93a99] [7fdcaf1b88c1] [9d8ec982e3cb]
50. **Unique only works if it is also desirable.** A renewal premium of playing cards with the editor's picture flopped. [f90dfb9c9072]
51. **Perceived value is judged against the price being asked.** Playing cards feel worth about $1, too little to motivate renewing a $79 a year newsletter. An IBM Olympic pin cost about $1 but copy hinted it could become a collectible. [f90dfb9c9072]
52. **A premium can dramatise the USP even if it looks unrelated,** if copy builds a logical bridge. Newsweek's radio: radio is the fastest way to get news, Newsweek is the best way. [e068cda93a99]
53. **Say "free", not "complimentary",** even to executives. "Everybody wants free stuff." [9d8ec982e3cb]
54. **Two premiums are frequently better than one** (Dick Benson). Information buyers especially like multiple premiums. [6c1eb9526af9]
55. **Total premium value should exceed the price of the main product** (Michael Bell of Phillips Publishing), so the buyer feels the core product is free. Card: a hypothesis, not a rule. [6c1eb9526af9]
56. **Premiums can drive first purchase and continuity.** First purchase: Gevalia's free 12-cup coffeemaker with the first coffee order. Continuity: Franklin Mint's Civil War chess set, 1 piece a month, free board after all 32 pieces. [96a6bce07192]
57. **Keeper premiums raise response.** Let the buyer keep 1 or 2 bonus items even if they return the product. [e3d657e18bdc]
58. **Relevance of the gift: sources disagree by goal.** Benson: for sales, perceived value and desirability matter most; relevance is secondary. Bly: for lead generation, pick a related premium, because unrelated high-value gifts bring freebie seekers, while modest related gifts raise both total and qualified responses. [eb0b5c5a0052] [31e5cd2ce563]
59. **B2B buyers can be put off by gifts.** Many firms (especially defence contractors) ban vendor gifts; buyers want to look like sharp buyers; an expensive gift makes some suspect the price is inflated. A larger discount may beat a personal gift. If you give one, make it useful to the buyer's company. [eb0b5c5a0052] [31e5cd2ce563]

### 2.7 Price and payment terms

60. **A mail-order offer has 4 parts: price, payment terms, premium, guarantee.** All 4 can change response a lot. Price is "probably the most important to test" and is often counterintuitive: the lower price frequently loses. [608cbae96529] [60fcb07453d8]
61. **Price signals positioning.** A $79 software product appealed to neither consumers (too high) nor professionals ("not a serious tool"). Refocused on professionals at $129, sales "soared". Card: it was a repositioning, not proof that raising price lifts sales. [608cbae96529]
62. **State a fixed saving in dollars, a variable one in percent.** "$50-off" beat an equal "15%-off" coupon by nearly 3 times the revenue in one email test. When the saving varies by product, a percent works (Geico: "15 minutes could save you 15 percent"). [582638c44d2e]
63. **Buyers react to the first payment and discount the rest.** In installment offers, the prospect weighs the first installment. [43f3ba786866] [acb243537e19]
64. **"Free" is the strongest offer word; "the magic of a dollar" and "the magic of a penny" come close.** [9ee3a3ddedd2]
65. **Sticker shock comes from failing to show value, not from the price.** Use Mike Pavlish's "drop-in-the-bucket" technique: make the price look small next to the value, and do it before the price appears. Bly uses it for every offer, not only high prices. [51cc47d99259] [baa9c811749e]
66. **Name higher-priced alternatives first, so the real price brings relief.** Cialdini's sweater retailer put pricier sweaters at the front so the target sweaters looked like a bargain. [baa9c811749e]
67. **Compare across categories, not within.** Compare a newsletter to private consultation or training, not to another newsletter. [baa9c811749e]
68. **Divide by the length of service, never by page or book.** Specialist information always looks expensive per page next to trade books. [d6f9d5596273]
69. **Price resistance falls as belief grows that you are the unchallenged expert** in your niche. [e3d657e18bdc]
70. **In a good, better, best set, most buyers take the middle.** The cheapest looks not good enough, the dearest too expensive. Bly gives no measured split. Low-priced and free tiers also stop people calling you greedy and feed a funnel upward. [abb86777aeb2] [7367beea9d44]

### 2.8 Guarantees

71. **A strong guarantee is needed because buyers will not buy sight unseen from a stranger.** Design 4 things: length, conditionality, coverage, activation. [729e71de2194] [4f18a85334fc]
72. **Longer guarantees generally raise response.** Bly prefers 60 or 90 days over the common 30. In his conclusion: at least 60, preferably 90 days, unconditional. [729e71de2194] [c7ccc30477c8]
73. **Unconditional is best.** Conditions such as "in saleable condition" or "show a sincere effort" make buyers expect a dispute and "can depress sales". Say it plainly: "unconditional, iron-clad, money-back guarantee of satisfaction" or "We'll refund your money in full, no questions asked." Works for merchandise and information alike (Lewis Elwin, custom-lettered uniforms: few returns; "most of them were honest"). [729e71de2194] [4f18a85334fc]
74. **People are basically honest.** A few take advantage, but the extra orders from a strong guarantee outweigh them (Bly's e-book experience). [2de80b052adc] [d4173b53796a]
75. **The guarantee must be clear and visible.** Remove ambiguity; do not bury it in body copy or an 8-point footnote. [7e4c3385639c] [4e5ab6ba27a6]
76. **Putting the guarantee in the headline answers scepticism about a big claim.** Example: "Develop applications up to 6X faster, guaranteed or your money back." [5b5cd348dd0a]
77. **Design the offer so all risk sits with the seller** (conclusion). [c7ccc30477c8]

### 2.9 Urgency and scarcity

78. **"A decision deferred is a decision not made."** A mailing is like the Sunday paper: if it is not read within 48 hours, it probably never will be. Give a reason to act now, while the piece is in hand. [ea94a39e0ec6] [c5deb26d06e5]
79. **The simplest urgency tool is a time limit.** Specific date or generic window; both work (choice rules in section 4). [ea94a39e0ec6]
80. **Too short a deadline makes people feel rushed; too long removes pressure.** [ea94a39e0ec6]
81. **A limit works best with a credible, specific, true reason** (supply, staff capacity, seats, service quality). Bly also says an invented reason "can work" if it respects the prospect's intelligence. Card and modern law: do not publish limits you will not enforce. See section 9. [ea94a39e0ec6] [c5deb26d06e5]
82. **A fixed print run is not credible for a downloadable file.** "Only 1,000 copies" is especially suspect for a PDF or MP3. [ea94a39e0ec6]
83. **Once you state an expiry date you generally cannot extend it.** Run time-limited copy past your attorney. [ea94a39e0ec6]
84. **Word choice changes response.** "Free use" beat "free trial" by 15%, because "trial" meant extra work to IT staff. [7a9d0f1bb6c4]

### 2.10 Samples, trials, evaluations, loss leaders

85. **Samples, trials and evaluations are strong offers,** especially when the buyer can test on their own material or need. [4e2954e81935] [7a9d0f1bb6c4]
86. **Trials fail through inertia, not only fear.** Prospects send for the product and do nothing. Add a time-bound buying incentive and send reminders during the evaluation. [7a9d0f1bb6c4]
87. **Free usage units can beat an equal cash discount** ("30 free minutes" pulled about twice "$5 off" for MCI). [7a9d0f1bb6c4]
88. **A loss leader turns a shopper into a customer; profit comes from later sales at normal markup.** Retailers call them "door busters". [b1e72aa6f5b2] [ab07ca65bb72]

### 2.11 Reply elements and copy priorities

89. **Most copy polishing makes no difference.** Punctuation, grammar and "the" vs "a" rarely move response (Bly's opinion). Spend the care on 3 hot spots: position-critical copy (openings), the big idea, and offer wording on the reply element. [5bb68d5c21f5]
90. **Lead with the offer only when it is unusually strong or unusual.** A strong but standard offer goes in the close. The more compelling the offer, the more prominent. [5b5cd348dd0a]
91. **One word on the order form can decide a winner** when it changes the offer itself ($100 vs $500 a year; 10-day vs 90-day guarantee). A concrete "free information kit" beats "send for more information". [5bb68d5c21f5] [5b5cd348dd0a]
92. **Keep a reply device even if most people reply online or by phone.** It signals that a reply is expected and lifts total response across all channels. [361c9d4b9ad4]
93. **Offer multiple response channels.** Different prospects respond differently. [7fdcaf1b88c1]
94. **Every extra form field costs responses.** [7fdcaf1b88c1] [435dd5f2cbb1]

### 2.12 Testing

95. **Expect losing tests.** The best baseball team still loses about 4 in 10 games. If 1 test in 2, 3 or even 5 wins, you can make a lot of money. Small firms that test once every few years and quit after one flop miss the offer that can multiply sales 2, 3 or 5 times. [3ab744029b93]
96. **A one-shot launch is a bad bet.** Bly says product-launch promotion success can be as low as 10%, so a single promotion faces 10 to 1 odds. [3ab744029b93]
97. **Settle offer arguments by test, not opinion.** Claude Hopkins: "Advertising arguments should only be settled by testing." Peter Betuel: what matters is what prospects prefer, not what you prefer. "You can never predict which offer will get the best response." [3ab744029b93]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Build a lead offer set (source: Ch. 1)
1. Decide the primary action you want (information request or meeting) using the soft-primary and hard-primary conditions in 2.2 (items 13 and 14).
2. Write the primary offer and stress it in the headline area, body and graphics.
3. Add a secondary offer for people who will not take the primary. Put it at the end of the copy or in the P.S. Example: "P.S. For a free special report outlining six tax-saving strategies offered by our firm, just complete and mail the enclosed reply card."
4. Add a third option: negative ("Not interested right now because: ___") if you are testing a new product or offer or expect specific objections; deferred ("Not interested right now. Try us again in (month/year)") if many will buy later.
5. For a negative option, ask in the P.S. with a reason: "Even if you are not interested in [product], please complete and return the enclosed reply card so we can give your free [item] to another person on our list."
6. Follow up each path: send literature to soft responders, call hard responders, call deferred responders at the month they named, and have a salesperson answer the objection a negative responder wrote.
7. Track the split of replies by box and the conversion of each group to sales. [needs brand data: reply split by option and lead-to-order rate]
Card adaptation for ecommerce: soft = buying guide, fit quiz, checklist or back-in-stock alert for email; hard = consultation, sizing or stylist appointment, custom quote or direct bundle checkout; negative = exit question "why not today" routed by reason (price, fit, timing); deferred = "remind me before winter" or replenishment date. Check the email, quiz or CRM tool can store the answer and trigger the branch. [verify UI] Judge by downstream purchase and contribution, not email volume. [1944526d2b8a] [207cd91b5fae] [b4af036c7d67] [c2720146fa16]

### 3.2 Create a bait piece in 7 steps (source: Ch. 2)
1. **Pick the topic.** It must interest the reader and help sell your product. Typical: how to do a thing, or how to choose a product. Include a neutral-looking "what to look for" section your product meets. Make it narrow. [04eeb7082406]
2. **Write the title.** "How to" titles and number titles ("7 Ways", "6 Steps", "14 Winning Methods") work. Title is "all-important" because it decides whether people send for it. Examples that ran: "15 Ways to Improve Your Collection Efforts" (RMCB); "7 Questions to Ask Before You Invest in DP Training... and One Good Answer to Each" (Chubb Institute); "33 Ways to Make Better Displays" (Display Masters); "Family-Owned Businesses: The 3 Most Common Pitfalls... and How to Avoid Them" (Consulting Dynamics); "Choosing Business Software" (IBM). Audience-specific pattern: "What Every Ad Agency Executive Should Know About Maximizing E-Mail Delivery Rates". Avoid a title that sounds like a competitor's. [04eeb7082406]
3. **Pick the format.** Tip sheet (1 sheet), booklet, special report, PDF white paper, kit, manual, book, poster, audio, video, CD-ROM, webinar, podcast. Start cheap when demand is unproven (card). To serve several segments, keep the body and change only the introduction and cover. [618d85f36dcb]
4. **Describe it enticingly.** Name it for its value ("consumer awareness guide", "executive briefing"), not its bare format. The content must justify the name. [4cd653974efd] Note: the book promises lists of titling conventions and label suggestions here, but they are missing from the exported text (likely images).
5. **Put a price on the cover** (upper right corner), even though it is free. Use the price ranges in section 4. Then list every bait piece for sale on your website at that cover price, so you can honestly say the price is real. [38a0eb6af210] [769c1d67e28c]
6. **Package it.** For 50 pages or more, use a 3-ring binder and call it a manual. Print the cover on a separate sheet or use a clear "trap-in" sleeve so outdated covers do not waste custom binders. A proven, long piece can become a self-published book. [618d85f36dcb]
7. **Write the content.** Deliver the title's promise, be accurate, stay pure information in the body, sell only on the covers and back pages, and do not tell so much that the reader no longer needs you. [ef7f4d952153]
Then: feature the bait piece prominently in the ad and on the reply device, with a picture of its cover. [04eeb7082406]

### 3.3 Source bait-piece content cheaply (source: Ch. 2)
1. Write a feature for a trade journal and reprint it as a tip sheet or special report. Mark the manuscript "First rights only" so you keep reprint rights. Call it a "tip sheet" or "special report", not "article reprint". Cite the original publication and date in small type.
2. Reprint association or society publications (with permission or for a fee) and add your own front and back cover with logo and contact details. Example: an orthodontist mailed a reprinted society booklet with a cover letter, "with good results".
3. Dealers and distributors: use manufacturer literature with your label.
4. Use US government publications, many of which are not copyrighted (a radon firm reprinted "Radon in the Home" with its name on top).
5. Use generic reprints only for generic needs; create original content for specialised products.
Card caveat: confirm rights, attribution and licence for each item; "first rights" does not grant unlimited reuse by itself; a reprint must not imply endorsement. [web check: current copyright status and attribution rules for any reused government or association material] [b6c6fd19bccb]

### 3.4 Qualify leads when freebie seekers are a problem (source: Ch. 2)
Use some or all, knowing each one lowers total response [dc0cb3cc7271]:
1. Charge a nominal fee for the catalog or literature.
2. Ask many questions about the prospect, or require a survey or questionnaire.
3. Make prospects pay return postage.
4. Say you will not fulfil requests without a phone number.
5. Write "Serious inquiries only, please."
6. Do not stress guarantees, money-back, free offers, risk-free trials or estimates.
7. Do not send a premium on inquiry; hand it over at the sales meeting or send it on purchase.
Bly's preferred route for B2B: keep the piece free and add qualifying questions on the reply card, such as "What type of business are you in?" and "What is your immediate need?".

### 3.5 Write a hard offer (source: Ch. 3, 5 rules)
1. Replace "salesperson" with a helpful title (section 6 list). Use a real title only if true (say "financial planner" only if they are planners).
2. Replace "sales presentation" with a named free service (section 6 list).
3. Make the first meeting free and say so plainly: "This initial consultation is absolutely free, with no obligation of any kind."
4. Raise the meeting's perceived value: name a concrete deliverable and a result. Model: Timeplex's "FREE network topology analysis that can reduce your monthly line costs 10% to 20% or more."
5. State "no obligation" in the letter and on the reply form. Sample reply line: "Please have a technical consultant call to arrange a free analysis of my network requirements. I understand there is no cost for this initial analysis, and no obligation."
Card adaptation: in ecommerce, name the step as access to expertise (fit consultation, shade match, configuration check) and only offer what the store can fulfil fast. [1944526d2b8a] [207cd91b5fae] [9d8ec982e3cb]

### 3.6 Audit an offer's wording for fairness and clarity (source: Ch. 4)
1. Read the offer as if new. Do not reuse boilerplate from earlier promotions.
2. Check each payment path gets equal treatment. Example flaw: with prorated refunds on "unmailed issues", a bill-me subscriber who cancels gets issue 1 free while a pay-up-front subscriber pays for it. Fix: full money-back within the first 30 days, prorated after.
3. Define "lifetime": whose lifetime, and does it cover wear, negligence, boredom or wanting a newer model.
4. Replace "full refund on the unused portion" if you mean a prorated refund; if you mean all money back, say "We'll refund your entire subscription fee in full" or "every penny you paid".
5. Replace "free issue" with "risk-free issue" or "no-risk issue" unless it is truly extra (13 issues for the price of 12, for example an evergreen first issue in the welcome kit).
6. Card additions: check the same terms appear in ads, product page, cart, checkout, confirmation emails and support macros, and test comprehension on people new to the offer. [needs brand data: current refund policy text and support macros] [web check: consumer-protection, auto-renewal and refund rules in each selling market] [4e5ab6ba27a6]

### 3.7 Protect margin with modified bill-me terms (source: Ch. 4)
1. B2B: offer both pay-with-order and a bill-me option.
2. If the product is costly or easily damaged: "[ ] Bill me. (NOTE: Product not shipped until payment is received.)"
3. If the premium is attractive: ship the product now, hold the gift: "[ ] Bill me. (NOTE: Bonus Gift is shipped when payment in full for your order is received by us.)" Consumer version: "Bill me. (Bonus gift sent upon receipt of payment.)"
4. If the premium is costly: state that a returned product must come back with the premium.
5. To cut no-pays: require a signature or initials, or a purchase order number: "[ ] Bill me. Our purchase order number is: ___ Signature (required for all bill-me orders): ___". On phone and online orders ask for the PO number. Do not require the actual PO document; busy buyers will not produce it.
6. Measure pay-up rate, not just orders.
Card adaptation: invoice or net terms for approved business accounts, orders held as pending until paid, bonuses released on cleared payment, and the sequence explained before checkout. Verify the ERP or order system can hold and release reliably. [verify UI] [needs brand data: B2B share of orders, overdue invoices, no-pay rate] [1470cceef4db] [48c07a67fbf9]

### 3.8 Choose and present premiums (source: Ch. 5)
1. Pick a candidate that scores on the 6 traits (item 49). Drop anything cheap-feeling relative to the order price.
2. For information products, choose from the newsletter premium types in section 6.
3. Decide single vs multiple. Benson: 2 often beat 1. If stacking, give each item a specific job.
4. Put a price on each report cover and sell each one on your site at that price so you can say "We are not making this price up. We actually sell this report at this price on our website."
5. Aim for total premium value above the main price (Bell), as a test.
6. Show a picture of the premium on the order device; fan out multiple report covers.
7. Test the premium against a no-premium control or another premium, one per campaign.
[needs brand data: premium unit cost, fulfilment and shipping cost, order price] [f90dfb9c9072] [6c1eb9526af9] [31e5cd2ce563]

### 3.9 Present a price (source: Ch. 6, drop-in-the-bucket plus 12 tactics)
1. Before showing the price, build value: say what the buyer gets and what the alternatives cost.
2. Pick the tactics that fit (numbered as in the book):
   1. Compare to the cost of hiring the author or expert (Bly: $500 an hour = about $42 per 5 minutes; "less than I charge for just 5 minutes of my time").
   2. State higher prices first so the real price brings relief (laser surgery $1,000, optician glasses $300, mail-order readers $19.95).
   3. Make an apples-to-oranges comparison (newsletter vs speechwriter or managed account).
   4. Spread payments (quarterly for a service costing thousands; monthly "till forbid" billing for Internet services; "$19.95 a month" vs "$240 for one year").
   5. State the price in its smallest terms per unit of time (per day), never per page or per book.
   6. Value the component parts, and position 1 or 2 parts as keeper premiums.
   7. Add an element the buyer cannot easily price (a CD-ROM with a loose-leaf binder breaks the book-to-book comparison).
   8. "Find a solution with your pocket calculator" (compounding example; see section 9 on why this is risky).
   9. Pre-empt the objection: state a high price up front to screen and add exclusivity ("This service is for serious investors only. It costs $2,500 a year. If that price scares you, this is not for you.").
   10. False close: reach the ask, then "But wait, there's more", add a benefit, then ask.
   11. Add a sweetener: extra premium, longer warranty or guarantee, free service or support, 2 for 1.
   12. Establish yourself as the leading expert.
3. Show the actual price in full (card rule; Bly does not say otherwise but his examples lead with the smaller figure).
4. Test the framed version against a plain-price control on purchases, refunds and margin (card).
[needs brand data: real alternative costs that can be substantiated, product life or use period for per-day math] [51cc47d99259] [baa9c811749e] [d6f9d5596273] [e3d657e18bdc] [43f3ba786866]

### 3.10 Build a good, better, best ladder (source: Ch. 6)
Services/information version, top to bottom: premium consulting (thousands of dollars); mid-range coaching (weekly calls, online help); packaged information product ($100 to $300); starter book or e-book ($10 to $50); free content ("kick-the-tires": blog, podcasts, webinars, e-newsletter, reports).
Physical product version (supplement marketers): top = 6-month supply at a discount with free premiums and free shipping; middle = 3-month supply with 1 free bonus and free shipping; bottom = 1 bottle (1-month), no premium, buyer pays shipping. Average unit of sale lands at a 3 or 4-month supply.
Card steps: give every tier a real job, make differences easy to compare on one screen, label "most popular" or "best value" only when true, and check the top tier stays profitable after gifts and freight. [needs brand data: tier mix, margin per tier, freight cost] [abb86777aeb2] [7367beea9d44]

### 3.11 Design a guarantee (source: Ch. 7)
1. **Length.** Pick from section 4 thresholds. Match it to when the buyer can judge the product (card). Avoid long windows for products that date (annual directories: 30 days).
2. **Conditionality.** Default to unconditional. If a condition is needed (card: hygiene, customisation, safety), state it simply and specifically.
3. **Coverage.** Decide whether shipping is refunded. If not, say "product refund", not "full refund". Decide who pays return shipping (some sellers include a prepaid label or arrange UPS or freight pickup). For seminars: dissatisfied attendees tell the leader by the first break on day 1, hand in materials and get a full refund.
4. **Activation.** Start the clock on receipt of goods, not order date. Spell out how to claim (supplements: return the unused portion "or even the empty bottles"; corncob pipes: break off the stem and mail it back).
5. **Clarity.** Remove performance-conditional wording. Model: "If you do not make extra money trading options with our program, or you are not 100 percent satisfied for any other reason, or for no reason, just return the program within 90 days for a full refund."
6. **Make it stand out.** 12-point type, large bold headline, box or certificate border, owner's signature under it, on the order device too. Card: place the short version near the price or add-to-cart with a link to the full policy; check mobile readability.
7. **Late claims.** A few days to a week late: let it slide. Months late: decline. Reasonable or regular customer: offer a credit instead of a refund.
8. **Damages claims.** Bly (not a lawyer): generally not liable beyond product cost except extraordinary cases; consult an attorney.
[needs brand data: refund rate, return cost per order, reverse-logistics cost, chargebacks] [web check: statutory return and warranty rules per market] [729e71de2194] [4f18a85334fc] [7e4c3385639c]

### 3.12 Test an "unfair advantage" guarantee (source: Ch. 7)
1. Use it where the product cannot be returned or has near-zero marginal cost (e-books, downloads, software).
2. Tell buyers they may get a full refund and keep the product. Bly's copy: "If you aren't convinced that The Copywriter's Toolkit is the smartest investment you've made in your freelance copywriting business this year, just let me know within 90 days. I'll send you a prompt refund of your $39. And you can still keep the e-book with my compliments. That way, you risk nothing."
3. For a usable tool, say openly the buyer can use it during the trial and still return it (Y2K recoding tool).
4. Measure conversion and refund rate. Keep it only if extra orders outweigh refunded access (card).
Card limits: not for physical goods without a returns-cost model; not for high-value software, licences or files likely to be shared at scale. [needs brand data: digital product share, refund rate] [2de80b052adc] [d4173b53796a] [322b4dd8abd3]

### 3.13 Add urgency (source: Ch. 8)
1. Choose a specific date if you control timing; a generic window ("Respond within the next 15 days") if print runs are large or mailings arrive at unknown times. Card: online, use a real date and time with time zone.
2. Set the length (section 4).
3. Email: send 1 or 2 reminders as the deadline nears. Landing page: show a real-time countdown.
4. Add a quick-response bonus if useful (free report for reply within 7 days).
5. If you limit quantity or places, give the true reason (staff capacity, seats, service quality) and make it specific.
6. Early-bird pricing for events and pre-publication offers (amounts in section 4).
7. Get attorney review; do not extend a stated deadline.
Card additions: show the post-deadline price, close or change the price when stated, keep inventory counts synced across store, ads, email and support. [needs brand data: real stock and production limits] [web check: false-urgency and fake-scarcity rules in each market] [ea94a39e0ec6] [c5deb26d06e5]

### 3.14 Run a trial or evaluation (source: Ch. 8)
1. Offer a sample on the buyer's own material or need (send a 5-pound bag of compound to see pellets; evaluate a water sample; prototype an app in 30 minutes).
2. Or offer a trial: use it for a set period at no cost, return at the maker's expense (pelletizer: 1 month free).
3. Or a product evaluation: "If it is not, send it back and owe us nothing. If it is, you can buy it for the list price of $X."
4. Counter inertia: add a buy-within-period incentive ("decide to invest within the next 30 days, you will get 10 percent off").
5. Send reminders by mail or phone during the evaluation.
Card: state requirements, timeline and shipping before opt-in; measure activation, return rate and conversion, not signups. [needs brand data: sample cost, trial return rate] [4e2954e81935] [7a9d0f1bb6c4]

### 3.15 Run a loss-leader or affiliate entry offer (source: Ch. 8)
1. Pick an entry item with wide appeal and real value.
2. Price it at or below cost.
3. Ship it with an offer for the next, higher-margin purchase.
4. Online version: set a $39 list price on an e-book; ask affiliates to sell it at $19 ($20 off); pay them 100% commission (the whole $19), which equals 50% of $38, so it gets attention; you keep the buyer list.
Card additions: plan the back end (cross-sells, replenishment, bundles, email flows) before traffic arrives; cap the loss you can fund; judge on cohort contribution and repeat purchase. [needs brand data: product cost, pick-pack, shipping subsidy, repeat rate, payback window] [b1e72aa6f5b2] [ab07ca65bb72]

### 3.16 Build the reply or order device (source: Ch. 9, 9 techniques)
1. Show a picture of the premium (report cover; fan out several).
2. Highlight the guarantee: certificate border, owner's signature, legible type.
3. Leave enough space for name and address.
4. Put the key code on the mailing label and design the piece so the label comes back with the reply.
5. Track phone replies with "Ask for Betty" or "Ask for Extension 112"; send web traffic from each promotion to its own URL.
6. Use short, memorable domain names that connect to the product.
7. On web lead forms, ask the fewest questions: consumer = name and email; B2B may add company and phone; phone is required if sales will call the lead.
8. On online order pages, prevent "AVS mismatch" by telling buyers the name entered must exactly match the name on the card.
9. Test colour: order buttons and the order form's paper colour (canary yellow or robin's egg blue, different from envelope and letter).
Other formats: reply card with tick boxes (prospect pays postage or prepaid BRC); perforated tear-off stub showing the premium, the guarantee, or an order record; 8.5 x 11 inch form with bottom-third tear-off card; invoice-style order form listing each feature as an item with its value (Kiplinger's); application-style form for membership, mortgage or insurance; tokens, stamp sheets and peel-off stickers; yes/no/maybe stickers (yes = order, maybe = free trial, no = negative offer).
Card adaptation: tell shoppers which fields are required and why, review field-level abandonment, use UTMs or codes per variation. [verify UI] [361c9d4b9ad4] [7fdcaf1b88c1] [435dd5f2cbb1]

### 3.17 Test offers (source: Ch. 9)
1. Split the audience into equal cells, one variable each (example: 5,000 postcards, half "Tastes Great", half "Less Filling").
2. Send each cell to its own URL. Make the landing page headline match the postcard or ad.
3. Measure click-through, conversion and leads per cell.
4. Online, keep testing headlines, visuals, prices, bonuses, guarantees, subheads and leads; roll winners into the page.
5. Test a series of A/B splits. Do not judge a product on one promotion.
Card: preselect the success metric; use contribution margin for purchase tests and later purchase rate for lead tests; need enough sample and stable tracking. [needs brand data: traffic volume per cell, baseline conversion] [3ab744029b93] [abaef45f549d]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Selling brand image when the job is orders; no clear offer. [abaef45f549d]
2. Offering only 1 response option. [1944526d2b8a]
3. Dismissing soft leads as brochure collectors. [c2720146fa16]
4. Skipping bait pieces because they seem a cliche, overused, already used by competitors, or because prospects "have too much to read". Bly answers each. [04eeb7082406]
5. Charging for a bait piece to screen freebie seekers (SR's $3 story). [dc0cb3cc7271]
6. Putting a sales pitch inside the bait piece body, or giving away so much the reader no longer needs you. [ef7f4d952153]
7. Setting a cover price so high it looks like a scam. [38a0eb6af210]
8. Printing covers directly on binders, so dated content wastes stock. [618d85f36dcb]
9. Using general printers for books (2 to 3x cost). [618d85f36dcb]
10. Using disks or software premiums for audiences who are not computer literate. [753260589d73]
11. Using "salesperson" and "sales presentation" in hard offers; charging for the first meeting; not saying "no obligation". [9d8ec982e3cb]
12. Treating the offer as boilerplate; payment paths that reward the less desired path. [4e5ab6ba27a6]
13. Ambiguous "lifetime guarantee", "full refund on the unused portion", "free issue". [4e5ab6ba27a6]
14. Sending a generous premium on bill-me before payment. [48c07a67fbf9]
15. Using a bestselling book as a premium (many own it); offering a choice of premiums. [48c07a67fbf9]
16. Counting orders instead of paid orders on bill-me. [48c07a67fbf9]
17. Demanding advance payment or an actual purchase order document from B2B buyers. [1470cceef4db]
18. Making buyers read instructions or fine print, or fill long forms. [7fdcaf1b88c1]
19. A premium that is unique but not wanted, or cheap relative to the price (playing cards). [f90dfb9c9072]
20. Writing "complimentary" instead of "free". [9d8ec982e3cb]
21. Assuming the lowest price wins. [608cbae96529]
22. Revealing the price before building value (sticker shock). [51cc47d99259]
23. Per-page or per-book price comparisons for specialist information. [d6f9d5596273]
24. Conditional guarantees ("saleable condition", "sincere effort", returning worksheets). [729e71de2194]
25. Lifetime or 1-year guarantees on products that date or can be reused yearly (directories). [729e71de2194]
26. Saying "full refund" when shipping is not refunded. [729e71de2194]
27. Starting the guarantee clock at order instead of receipt; not saying how to claim. [729e71de2194]
28. Burying the guarantee in body copy or an 8-point footnote. [7e4c3385639c]
29. Performance-conditional guarantee wording that implies small gains void the refund. [4e5ab6ba27a6]
30. Requiring e-book buyers to "delete" the file (unenforceable). [2de80b052adc]
31. No deadline; a date that arrives already expired because of print or mail delays. [ea94a39e0ec6]
32. Deadlines so short buyers feel rushed. [ea94a39e0ec6]
33. Scarcity claims that are not credible, especially for downloads. [ea94a39e0ec6]
34. Extending a stated expiry date. [ea94a39e0ec6]
35. Expensive unrelated premiums in lead generation (freebie seekers) and personal gifts to corporate buyers. [eb0b5c5a0052] [31e5cd2ce563]
36. Free trials without a decision incentive or reminders. [7a9d0f1bb6c4]
37. Agonising over punctuation instead of the opening, big idea and offer wording. [5bb68d5c21f5]
38. Dropping the reply card or coupon because "everyone responds online". [361c9d4b9ad4]
39. Cramped name and address space on the order form. [361c9d4b9ad4]
40. Landing page headline that does not match the ad or postcard. [3ab744029b93]
41. One promotion per product with no testing; quitting direct marketing after one loss. [3ab744029b93]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Fit for ecommerce.** Almost every example is direct mail, B2B lead generation, subscriptions, seminars or information products. The ecommerce steps above come from the cards, not the book. An agent should treat Bly's numbers as test ideas for a store, never as benchmarks. [60fcb07453d8] [abaef45f549d]

**Conflicts with common ecommerce advice:**
- Guarantees: Bly wants unconditional guarantees of 60 to 90 days. Physical-goods stores usually carry return shipping, restocking, hygiene and resale costs (for example pierced jewellery, personalised items, perishables), so unconditional long windows can cost far more than for e-books. Cards require a returns-cost model first. [needs brand data: return rate, reverse-logistics cost, resale rate] [c7ccc30477c8] [322b4dd8abd3]
- Email capture: DTC stores often use a % discount for sign-up. Bly and Benson favour free information or premiums over cash discounts, and dollar amounts over percentages. Unproven for stores; test. [60fcb07453d8] [582638c44d2e]
- Urgency: Bly says an invented reason for a limit "can work". Modern consumer-protection rules treat false urgency and fake scarcity as deceptive. Do not follow that line. [web check: false-urgency and dark-pattern rules in each selling market, for example India, US, UK, EU] [ea94a39e0ec6] [c5deb26d06e5]
- Installment and first-payment framing, $1 card-capture trials and "till forbid" billing are now regulated (auto-renewal, negative-option and BNPL rules) and payment-provider sensitive. Bly's tactic of leading with the first payment conflicts with modern drip-pricing and total-price disclosure advice. Cards require the total price visible. [web check: auto-renewal, negative-option and instalment disclosure rules] [acb243537e19] [43f3ba786866]
- Tactic 8, "find a solution with your pocket calculator", shows 5% a month compounding $10,000 to $3.4 million in 10 years (and $912 million at 10%). Bly says "you can almost always make the numbers come out in support of your selling proposition". This is misleading for most products and illegal-risk for financial claims. Do not use as a pattern.
- Tactic 10, the false close ("But wait, there's more"), and "messy" order forms are dated infomercial and mail habits; test before use, and Bly himself reports that marketers disagree on messy vs clean forms.
- Leading with the offer: Bly says stress the offer everywhere, yet also says lead with it only when it is unusually strong. The cards follow the second rule. [5b5cd348dd0a]

**Internal inconsistencies in the text:**
- The Copywriter's Toolkit is $79 in the drop-in-the-bucket section but $39 in tactic 1 and in the guarantee copy.
- American Speaker is from "Georgia Publishing" in one place and "Georgetown Publishing" in another.
- "Admiral Bird Society" should likely be Admiral Byrd (Hank Burnett's letter). Not verified. [web check: Hank Burnett Admiral Byrd letter details]
- The Florida Marlins at 23-14 lost about 38% of games; Bly rounds to "4 out of every 10".
- Software premium value is given as $49 to $499 in one chapter and $49 to $300+ in another.

**Card over-reach (card says more than the source):**
- [38a0eb6af210] says "the source favors an odd number" for a lead magnet value. The text gives the $29 example and price ranges but never recommends odd numbers.
- [9ee3a3ddedd2] frames "dollar and penny endings" as price-ending tests. The source's "magic of a dollar/penny" means $1 or 1-cent entry offers, not price endings like $19.99. Treat that card as a card-author idea.
- [d4173b53796a] says the source treats guarantee conditionality as a trade-off. The source says unconditional is best and conditional guarantees depress sales.
- [322b4dd8abd3] says the source "provides no numeric lift" for keep-the-product refunds; the text gives David Powers' +21% and Bly's under-1% refund rate.
- [51cc47d99259] and the header describe comparing "$39 product to a $500/hour consulting rate". In the text, $500 an hour is Bly's own fee used to say the product costs less than 5 minutes of his time.
- [c5deb26d06e5] calls urgency a "ratchet" that tightens; the book gives reminder emails near the deadline but not a ratchet model.

**Evidence gaps.** No test lists sample sizes, confidence, test duration or margin effects. The 10x, 2x, 6x and 21% figures are single cases. Benson's rules are from 1992. The 20% per-field abandonment figure (DM News 2008) is far higher than most modern form data suggests. [web check: current form-field abandonment research] The 57% white-paper figure has no named survey.

**Missing from the export.** The lists of "conventions for titling bait pieces" and "suggestions on how to label your premium" are announced but absent (likely images). Diagrams (sales cycle, reply card, tear-off stub, invoice-style form layout, Kiplinger's form, yes/no/maybe examples, Dale Carnegie coupon ad, Rodale's 10 reports) are missing. The Appendix: Resources and About the Author are not in the text.

**Thin on how.** No method for choosing guarantee length from data, no way to size a test, no guidance on bundle or tier price gaps, and no numbers on return costs. Costs for printing, audio and video are 2009 and must be re-quoted. [web check: current print-on-demand and short-run costs if physical guides are planned]

**What an agent needs before acting on this source:** [needs brand data: gross margin by SKU, AOV, return and refund rates, premium and sample unit costs, shipping cost, repeat purchase rate, email capture rate, payment options available, B2B share] and [web check: consumer-protection rules on guarantees, urgency, reference prices and auto-renewal in each selling market].
