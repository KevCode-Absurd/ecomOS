## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator design the acquisition offer itself, not just the discount size. It covers 3 offer types: (1) the front-loaded subscription offer with a welcome kit, for consumable and subscription brands; (2) the same-product bundle with a pre-selected middle tier and "free item" framing, for apparel, accessories and multi-unit goods; (3) the first-order pop-up offer, including dollar vs percent framing by AOV, mystery discounts, store-credit framing and stacking the site's existing discount into one advertised number. It closes with how to judge an offer test: opt-in rate, placed-order rate, gross profit and 90-day LTV. It is strong on offer structures and framing, with real page examples. It is thin on test sizes, stopping rules, margin math and compliance.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 An offer is a stack of benefits; a discount is only 1 part of it
Source 07 defines an offer as "a series of benefits and advantages that a customer gets from buying with you". A discount is only percent off. Testing 10% vs 20% vs 30% off is "discount testing", not offer testing, and the presenter calls it "the biggest reason why brands don't break through the ceiling". Real lift comes from changing the structure: subscription vs one-time, gift vs no gift, credit vs cash off, access, guarantee. He adds that the fastest-scaling brands put "real strategic thought and ad money" behind offer tests and test "religiously". He also says a deep cut like 50% or 90% off will sell but "is not going to last you very long". Condition: his opinion from client work; it does not address price-elastic or margin-constrained categories (card caveat). Card addition, not in the transcript: keep the normal-price path visible as the anchor so the offer reads as a stack, not a permanent markdown. [82ae4d2b081b]

### 2.2 The offer, not the team, ads or product, is what separates 7-figure from 9-figure brands
The opening claim: 2 brands can sell the same product, and the one that hits 9 figures does so because of the offer. "It's not the team. It's not the ads. It's definitely not the product." He also says a strong offer "will get [people] to buy more than any other creative can" and will "get people to convert no matter the product". Condition: this is rhetoric, not evidence. No comparison of matched brands is given. Do not use it as a rule that product and creative do not matter. [82ae4d2b081b]

### 2.3 Push subscription on the first order, because subscribers are worth more
Subscribers repeat at a much higher rate. His cohort chart: average month-6 LTV about $226 for subscribers vs about $138 for non-subscribers. He also says only about 10% of people who place a one-time order later come back and subscribe. So the first order is the moment to win the subscription. Condition: 1 agency dashboard, 1 brand context. Subscribers self-select for higher intent, so part of the gap may not be caused by subscription (card caveat). `[needs brand data: subscriber vs non-subscriber cohort LTV at a fixed horizon, and share of one-time buyers who later subscribe]` [02a3e80a55a4] [971d6e5bf0ce]

### 2.4 A flat recurring discount gives no reason to subscribe today; front-load it
"Subscribe and save 15% on every order does not incentivize me to subscribe on my first order." Instead, raise the first-order discount and lower the recurring one. Example: Armra, 30% off the first order, then 5% recurring. His recommendation for a brand he reviewed: lead with "subscribe and save 25%" on the first month, then state 10% recurring on every order. His reasoning: "at that point we already got them", and the lower recurring rate saves margin on every later order, so it is "probably going to make us more net margin". Condition: the 25 to 30% first / 5 to 10% recurring numbers are his recommendation and brand examples, not tested thresholds (card caveat). The net-margin claim is not shown with numbers. `[needs brand data: gross margin, subscription retention curve, orders per subscriber]` [971d6e5bf0ce] [5ff86e2e0a6f]

### 2.5 Free gifts must improve use of the core product
Gifts should "actually contribute to the customer experience" and help consumption, which he says helps long-term retention. "We do not want a tote bag. Otherwise, this is pointless." Example: Everyday Dose (coffee) gives a creamer, an electric frother (without it the coffee clumps), a gunmetal spoon instead of plastic ("feels special"), and a breathwork app. Each gift carries a stated dollar value on the page. Condition: the coffee kit is an illustration, not a template for every category. Kit COGS and sourcing belong to 18-COG; do that math before committing (card caveat). [494b95673d4f]

### 2.6 Stack value and make it subscription-only
IM8 and AG1 put the welcome kit, gifts, program access and best price only on subscription. A one-time buyer pays more and gets none of the kit. He says brands "take items that are low cost for them to produce", "things that help the customer experience", bundle them and make them "only available on subscription". Condition: page snapshots from 2 brands; offers may have changed `[web check: IM8 and AG1 current subscription offers]`. [971d6e5bf0ce] [bbc8899c72f2]

### 2.7 Fund a rich offer by raising price, then discounting to the target price
Asked how brands afford stacked offers: "They just jack up their prices and then you stack up the value." He repeats it for first-order offers: "that doesn't mean slashing your margins. You can jack up your price and then discount down to your target price." Condition: the source says nothing about credibility of the anchor, customer reaction to permanent anchors, or pricing law. Reference-price and "was" price rules vary by market (card caveat). `[web check: reference-price and strike-through pricing rules in the selling market]` [bbc8899c72f2]

### 2.8 Bundles are the best offer for fashion and accessory brands
He calls the simple bundle "the best by far offer for fashion and accessory brands". Reason: most clothing brands push 1 product, but people like different styles, so sell them as a bundle and stack the value. Example: True Classic, which he says does over $500M selling plain t-shirts. Condition: his opinion plus 1 very large brand; not a benchmark. [61cf8d39f4bb]

### 2.9 Pre-select the middle bundle tier and anchor it against the single price
True Classic auto-selects the 6-pack on the page and calls it "the staple six-pack" in all ad messaging. It is the AOV target ($120). The single is the most expensive per unit, so every bundle reads as a saving. The 6-pack is not the biggest option on offer: "most people opt towards a middle option. The biggest seems too scary, but a middle option is much more friendly." All ads point at the pre-selected tier. Condition: 1 brand example. Card caveat: pre-selecting a larger quantity can raise returns in fit-sensitive categories. Card 61cf8d39f4bb adds "make the largest tier visually heavier"; the transcript does not say this. [61cf8d39f4bb]

### 2.10 "Free" beats the equivalent percent off
"After meticulous testing, we have found out that free always works better. People just like free." So instead of "33% off 3 products", say "buy 2 get 1 free". Also "buy 3 get 2 free". Example: Live Fresh (toothpaste), which he says scaled to 8 to 9 figures with offer tests. Condition: "always" is his generalization from his company's tests; no numbers are shown (card caveat). Keep the total price the same so only the framing changes (card step). [52bf7bcc54a4]

### 2.11 Bundle the same product, not a mixed assortment
He does not recommend bundles of different products, for example creatine plus protein powder plus other supplements. "We don't see bundles like that perform as well", because the buyer "might only want 2 of the 3 items". He pushes same-product bundles: buy 2 get 1 free of the same product in a different flavor, a different color, or more of the same. Condition: his observation, not a tested rule. Some categories have real routine-based cross-sell (card caveat). [a4348a9802d1]

### 2.12 Keep the core offer evergreen and run a separate flash-deals page for urgency
True Classic keeps bundles as the evergreen core offer. It also runs ads to a flash-deals page with a countdown to midnight, custom deals on items like overstock, and a "79% claimed" bar. The bestseller 6-pack is linked below the flash deals. He says this "adds a ton of urgency" and "makes it feel special". Condition: he says he does not know whether they actually refresh the deals every day. Fake scarcity is regulated in some markets (card caveat). `[web check: countdown and scarcity claim rules in the selling market]` [bb89fca1e69e]

### 2.13 The first-order pop-up offer is the most used and most often done wrong
Many brands (his example: Ridge) show standard prices with no discount and run sales now and then. He says that setup "is okay" and can be run well, but "it is going to be really hard to get first orders". Most brands that do add a first-order offer "just throw up a 10% off discount". He says that is not enough. [1f0ee6e31e5c]

### 2.14 Test offers in the pop-up; the incumbent may be the weak one
An 8-figure client had run "a free gift with your first order" for years and did not want to change it. His agency ran an 8-variant pop-up test on equal traffic (free gift, different percents, different dollar-off amounts, cash back, a different free gift, a bundle), cut it to 4, and "$25 in credit" won by a wide margin. Full numbers are in section 7. Lesson in his words: the offer "is one of the biggest levers that you can pull on a business". Condition: 1 client test. [db90a9c32f90] [e54276a5821f]

### 2.15 Use dollar off above about $100 AOV and percent off below it
"In most cases, if your AOV is above $100, your dollar off offer is going to perform better. But if your AOV is under $100, then your 20% off offer is going to convert better." Reason: people compare the 2 numbers. At $50 AOV, 10% is $5; "10 is the higher number than 5". At $200 AOV, 10% is $20; "$20 off sounds a lot better". He calls switching a high-AOV brand from percent to dollar off "a really big lever" his agency pulls on arrival. He says to start testing with plain percent off vs dollar off. Condition: his heuristic, not a benchmark. Very high AOV may make dollar framing look small; retest as price moves (card caveat). The threshold is in USD; no local-currency version is given `[needs brand data: AOV in local currency]`. [febdce2ad8fb]

### 2.16 Mystery framing lifts opt-in
"One of my favorites is the mystery discount." Wrap any offer (percent, dollar or gift) as "you've got a mystery offer". "That mystery really gets people enticed to want to opt in. We see really good results from that." A close cousin is "exclusive discount", also hidden. In his pop-up test the mystery variant (actually $25 off) beat the free gift and 10% off on opt-ins and placed-order rate, but lost to the explicit "$25 in credit". Condition: evidence from 1 brand's test. If the revealed value feels small, the framing can hurt trust (card caveat). Card addition: reveal the value in the follow-up message; the delivery sequence is 14-EML. [e54276a5821f]

### 2.17 Frame dollar off as store credit
"You could frame Dollar Off as store credit." The winning pop-up said "you've got $25 in credit". Condition: 1 test. Card caveat: store credit only works if you can predict redemption and breakage; otherwise it is a future liability. The source does not say whether the $25 was a real credit balance or a $25-off code. [db90a9c32f90] [e54276a5821f]

### 2.18 Unusual percent values are a framing option
Percent off "sounds pretty basic", but "you could do a random number like 18%. I've even seen brands do something like 17.5%." Condition: he gives no test result for odd numbers. It sits inside his own rule that swapping percentages alone is discount testing (2.1), so treat odd values as a framing test, not a structure test. [51d44fdc7df5]

### 2.19 Only 4 first-order offer types are worth running
His shortlist: dollar off, percent off, free gift (which includes buy 1 get 1 and buy 2 get 1), and cash back. "I wouldn't recommend anything else." He is "not a fan of giveaways" ("low-quality leads and they don't really convert") and does not "typically recommend free guides" ("you don't have high intent around that. People just buy from offers and discounts"). Condition: his agency's view. Content-led or community offers may suit long-consideration categories (card caveat). [1f0ee6e31e5c]

### 2.20 Stack the site's existing discount into the first-order headline
"Use the offer that's already on your website and stack that with your first order discount." Advertise the combined value as 1 dollar number. Do not let the site offer and the pop-up offer conflict: "Make sure you don't have conflicting offers with your website and then also with your pop-up form first order. And make sure you combine them so you have the juiciest offer available." Worked example in section 7 (hemp cream). Condition: the combined claim must hold at checkout, and stacking an evergreen discount with a welcome code deepens the real first-order discount, so check margin (card caveats). [9e01130cee63]

### 2.21 Judge offers on gross profit and 90-day LTV, not first-order results
He reads opt-ins, placed-order rate and revenue per variant, then says to look at gross profit, because a free gift and a discount cost different amounts. He closes: "measure your 90-day LTV cuz maybe an offer works better on the first purchase, but it gets repeat purchases really low." Track the downstream effects, not just the first order. Condition: he gives no 90-day LTV result for his own test. Card addition: match the LTV window to the product's repeat cycle. `[needs brand data: repeat cycle length, gift COGS, discount cost]` [89fd70abc2ef] [db90a9c32f90]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Build a front-loaded subscription offer (subscription or consumable brand)
Source 07 gives 2 explicit steps and a model menu. Steps 3 to 6 are from the cards and his examples.
1. Front-load the discount. Replace "subscribe and save X% every order" with a high first-order discount and a low recurring one. His recommendation: 25% off the first order ("subscribe and save 25%" as the headline), 10% recurring shown below it. Aggressive example: Armra, 30% first, 5% recurring. [971d6e5bf0ce]
2. Add strategic free gifts that help the customer use the product (see 3.2). [494b95673d4f]
3. Make the kit, gifts and best price subscription-only. Price one-time higher. [971d6e5bf0ce] [bbc8899c72f2]
4. Show the one-time price next to the subscription price so the saving is explicit (IM8: $91 vs $130; AG1: one-time $99 vs subscription at 20% off plus $43 of gifts). [bbc8899c72f2]
5. Optional: add a longer-term tier with more value. IM8's 90-day plan: 30% off, $100 welcome kit, a 90-day program and a 90-day money-back guarantee. Its 30-day plan: 20% off, a smaller kit ($81 as transcribed), no program. AG1's 3-month plan: $72 of gifts vs $43 on 1-month. [5ff86e2e0a6f]
6. Test price points, gift sets and terms. Validate on 90-day LTV and gross profit, not first-order margin (see 3.6). [971d6e5bf0ce] [89fd70abc2ef]
`[needs brand data: gross margin at the net first-order price, recurring margin, churn by month, gift COGS]`

### 3.2 Choose welcome-kit gifts
1. List what blocks or degrades use of the product (his coffee example: clumping without a frother). Card step. [494b95673d4f]
2. Pick gifts that remove the block or make use feel special (frother, creamer, metal spoon, app). [494b95673d4f]
3. Prefer items that are low cost to you and high value to the customer. [bbc8899c72f2]
4. State a dollar value for each gift on the page so the stack looks bigger than the discount. [494b95673d4f]
5. Exclude unrelated swag such as tote bags. [494b95673d4f]
6. Test gift sets like any other offer variable, and check whether kit recipients repeat more (card step). [494b95673d4f]
The source skips sourcing, fulfilment and COGS. Route to 18-COG.

### 3.3 Pick a subscription offer model
Source 07's 4 models, weakest to strongest in his ranking (model 4 is not ranked):
1. Basic recurring discount: 10 to 20% off every order, no front-loading, no gifts (examples: "Newtonic" 15%, Bloom 10%).
2. Heavy front-loaded discount: e.g. 30% first, 5% recurring (Armra, Gruns).
3. Welcome kit: recurring discount plus free gifts with stacked value. He calls this "the best and most effective" and "the banger offer".
4. Subscription only: no one-time purchase option.
Then "test these offer types. Test these price points, test the free gifts, and see what works the best." Card caveat: subscription-only can suppress trial in high-consideration or high-price categories. [5ff86e2e0a6f]

### 3.4 Build a bundle offer (apparel, accessories, multi-unit goods)
1. Choose 1 hero product. Bundle more of the same, or the same product in other colors or flavors. Avoid mixed-product bundles. [a4348a9802d1]
2. Set 3 tiers, for example 1, 3 and 6. Step the per-unit price down each tier (True Classic: single $35, 3-pack $23 per item, 6-pack $20 per item, $120 total, "$89" saved). [61cf8d39f4bb]
3. Pre-select the middle tier and treat it as the standard. Do not make the default the largest option. [61cf8d39f4bb]
4. Show per-item price and savings against the single price. [61cf8d39f4bb]
5. Where quantities allow, reframe the discount as a free item: "buy 2 get 1 free" not "33% off 3"; "buy 3 get 2 free". [52bf7bcc54a4]
6. Point all ad messaging at the pre-selected tier ("the staple six-pack"). [61cf8d39f4bb]
7. Optional: add a separate flash-deals page (see 3.5). [bb89fca1e69e]
Page layout and section order route to 07-LPG. Card steps not in the transcript: track bundle AOV and return rate, not just conversion. [a4348a9802d1]

### 3.5 Add a flash-deals page (only on top of an evergreen core offer)
1. Keep the core bundle offer evergreen and behind most ads. [bb89fca1e69e]
2. Build a separate page with a countdown to a fixed time (his example: midnight) and custom deals, for example on overstock. [bb89fca1e69e]
3. Show a claimed or stock signal (example: "79% claimed"). [bb89fca1e69e]
4. Link the core bundle bestseller below the deals. [bb89fca1e69e]
5. Run ads to the flash page. [bb89fca1e69e]
6. Card step: actually refresh the deals, or buyers learn the timer is fake. The source does not know whether True Classic does. `[web check: scarcity and countdown rules in the selling market]` [bb89fca1e69e]

### 3.6 Run a first-order pop-up offer test
1. Gather ideas. Browse competitor and adjacent-brand pop-ups and his named inspiration gallery. Capture exact wording, not just the discount. [51d44fdc7df5] `[web check: pop-up inspiration gallery still live]`
2. Shortlist structurally different offers: free gift, percent off, dollar off, a second dollar amount, cash back, a different free gift, a bundle, mystery framing, store-credit framing. His test used 8 variants. Do not test only discount depths. [db90a9c32f90] [1f0ee6e31e5c] [82ae4d2b081b]
3. As a first test, he recommends plain percent off vs dollar off, sized to the AOV rule in 2.15. [febdce2ad8fb]
4. Give every variant equal traffic. Change only the offer on the pop-up. [db90a9c32f90] [89fd70abc2ef]
5. Cut to the leaders (he went from 8 to 4) and keep running. The source gives no rule for when or how to cut. [db90a9c32f90]
6. Read per variant: opt-ins, placed-order rate, revenue, then gross profit after gift COGS or discount cost. [db90a9c32f90] [89fd70abc2ef]
7. Before rollout, check 90-day LTV for each variant's buyers. [89fd70abc2ef]
8. Roll the winner into the pop-up and the welcome flow (flow build routes to 14-EML). [9e01130cee63]

### 3.7 Stack the site offer into the first-order headline
1. List every live discount: site-wide, subscription savings, bundle savings, evergreen promos (card step). [9e01130cee63]
2. Convert each to a dollar value at the product's price or AOV. [9e01130cee63]
3. Confirm which discounts actually combine at checkout. [9e01130cee63] `[verify UI]` for the store platform's discount-combination settings.
4. Advertise the total as 1 dollar figure in the pop-up and welcome flow, or "up to $X off your first order" if there is a further stackable layer. [9e01130cee63]
5. Remove any conflicting offer between site and pop-up. [9e01130cee63] [1f0ee6e31e5c]
6. Recheck first-order margin at the full stacked discount. [9e01130cee63]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Slapping on "10% off" and leaving it; running an always-on 20% sale; giving a discount only for an email. [82ae4d2b081b] [1f0ee6e31e5c]
- Testing only discount depth (10 vs 20 vs 30%) and calling it offer testing. [82ae4d2b081b]
- Going to 50% or 90% off for quick sales that do not last. [82ae4d2b081b]
- A flat "subscribe and save 15%" as the whole subscription offer. [971d6e5bf0ce]
- Unrelated free gifts such as tote bags ("do not offer stupid free gifts"). [494b95673d4f]
- Driving all traffic to 1 single product in apparel instead of a bundle. [61cf8d39f4bb]
- Making the largest bundle the default. [61cf8d39f4bb]
- Mixed-product bundles the buyer only half wants. [a4348a9802d1]
- Leading with "33% off" when "get 1 free" is available. [52bf7bcc54a4]
- Percent off at high AOV (above about $100). [febdce2ad8fb]
- Giveaways and free guides as first-order hooks. [1f0ee6e31e5c]
- Advertising only the welcome code while a bigger site discount also applies; conflicting offers between site and pop-up. [9e01130cee63]
- Keeping an incumbent offer for years without testing it. [db90a9c32f90]
- Judging a test on first-order results only; an offer can win the first order and lower repeat purchases. [89fd70abc2ef]
- Comparing revenue instead of gross profit when gift and discount costs differ. [db90a9c32f90]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- Possible duplicate: source 06 in this folder has a near-identical title. This file was written without reading 06. Given the title, it is likely the same talk, a re-upload or a close cut. Pass 2 should compare the 2 and count shared numbers ($226 vs $138, the 8-variant pop-up test, True Classic 6-pack, Armra 30/5) once, not as 2 agreeing sources.
- Commercial bias: the talk sells an email and retention agency, its analytics platform, a paid community, and praises 1 pop-up tool. Every case result comes from his own clients or screenshots. No sample sizes beyond opt-in counts, no confidence levels, no control groups.
- Internal inconsistencies:
  - Stacking arithmetic does not reconcile. If 15% = $25, the price is about $167 (25 / 0.15). Then 25% is about $42, not $35, and 35% is about $58. He moves between "$35", "almost $50", "up to $50" and "like $60". He also adds percentages (15 + 10 = 25), but stacked percent discounts usually compound: 1 - (0.85 x 0.90) = 23.5%, and with a further 10%, 1 - (0.85 x 0.90 x 0.90) = 31.15%. Whether they add or compound depends on the checkout platform `[verify UI]`. Do the math in code and only advertise what checkout gives.
  - Store revenue lift is stated as $200K and then about $400K.
  - Agency size is stated as 300+ brands and as 100+ brands.
  - The pop-up test's "$25 off" and mystery variants are not cleanly separated in the transcript.
- Rule of thumb vs his own rule: he calls odd-percent ideas (18%, 17.5%) useful, while also saying pure discount-depth testing is not offer testing. Treat odd numbers as a framing test, low priority.
- Card additions not in the transcript: "keep the normal-price path visible" [82ae4d2b081b]; "make the largest tier visually heavier" [61cf8d39f4bb], which conflicts with the transcript's point that the default is the middle tier because the largest feels scary; "reveal the value in the follow-up" [e54276a5821f]; "one core offer per segment" [1f0ee6e31e5c]; "match the LTV window to the repeat cycle" [89fd70abc2ef]; store-credit breakage and liability [db90a9c32f90]; returns risk on larger default bundles [61cf8d39f4bb]. They are sensible but are card-writer inferences.
- Conflicts with common ecommerce advice:
  - "Raise your price, then discount down" and permanent strike-through anchors conflict with reference-price rules in many markets (for example rules that the "was" price must be a real recent selling price) `[web check: reference-price rules in the selling market]`. Common advice warns against fake anchors; the source does not.
  - Countdown timers and "79% claimed" bars risk fake-urgency rules if not real `[web check: dark-pattern and scarcity rules in the selling market]`.
  - A deep front-loaded subscription discount is often warned against because it can attract buyers who cancel after order 1. The source's 90-day LTV check is the guard, but he shows no churn data for front-loaded offers.
  - Subscriber LTV gaps partly reflect self-selection; the source treats the gap as caused by subscription.
  - The $100 dollar-vs-percent threshold matches the widely cited "rule of 100" in pricing psychology, so it agrees with common advice rather than conflicting `[web check: rule of 100 origin]`.
  - Subscription-only (model 4) cuts trial; many operators keep a one-time option for new buyers.
- What is missing: minimum traffic or orders per variant, test length, stopping rules, how to cut 8 variants to 4, margin and break-even math for front-loaded discounts and kits, churn after the first subscription order, how to deliver and track store credit, gift fulfilment, return rates on bundles, and any non-USD guidance. Welcome-flow build is routed to 14-EML, page layout to 07-LPG, kit unit economics to 18-COG.
- Currency: all thresholds are USD. For a brand selling in another currency, convert AOV before applying the $100 rule, and note that the rule was not tested outside USD pricing `[needs brand data: AOV and price points in local currency]`.
