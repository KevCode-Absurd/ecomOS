## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator design a front-end acquisition offer that lifts conversion and units per order without giving away margin, and prove it with margin math before launch. It gives: an offer vs discount definition (5 variables), the waterfall to model a discount (break-even ROAS, target CPA, contribution profit per order), why required ROAS climbs steeply with discount depth, the 5 offer mechanics with worked economics, a stacked evergreen offer example, upsell and cross-sell scoring and placement, post-purchase sequencing, exit-intent offer logic by funnel stage, a 4-question selection framework and 7 failure modes. It is strong on economics and judgment. It is thin on build steps (no apps, no settings, no test design, no sample sizes) and has no measured results.

## 2. Principles

1. **Most "ad problems" are offer problems, and most "offer problems" are discounting problems.** The offer is "the single biggest change" an operator can make for the largest lift "with practically zero cost": same creative, same site, same cost to acquire, but higher conversion rate and AOV. Condition: his opinion, no data. When a brand says it has an ad problem, check the offer first; when it says offer problem, check for habitual discounting. [45e7a80365bc] [6b7177266bbe]
2. **Hierarchy of influences (his framework, lowest to highest leverage): budget, account structure, creative, offer, brand, product.** Budget is fuel; more budget sells more only if the rest works. Account structure can let everything down (retargeting the same people, pushing the wrong products). Creative is how people hear about you. Offer is what they get and why now. Brand is why they buy from you, not a competitor. Product is why they buy at all, and matters most at bottom of funnel and repeat purchase. Higher levers give bigger deltas but are harder to change, so teams spend time on budget and structure. Condition: his ranking, not measured. Card caveat: a strong offer does not excuse broken account structure. The curator brief says to skip this framework; it is kept here only as context. [45e7a80365bc]
3. **An offer is not a discount.** A discount is a percentage off, a coupon code or a store-wide markdown. An offer has 5 variables: [a3756b7bf5f1] [5a6c2fe3c283] [6b7177266bbe]
   - (1) Product positioning: what problem it solves, for whom, and why right now.
   - (2) Price architecture: percentage off, threshold offer, units per transaction (UPT) offer, bundle structure. Example he gives: discount 20% but force UPT to 3 to 4 so there is no gross margin compression.
   - (3) Value mechanic: free shipping, free gift, buy one get one, samples, second-order tier, subscription unlock.
   - (4) Risk reversal: returns, guarantee, trial period, sample-only purchase.
   - (5) Urgency: a drop, a seasonal tie-in, a window before stock runs out.
   A discount only fills the price architecture slot. The other 4 can carry an offer with no discount at all.
4. **Value is a perceived gap between the business's cost and what the buyer gets.** The buyer's view: an offer is good when it looks like the business cannot be making money ("that's got to be cost of goods"). That is why a blanket 50% or 60% off works. The business's view is the inverse: get people to spend as much as possible above COGS while they feel they pay near COGS. Best case is real value for both, such as a brand that owns software giving free licences that cost it nothing to deliver. Condition: his framing, "in my opinion", not a tested model. [0c2ae5495d35] [a3756b7bf5f1]
5. **Flat discounting re-anchors your price.** Too much flat discounting trains customers that you are always on sale, you become a discount brand, and margin compresses permanently. His example: launch at 60% gross profit, end up at 45% because you must always discount to get repeat orders. Weekly promo rotation across a 52 week calendar is "the complete wrong definition" of offers. [a3756b7bf5f1] [0c2ae5495d35] [8074b0c6c718]
6. **Know the full-price waterfall for every product before any offer.** Price, all-in COGS (including shipping, fulfilment, transaction fees), gross margin, break-even ROAS, target CPA, contribution profit per order. "Every single brand should know this exact waterfall for every single product." Worked numbers in section 7. `[needs brand data: all-in COGS per SKU including shipping, 3PL and payment fees; current target ROAS or CPA]` [0b43c974ae3c] [9fe82ea9bb52]
7. **Discounts raise UPT, but COGS is paid on retail value, not discounted value.** Modelling that ignores the UPT lift understates AOV; modelling that applies COGS % to the discounted AOV overstates margin. Net result in his example: revenue per order rises, gross margin falls, and the ROAS needed to hold the same profit rises by about 50%. [0b43c974ae3c] [9fe82ea9bb52]
8. **Required target ROAS rises much faster than break-even ROAS as discount deepens.** Margin falls to your profit target (for example $40 contribution profit) much sooner than it falls to $0. So a 15% vs 25% discount can mean something like an 8x vs a 20x required ROAS in his illustration, which makes deep discounts "inapplicable". He calls break-even growth "linear" and target growth "exponential"; see section 9 on that wording. [99f79f8d840d] [9fe82ea9bb52]
9. **Small discounts can lose money.** At 5% to 10% off, nobody adds more to cart, so price falls with no UPT offset and contribution profit per order drops. Past an inflection point, UPT starts to rise. Contribution profit is a function of average unit retail (falling) and UPT (rising). Past about 30% to 40%, it is "not worth it for anyone". The best headline discount is where contribution profit per order peaks; for his illustrated brand that was 20%. Condition: needs your own history of flat-discount periods. `[needs brand data: UPT and contribution profit per order for each past flat-discount depth]` [99f79f8d840d]
10. **Sale efficiency lift is brand-specific.** Luxury brands that never discount: he has modelled 70% to 80% efficiency improvements during sales. Saturated brands known for discounting: about 5%. Model your own expected lift before you trust a discount. `[needs brand data: ROAS or CPA during past sales vs non-sale periods, and discount frequency]` [0b43c974ae3c]
11. **The offer starts with the customer, not with your margin.** "If you do that, you'll just fail because nobody cares what you want." Ask customers what held them back, what worried them, and what they would like to see. Build the added value on the biggest objection. `[needs brand data: customer surveys, pre-purchase objections, reviews]` [0afa90fbaa08] [42ec3370c194]
12. **Simple scales.** Layer in meaningful value, but subtract anything customers do not care about. Stacking too much becomes "too much for the consumer". [2105cc48bb5d] [42ec3370c194]
13. **Evergreen offers beat rotating promos.** A well-built stacked offer can run all year because it lowers the efficiency you must hit. His example drops required ROAS from 6x to about 2.9x at the same contribution profit (section 7). You can take the gain as a lower ROAS target or as more profit per order ($50 or $60 instead of $43). [2105cc48bb5d]
14. **Tie the best part of the offer to a behaviour you want.** In the stacked example, the 25% lifetime discount needs both the 90-day supply and a ticked subscription box. No tick, no offer. This pulls buyers into subscription on a 90-day billing cycle, which he says is where repeat transactions happen and cohorts lift. Urgency can sit on the lifetime rate (only available in this window). Condition: consumables with repeat purchase. [2105cc48bb5d] [858ec2cdad96]
15. **Cross-sells and upsells sit downstream of the main offer.** They are separate from the 5 mechanics. Judge them by incremental contribution profit per order, not take rate, AOV lift or margin alone. [52ef4486583d] [456ea796c7fe]
16. **Match exit-intent generosity to commitment.** Browsers get generous offers with urgency; cart abandoners get small offers; checkout abandoners get no discount, only risk reversal or free shipping. [ec51739ce59d]
17. **New and returning customers need different offers.** New: first purchase economics and getting them in. Returning: deepen cohorts by locking them in for a sustained period. [8290896c9a20]
18. **There are about 100 possible offers, about 10 good ones, and nobody knows the winner until it is tested in market.** Offers should not stay static. Large CPG brands may hold an offer 6 to 12 months while split testing others on dedicated landing pages and funnels, then rotate the winner in. Condition: creative and the rest of the funnel must already be dialled in, or the test is confounded (card caveat). [9e3f61688b5b] [39335b89c471]
19. **Q1 is an offer problem, not a seasonal dip.** Rotate in offers that fit that time of year. Q1 is one of the best times to test offers because conversion rates and market sentiment are suppressed. Condition: his opinion; no data given. [8074b0c6c718]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Model a flat discount before running it

1. Build the full-price waterfall per product: AOV (or price per unit), all-in COGS (assume it includes shipping, fulfilment and transaction fees), gross margin $ and %, break-even ROAS = 1 / gross margin %, target CPA = AOV / target ROAS, contribution profit per order = gross margin minus target CPA. [0b43c974ae3c]
2. Model the discount on price per unit, not per order. 25% off a $100 unit gives $75. [0b43c974ae3c]
3. Estimate the new UPT. Use past sale periods. His example assumes UPT goes from 1.0 to 1.63 (a brand that "never goes on sale"). `[needs brand data: UPT in past sales]` [0b43c974ae3c] [9fe82ea9bb52]
4. New AOV = discounted unit price × UPT. [0b43c974ae3c]
5. New COGS = COGS % × pre-discount retail value of the cart (full price × UPT), not × discounted AOV. [0b43c974ae3c]
6. New gross margin = new AOV minus new COGS. New break-even ROAS = new AOV / new gross margin. [0b43c974ae3c]
7. To hold the same contribution profit per order: allowable CAC = new gross margin minus the old contribution profit target. Required ROAS = new AOV / allowable CAC. [0b43c974ae3c]
8. Compare the required ROAS to the old target and ask: can we realistically be that much more efficient? Use your own history of sale-period efficiency lift (principle 10). If not, use a non-discount mechanic. [0b43c974ae3c] [9fe82ea9bb52] [f8754813a6a1]
9. Hand the new target CPA and ROAS to the paid team so they do not spend against the old target (card adds this step explicitly; it follows from the talk). [9fe82ea9bb52]

### 3.2 Find the best discount depth from your own history

1. Collect past flat-discount periods at different depths with UPT, average unit retail and contribution profit per order. Data must be consistent enough over time; messy history makes this unreliable. [99f79f8d840d]
2. Plot discount rate (x axis, 10% to 50%) against contribution profit per order (y axis). [99f79f8d840d]
3. Expect profit to dip at 5% to 10% (no UPT lift), rise after the point where buyers start to care, then fall again as margin loss outweighs UPT. [99f79f8d840d]
4. Headline the discount at the peak. His illustration: 20%. [99f79f8d840d]
5. Separately plot required target ROAS against discount depth at your profit target. Rule out any depth whose required ROAS you cannot hit. [99f79f8d840d] [f8754813a6a1]
6. The source says the agency has tooling for this and will sometimes model it for brands; the calculator link is in the video description. `[web check: whether the presenter's discount-to-efficiency calculator is still available]` [f8754813a6a1]

### 3.3 Run a disciplined flat discount (mechanic 5)

1. Only run it for: (a) a few SKUs with very high gross margin, or (b) grade C inventory that is not moving and must be turned back into cash. [2b9021591176]
2. Model the AOV and UPT lift from previous discounts so you do not over-discount (3.2). [2b9021591176]
3. Model the target ROAS needed at the new discount and judge whether the platforms can hit it. [2b9021591176]
4. Run flat discounts rarely: 1 to 2 times a year, to protect brand equity and price perception. Condition: his guardrail, not tested. [2b9021591176]
5. Purpose check: turning stock into cash is valid; rescuing top-line revenue is not. Brands that must discount to hit a revenue number "will always have worse margins" and worse cash flow. [2b9021591176]

### 3.4 Reposition a hero SKU into many offers (mechanic 1)

1. List the personas, problems and mechanisms the same SKU can serve. His example for Gruns daily greens gummies: gut health for bloating, GLP-1 support, fibre for digestion, hair health, cognition and focus, multivitamin for business professionals ("and they have way more"). [ec3b35527187]
2. Test these as personas and concepts in ads; find the ones that work best in the account. `[needs brand data: angle or persona performance from the ad account]` [ec3b35527187]
3. For a high-performing angle, build a dedicated landing page and craft the offer around it: adjust the product title and positioning to the angle; the SKU stays the same. [ec3b35527187]
4. Add partner value that costs you nothing. His idea: on the GLP-1 page, if the buyer takes a subscription on 90-day billing, they get 25% off GLP-1s with a partner provider; ask the partner for that discount and a cut (for example 20%) on future purchases, so the offer may make money. Condition: his idea, compliance unknown. `[web check: health claim and partner referral rules for the category and market]` [ec3b35527187] [0c2ae5495d35]
5. Use the product's result timeline as offer structure. His idea: if hair results are only measurable after 90 to 120 days, say so plainly, then add a free extra 30 days to the 90-day subscription. Deliver it as 1 free unit inside the second 90-day order (about 33% off that second order), not a 4th unit in the first order, to protect first-order margin. Condition: only if studies and customer evidence support the timeline. `[needs brand data: evidence for any result timeline claimed]` [ec3b35527187]
6. Margin impact of repositioning is nil or positive. Best on 1 hero SKU. Without repositioning, offers stay broad and generic (a GLP-1 add-on is irrelevant to the roughly 90% of buyers who are not on GLP-1s, by his guess). [ec3b35527187]

### 3.5 Build a bundle (mechanic 2)

1. Use it if you have multiple SKUs, or a consumable you can sell as multiple units of the same SKU (for example 30-day to 90-day supply). A single-SKU, non-consumable brand cannot really use it. [6a0ef806927d]
2. Expect UPT to rise by design. The cost saving comes from shipping and fulfilment, not unit cost. [6a0ef806927d]
3. Model shipping cost per units in the box, and compare to the shipping cost baked into the per-unit price. His numbers: 1 unit ships for $12, 2 for $14, 3 for $17; price carries 3 × $12 = $36, so a 3-pack saves $19. `[needs brand data: shipping and 3PL cost by units per order]` [6a0ef806927d] [2105cc48bb5d]
4. Run the discount economics (3.1) on the bundle. His example of a common bundle: buy 2 board games, get 25% off. [6a0ef806927d]
5. Treat bundles as the simple, obvious offer. He says most interview candidates answer "a good offer" with "high discount" or "bundle"; bundles protect contribution margin, but you "could do a lot more". [6a0ef806927d]

### 3.6 Build a gift with purchase (mechanic 3)

1. Use it if you have a very low-cost secondary SKU that pairs well and can be given away. It pushes UPT up with near-zero margin impact. [0afa90fbaa08]
2. Model base vs offer. His beach towel example is in section 7: contribution margin doubles and break-even ROAS falls slightly. [0afa90fbaa08]
3. Check that the gift is convincing: "do people really care about getting a bag?" Base the gift on customer research. [0afa90fbaa08] [8074b0c6c718]
4. Raise perceived value of the gift by selling it on the site. His idea: list the bag at $60 and present "2 towels, $160, now $100" (about 40% off) instead of "$100 plus a free bag". `[web check: reference-price and "was/now" rules in the brand's market before showing a struck-through total]` [0afa90fbaa08]
5. Put the biggest objection's fix on the higher-AOV tier, not the base. His example: buyers fear towels will not last (kids ruin towels), so add a lifetime guarantee only when buying 2. This lifts conversion on the bigger offer specifically. Condition: only if that objection is real; a lifetime guarantee means nothing for a consumable like creatine gummies. [0afa90fbaa08]
6. Do not bolt on things the audience does not care about, such as a quarterly prize draw for a $5,000 Fiji holiday. [0afa90fbaa08]
7. A mystery gift can cost $0 to $3: sample packs of other products, a drink bottle bought on a large PO (if it fits the box and does not raise shipping), or a co-marketing partner's sample supplied free. He leaves a gift this cheap out of COGS. [2105cc48bb5d]

### 3.7 Set tiered threshold offers (mechanic 4)

1. Export order values for all orders. Do not use the single AOV figure Shopify reports. `[needs brand data: order-level order values]` [a1a57841df7a] [c9081fedfb43] [f0cd279bc44b]
2. Plot the distribution (count of orders by order-value band). [a1a57841df7a]
3. Check for 2 peaks ("binomial"; the statistical term is bimodal). Common where the current offer is "buy 1 or buy 3", and in fashion or wide catalogues where carts are built freely. [a1a57841df7a]
4. If there are 2 peaks, set 1 offer aimed at each peak to shift each one up. Use different mechanics per peak if useful (for example a gift with purchase for one peak, a different mechanic for the other). [a1a57841df7a] [9e3f61688b5b]
5. If there is 1 peak, focus on moving the core audience up one step, for example from a 30-day to a 90-day supply. [c9081fedfb43] [9e3f61688b5b]
6. Place the threshold unlock in the cart: a progress bar at the top ("add one more product and get X"), with samples or a complementary product unlocked at the threshold. [fa0ea3889627] [3ddf9722dfa2]
7. Card adds (not in the talk): set each threshold just above the next peak, avoid setting it between peaks, re-plot after any price, bundle or threshold change, and confirm each threshold still clears margin after the gift or shipping cost. [f0cd279bc44b]

### 3.8 Build a stacked evergreen offer (his supplement example)

1. Start from the product's natural larger pack (90-day supply) and headline a moderate discount (25% off). [2105cc48bb5d]
2. Add near-zero-cost value: free access to the brand's app with daily meal plans; a free mystery gift. [2105cc48bb5d]
3. Add a lifetime subscription discount (25% for as long as they stay subscribed, double the normal 10%), gated on ticking the subscription box. Add urgency on the lifetime rate. [2105cc48bb5d] [858ec2cdad96]
4. Model it with the waterfall, including shipping savings from 3 units (section 7). [2105cc48bb5d]
5. Run it 90 to 120 days, then survey buyers on which addition convinced them. [42ec3370c194]
6. Remove parts nobody cares about (for example the mystery gift) and test whether conversion changes. [42ec3370c194]
7. Check back-end effects: if a bonus (the app) drove sales but customers found it useless ("a bunch of chatbot meal plans"), it can hurt brand perception and retention revenue. Fix the deliverable or remove it. [42ec3370c194]
8. Repeat the cycle to raise first-purchase profit, lower CAC and improve retention together. The source gives no sample size or test length for these tests. [42ec3370c194]

### 3.9 Score and choose an upsell or cross-sell

1. Define terms. Cross-sell: a different, complementary product (t-shirt, then matching socks or shorts); best for multi-SKU brands. Upsell: more of the same (1-month supply, then 3-month supply with a discount). [08129edc97d2]
2. For each candidate, get take rate % (share of exposed customers who take it), the added order value of the item, and its gross margin %. `[needs brand data: take rate and margin by upsell SKU and placement]` [52ef4486583d] [456ea796c7fe]
3. Incremental contribution profit per order = take rate % × added order value × gross margin %. [52ef4486583d] [456ea796c7fe] [67ab77318d00]
4. Rank candidates on that product, not on any single input. [52ef4486583d]
5. Feed the result into acquisition: if you make $20 contribution profit per order, a $2 upsell gain is over 10%, so allowable CAC can rise about $2. [52ef4486583d]
6. Card adds: measure AOV lift against a non-exposed group or holdout, because buyers who self-select into an upsell overstate lift; re-check after COGS changes. [456ea796c7fe]

### 3.10 Place upsells by stage

1. In cart (medium friction, buyer still deciding): threshold unlocks, the progress bar, samples, or a complementary product at the threshold. Typical take rate 5% to 15%. Upsells here can lower purchase likelihood. [3ddf9722dfa2] [d2c3bb4a5a83]
2. Checkout (higher friction than cart, in his current view): do not sell another full product. Sell a low-friction add-on. His example: shipping protection at $4.99, about 30% take rate, about 100% margin (claims are rare and "normally the carrier will pay"), adding about $1 to $2 gross profit per order. `[needs brand data: shipping protection claim rate and carrier terms]` `[web check: consumer rules on checkout add-ons in the brand's market]` [3ddf9722dfa2] [d2c3bb4a5a83]
3. Post-purchase (near-zero friction, payment captured): set up a one-click add that charges the saved card instantly. "I would always have this." Take rates can reach 35% to 40% in his experience. Almost anything can be sold here; A/B testing pays most at this stage. `[verify UI]` for the platform's post-purchase and one-click support. [caf68780fe4e] [3ddf9722dfa2]
4. Sequence post-purchase offers: anchor high, then down-sell. Show a $50 to $60 upsell first. If declined, down-sell to $20 to $30. If taken, show another upsell slightly lower at $30 to $40. From there, or from a declined first down-sell, flow to down-sell 2. Expect declining take rates (for example 25% on the first, 10% on the next). Most brands lead with the cheapest offer, which anchors at $20 and makes every later offer look expensive. [6856ee4fdaf4]
5. He also describes chaining time-limited offers ("next 60 minutes, 30% off", then "because you took this one, 60% off another"). He finds it annoying as a consumer but effective as an advertiser. [6856ee4fdaf4] [caf68780fe4e]
6. Track take rate and incremental contribution profit separately by placement (card adds). [d2c3bb4a5a83]

### 3.11 In-cart upsell rules (his 5 rules)

1. One offer per slot in the drawer cart. 3 offers cause decision fatigue that lowers take rate more than it helps. [0fc1f8211e17] [67ab77318d00]
2. Relevance over price. A good price on an item that does not fit the cart does nothing. [0fc1f8211e17]
3. Favour high-margin SKUs over items that add only $4 to $5 of gross profit, when a relevant high-margin item could sit there. [0fc1f8211e17]
4. Keep it easy to understand. You have about 2 to 3 seconds of legibility, which usually means a flat discount on the added item. This is the opposite of front-end offers, where he wants more complexity. [0fc1f8211e17]
5. Measure by gross margin lift, not AOV lift, or you will over-prioritise low-margin SKUs with higher AOV. [0fc1f8211e17]

### 3.12 Exit-intent and abandoned-flow offers

1. Split exits into 3 stages: browsing, added to basket, reached checkout. [ec51739ce59d]
2. Browsing exit (no commitment, still product aware, not most aware): be generous, give a reason to buy, add urgency. [ec51739ce59d]
3. Basket exit (shown intent, may buy in 1 to 2 days): give a small offer only. [ec51739ce59d]
4. Checkout exit (friction stopped them): no discount. Offer risk reversal, a reminder, or free shipping. Be sceptical of giving margin; they will likely buy in 2 to 3 days. [ec51739ce59d]
5. He dislikes exit pop-ups as "gimmicky" and prefers abandoned-flow emails for the same job. The source gives no conversion benchmarks for these flows. [ec51739ce59d]

### 3.13 Select the right offer (his 4 questions)

1. What is the gross margin floor? Model whether the offer improves profit or just erodes margin. [9e3f61688b5b]
2. What does the AOV distribution look like? 2 peaks: split the offer across both thresholds. 1 peak: move the core audience up (30-day to 90-day). [9e3f61688b5b] [c9081fedfb43]
3. Single hero SKU or multi-SKU? Single: repositioning first, then gift with purchase, and disciplined discounting. Multi: native bundles, cross-category upsells with bundles, threshold mechanics that unlock products. [9e3f61688b5b] [9e7e3b4c4f44]
4. What is the buyer's repeat behaviour? With repeats: build subscription, gift presentation and lock-in periods into the front-end offer, timed to the purchase cycle. With little repeat: protect gross margin on the first order, since that is where most gross margin comes from. `[needs brand data: repeat rate and time between orders]` [9e3f61688b5b] [9e7e3b4c4f44]
5. Test in market on dedicated landing pages and funnels, then survey customers to iterate. [9e3f61688b5b] [39335b89c471] [6b7177266bbe]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Calling a blank discount an offer ("our offer this period is 25% off"). [8074b0c6c718] [6b7177266bbe]
2. Miscalculating gross margin: using COGS only (missing shipping and fulfilment, which can improve with bundles and higher UPT), or not knowing how margin sets efficiency targets, then discounting deeper than paid media can ever support. [8074b0c6c718]
3. Running the same offer to new and returning customers. [8074b0c6c718] [8290896c9a20]
4. Setting thresholds on mean AOV instead of the distribution. [8074b0c6c718] [c9081fedfb43]
5. The wrong gift: one customers do not care about (no change in take rate), or one that does not change the economics enough to matter. "It was just thrown in because gift with purchase sounds good." [8074b0c6c718] [0afa90fbaa08]
6. A year-round promo calendar rotating percentage discounts across categories and calling it offer rotation (he says "52 weeks" early and "25 week" later; the point is the same). [8074b0c6c718] [a3756b7bf5f1]
7. Treating Q1 as a seasonal dip instead of an offer problem. [8074b0c6c718]
8. Designing the offer around your margin instead of what the customer wants. [0afa90fbaa08]
9. Stacking so much into an offer that it becomes too much for the buyer. [2105cc48bb5d]
10. Keeping a front-end bonus that sells but disappoints, hurting retention. [42ec3370c194]
11. Token 5% to 10% discounts that cut price without lifting UPT. [99f79f8d840d]
12. Modelling a discount without the UPT lift, or with COGS on the discounted price. [0b43c974ae3c]
13. Discounting to rescue top-line revenue rather than to clear stuck stock. [2b9021591176]
14. Pushing the highest-margin product as the upsell just for margin, when fit drives take rate (but see the conflict in section 9). [52ef4486583d]
15. Judging upsells by take rate, AOV lift or margin alone. [52ef4486583d] [0fc1f8211e17]
16. Several offers in one cart slot. [0fc1f8211e17]
17. Selling full products at checkout. [3ddf9722dfa2]
18. Leading post-purchase with the cheapest offer. [6856ee4fdaf4]
19. Giving checkout abandoners a discount they did not need. [ec51739ce59d]
20. Teams spending their time on budget and account structure instead of creative, offer, brand and product. [45e7a80365bc]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Upsell example contradicts itself.** He says pushing the highest-margin product is a "massive mistake", then his A vs B example concludes the high-margin, half-take-rate option wins, but his stated inputs make the low-margin option win ($2.40 vs $1.80). Card 52ef4486583d also computes A as $1.60, which is wrong. His in-cart rule 3 says favour high-margin SKUs, while rule 2 says relevance over price. Agent rule: compute take rate × added value × margin with real numbers and let that decide; do not rely on the example's conclusion. [52ef4486583d] [0fc1f8211e17] [67ab77318d00]
- **Break-even curve wording.** He says break-even ROAS rises "linearly" with discount depth, then says it reaches infinity when margin runs out. Break-even ROAS = 1 / remaining margin %, so it curves upward, not a straight line. Target ROAS curves up faster because margin hits the profit target sooner. His "exponential" is a loose word for this steep curve. The ranking (target rises faster than break-even) holds.
- **Small sums off.** Break-even ROAS in the 25% sale is 2.22 as spoken but about 2.14 from his inputs; "nearly 50%" margin loss is about 42%. Conclusions do not change.
- **Stacked offer baseline is mixed.** The $43.33 profit target comes from the $100 single-unit baseline, applied to a $190 3-unit order. That is fair as "same profit per order" but it is not a like-for-like product comparison. The "2.5x AOV" and "double repeat purchase" figures are guesses.
- **"Binomial" means two-peaked (bimodal).** The card caveat notes the shape depends on your catalogue.
- **Card fa0ea3889627 misreads the talk.** It says "shipping is close to 100% margin". The talk says shipping protection (an add-on) is close to 100% margin. Its title says 6 mechanics; it lists 4.
- **Card additions not in the talk:** thresholds "just above the next peak" and "avoid between peaks" [f0cd279bc44b]; buy box presentation, keeping the one-time path honest, dunning, pause and skip [858ec2cdad96]; expedited delivery and small warranties as checkout add-ons [d2c3bb4a5a83]; holdout groups for upsell lift [456ea796c7fe]; "bundle for a named occasion" and "sell the outcome" [30b32ff526f1]. Treat as reasonable extensions, not source claims.
- **Congruence is the curator's baseline,** not the presenter's. Cards 5a6c2fe3c283 and a3756b7bf5f1 include it from the ingestion brief. The curator brief also says to skip the hierarchy framework and 52-week promo calendar advice.
- **Checkout vs cart friction is his changed opinion.** He used to think the reverse. No data given. Card caveat: test on your own catalogue. [3ddf9722dfa2] [d2c3bb4a5a83]
- **Reference pricing and claims risk.** Showing a gift at a list price to present "$160, now $100", "lifetime" discounts, GLP-1 and hair-health angles, and time-limited post-purchase offers may face consumer or health-claim rules. He flags compliance only for GLP-1. `[web check: reference pricing, subscription auto-renewal, pre-selected add-on and health claim rules in the brand's market]`
- **Chained post-purchase offers** are effective for the advertiser but annoying to buyers, by his own account. The brand should decide whether this fits its voice.
- **Missing how:** no apps or settings, no test design (sample size, duration, stopping rule) for offer tests on dedicated landing pages, no survey questions beyond "what held you back", no method to estimate UPT lift without past sale data, no retention data behind the subscription claims, no benchmarks for exit-intent flows, no guidance on risk reversal and urgency beyond naming them, no referral programme or price anchoring detail beyond the gift list-price idea.
- **Currency and market.** All figures are dollars (likely AUD or USD) with round numbers. Take-rate and RPV ranges come from his market and clients. `[needs brand data: the brand's own take rates, AOV distribution, UPT history and margins before using any threshold here]`
