## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a complete method to build and present a "Grand Slam Offer": an offer that cannot be compared to competitors, so the buyer decides on value, not price. It covers market choice, premium pricing logic, the Value Equation, a 5-step build (dream outcome, problems, solutions, delivery vehicles, trim and stack), and 5 enhancers (scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, naming), plus an order for refreshing a tired offer. For an ecommerce operator it is best used as a thinking frame for bundles, kits, launch drops, guarantees, promo calendars and offer names. Its numbers come from service businesses and are not ecommerce benchmarks.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 Offers, commodities and growth

1. **The offer is the start of every transaction.** Source defines an offer as the goods or services you give, how you take payment, and the terms. Bad offer = negative profit. Decent offer = no profit. Good offer = some profit. Grand Slam Offer = "fantastic profit". Condition: applies to any business. Evidence: author opinion. [78a797a2bcce]
2. **There are only 3 ways to grow: more customers, higher average purchase value, more purchases per customer.** An author note collapses this to 2: more customers, and more value per customer (profit per purchase plus number of purchases). Example: 10 new clients a month x $1,000 lifetime value = $10,000 a month cap. Condition: the book calls this a cap on monthly revenue; the card notes it is a cohort value, not period revenue. [4de99e1618e1]
3. **"Maintenance is a myth."** Source says the stock market grows 9% a year, so a flat business is falling behind. In a growing market you may need 20% to 30% a year just to keep up. Condition: illustrative, not an ecommerce target (card caveat). [4de99e1618e1]
4. **Commodity = price-driven purchase. Differentiated = value-driven purchase.** A commodity is a product available from many places. If a buyer thinks "these are the same, I'll buy the cheaper one", you are commoditized. Commodity prices fall to "market efficiency", where margins just keep the lights on. Look-alike offers also get weaker ad response because "they are all making the same offer". [61f1c72af108] [7a8d7aa38df0]
5. **A Grand Slam Offer sells in a "category of one".** Definition: an offer that cannot be compared to any other product or service, combining an attractive promotion, an unmatchable value proposition, a premium price, an unbeatable guarantee, and a money model (payment terms) that lets you get paid to acquire customers. The buyer's choice becomes "your product versus nothing". The fulfillment work can stay the same; only the offer changes. [78a797a2bcce] [7a8d7aa38df0]
6. **A Grand Slam Offer moves 3 numbers at once: response rate, conversion, price.** Same ad spend, more people respond, more of them buy, at a higher price. The effects multiply. Evidence: the lead-gen agency case (section 7). Condition: the multiple came from a service example; card says do not treat it as an ecommerce result. [78a797a2bcce]

### 2.2 Market choice

7. **Market matters more than offer, and offer matters more than persuasion.** Order: Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills. Rate each great, normal or bad. A "great" on a higher item overpowers lower items. A "normal" passes the weight to the next item. A "bad" stops everything unless a higher "great" cancels it. Examples: great market + bad offer + bad persuasion still sells (hot dogs at 2am after the bars close). Normal market + Grand Slam Offer makes "tons of money" even with bad persuasion ("most people reading this book"). Normal market + normal offer needs exceptional persuasion (hardest path). Condition: the source's model, not an empirical law (card caveat). [6fdb3e4dcd6e] [c6f5c92f4d60]
8. **You cannot succeed in a bad (shrinking) market.** Evidence: Lloyd's newspaper software had a zero-risk revshare offer and strong sales skill, but the market shrank 25% a year. Same person pivoted to automated mask manufacturing during COVID and did "millions per month" within 5 months. [3e5ddfe3f852]
9. **Screen a market on 4 indicators: massive pain, purchasing power, easy to target, growing.** Pain: they must desperately need it, not just want it; "the degree of the pain will be proportional to the price you will be able to charge". Purchasing power: a resume service failed because buyers were unemployed. Easy to target: associations, mailing lists, social groups, channels they all watch; ads to nursing students will not reach rich doctors. Growing: a tailwind; newspapers had the first 3 but not the 4th. [f1a3973e8921]
10. **"The pain is the pitch."** If you describe the prospect's pain accurately, "they will almost always buy". Pro tip: good writing makes the reader understand; good persuasion makes the prospect feel understood. [f1a3973e8921]
11. **Health, wealth and relationships are the 3 markets that always exist.** Find a subgroup inside one that has pain, money, is easy to find, and is growing. Example: "second half of life" relationship coaching for seniors over college students (more pain, more money, easy to find; "more people turning 65 each year than turning 20" at the time of writing). A "normal" market grows at the marketplace rate and has unmet needs in 1 of the 3 areas. Source says every market he has been in was normal. [a0b03b52542e] `[web check: current demographic claim]`
12. **Pick a niche and commit ("Don't make me niche slap you").** Switching niches after 1 failed offer restarts learning. "If you try one hundred offers, I promise you will succeed." A failed offer means the offer is bad, not you. Condition: does not cover a market with clear evidence of decline. [4efb9577e16a]
13. **Niching raises price for the same product ("riches are in the niches").** Under $10M a year, niching down makes more money for most. Above that, it depends on total addressable market (TAM); you may go up market, down market or into an adjacent market. Companies reached $30M+ a year in single niches (chiropractors, gyms, plumbers, solar, roofers, salon owners). Gym Launch avatar: microgym owner, about 100 members, signed lease, at least 1 employee, wants to help clients lose weight; everyone else is turned away. [4efb9577e16a] [29f874222dd1]
14. **Specificity ladder (credited to Dan Kennedy).** Time management course $19 > "for Sales Professionals" $99 > "for B2B Outbound Sales Reps" $499 ("25x") > "for B2B Outbound Power Tools & Gardening Sales Reps" $1,000 to $2,000. Generic weight loss $19 versus a program for shift nurses $1,997, "even though the core of the program is likely similar". Claim: "you can literally charge 100x more for the exact same product". **[text: service/info]** Condition: illustrative price jumps, not benchmarks (card caveat). [29f874222dd1]

### 2.3 Pricing

15. **People buy to get a deal: value must exceed price.** When value dips below price, they stop buying. The easiest way to widen the gap is to cut price, and that is "most of the time, the wrong decision". Price can only fall to $0 but can rise without limit. [d1d1f8ddde9a]
16. **Do not compete on price unless your costs are 1/10th of competitors'.** Kennedy quote: "There is no strategic benefit to being the second cheapest in the marketplace, but there is for being the most expensive." Raise price only after raising value (example: $100,000 of value for $10,000, "money at a discount"). [d1d1f8ddde9a] [582ef3d59a15]
17. **The common pricing process is a trap.** Look at the market, see offers, take the average, go slightly below, add "a little more", end at "more for less". The competitors you copy "are dead broke". The bottom 10% to 20% of operators wash out, new ones enter, and the cycle repeats. [d1d1f8ddde9a]
18. **Virtuous cycle of price.** Lower price: less emotional investment, lower perceived value, worse results, worst clients ("never satisfied until your service is free"), no margin to serve well. Higher price: the reverse, plus best clients who "cost less to fulfill", and margin to invest in systems, people and experience. Condition: the claim that low-price buyers are worse is the source's framing, not universal (card caveat). [8d158317a17c]
19. **Higher price can raise perceived value.** Evidence: an uncited wine study where tasters rated the same wine by its shown price. Goal: be priced so far above market that buyers think "there must be something entirely different going on here". `[web check: wine price study source and findings]` [50211f248483] [8d158317a17c]
20. **Price so it "stings a little" when customer effort drives the result.** "Those who pay the most, pay the most attention." Condition: only where the customer must do something to get the result, and only if you can deliver ("your product must deliver"). Conviction comes from having done it many times. **[text: service/info]** [8d158317a17c]
21. **Charge far more than your cost to fulfill.** "Think up to a hundred times more, not just two or three times more." But "never charge more than your product is worth", and the buyer should still get "a steal". Examples: phone companies, drug makers and media companies charge far above marginal cost. Condition: card says the 100x spread is not an ecommerce margin benchmark. [b93a4e79ec81]
22. **"99 percent of businesses need to raise their prices to grow, not lower them." "Profit is oxygen."** Condition: strong opinion, not a universal rule (card caveat). [582ef3d59a15]

### 2.4 The Value Equation

23. **Value = (Dream Outcome x Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Perceived Time Delay x Perceived Effort & Sacrifice).** Raise the top 2. Lower the bottom 2. The 4 map to 4 buyer questions: What will I get? How will I know it will happen? How long will it take? What is expected of me? Hormozi says he created it and has "never seen it displayed elsewhere". Condition: a decision heuristic, not literal math (card caveat). [110b031911f0] [0e0a8b7a8e72] [b314811091f7]
24. **Work the bottom of the equation.** Beginners make bigger claims (top side). That is "easy" and "lazy" and less unique, since "anyone can make a promise". The best companies make things immediate, seamless and effortless: iPhone, Amazon 1-click and fast delivery, Netflix. It is a division so that driving time and effort toward 0 drives value toward infinity (conceptual). [0e0a8b7a8e72]
25. **Perception is reality.** Value counts only when the buyer perceives more likelihood, less delay and less effort. Example: on the London Underground, a dotted map showing when the next train comes (a "few million dollars") raised satisfaction more than faster trains (billions). `[web check: London Underground dot-matrix sign claim]` [d3c0e5ca9001]
26. **Look for psychological solutions, not only logical ones (credited to Rory Sutherland).** Logical: faster trains, faster elevators, cheaper product. Psychological: the dotted map, mirrors in elevators, "make fewer of them and raise the price". Logical fixes have usually been tried already. [d3c0e5ca9001]
27. **Dream outcome: channel desire, do not create it.** Common desires: to be seen as beautiful, respected, powerful, loved; to raise status. Many vehicles serve the same desire. For beauty the book lists makeup, anti-aging creams and serums, supplements, shapewear, plastic surgery and fitness **[text: physical]**. The outcome that most raises status is valued most. For many men, money beats looks because money drives their status. Russell Brunson's wife preferred a minivan because it raised her status among other mothers. Condition: status is the source's framing, not every motive (card caveat). [be4f240618d8]
28. **Frame benefits by how others will see the buyer.** Example copy: a golf club that adds 40 yards; "your golf buddies' jaws will drop" **[text: physical]**. [be4f240618d8]
29. **When 2 offers serve the same desire, the dream outcome cancels out.** Likelihood, time and effort then set the price difference ("what makes one worth $50,000 and another $5?"). [0e0a8b7a8e72] [b314811091f7]
30. **People pay for certainty (perceived likelihood).** Would you rather be a plastic surgeon's 10,000th patient or the 1st? Raise likelihood through messaging, proof, what you include or exclude, and guarantees. [2e7527024ff5]
31. **Time delay has 2 parts: the long-term outcome and the short-term experience.** People buy the long-term outcome but stay for early wins. Give "a big emotional win early". Example: Gym Launch gets client ads live and a first $2,000 sale within 7 days. Weight loss: introduce the new client to another member, start with a more aggressive diet. "They buy the dream, but they stay for the benefits they discover along the way." Claimed "backed by science" with no citation (card caveat). [cd466b907220] [2ca08bfedd05]
32. **"Fast beats free."** "The only thing that beats 'free' is 'fast'." Examples: pay $50 to skip the DMV line, FedEx overnight, Spotify, Uber. If you compete against free, "double down on speed". Condition: strong opinion (card caveat). [4d408bb8adbd]
33. **Effort and sacrifice: done-for-you costs more than do-it-yourself.** Less effort, and buyers think an expert raises their odds. Liposuction with tummy tuck $25,000 versus bootcamp $100 a month for the same outcome. [1f9bfab9968a] [7653128fbf28]
34. **Score vehicles 0 or 1 on each driver to compare them.** Example: meditation versus Xanax for "relaxation". Supplements ($123B, Grandview Research) are "twice the size" of health clubs ($62B, IHRSA) because a pill is faster and easier; "people are more willing to pay $200 for supplements than they are a $29/mo membership" **[text: physical]**. Condition: rough heuristic, not a valuation model (card caveat). `[web check: market size figures]` [b314811091f7]

### 2.5 Building the offer

35. **Use divergent thinking to build the offer.** Convergent problems have 1 answer (math). Offers have many right answers and 1 "way more right". Warm up with the 2-minute brick exercise (section 3.2). [81b1872a99e6]
36. **Sell the vacation, not the plane flight.** "No one wants a membership; they want to lose weight." The dream outcome is the buyer arriving at their destination. [adcf603d84ff]
37. **Solve every perceived problem.** "If only one of these needs is missing in a solution, it can cause someone not to buy." Evidence: Hormozi refused clients who ate out, lost sales, then wrote an eating-out guide to close 1 sale and never lost that objection again. "Don't get romantic about how you want to solve the problem." Goal: sell the most people, at the highest price, with the highest margin. [cd4c2dd6a329] [d49905c44a5d]
38. **Sales-to-fulfillment continuum.** Doing less makes the offer harder to sell. Doing more makes it easier to sell but harder to fulfill. Find the spot that sells well and is easy to fulfill. Mantra: "Create flow. Monetize flow. Then add friction." [369bd294c7db]
39. **Over-deliver on your first Grand Slam Offer, then systematize.** "Maybe flying out isn't such a bad idea in the beginning." Use the cash flow to fix operations. The promise can stay the same while the "how" changes (Gym Launch kept "I will fill your gym in 30 days" when it moved from done-for-you to teaching). Condition: launch-phase opinion, not a sustainable default (card caveat). **[text: service/info]** [369bd294c7db]
40. **Favor high-value "one to many" solutions.** They have the biggest gap between cost and value. High one-time creation cost, then near-zero extra cost ("exactly why software becomes so valuable"). Save 1-on-1 for big value adds. [4febc6c2f078] [aeea5b86423d]
41. **A stacked bundle does 3 things.** Solves all perceived problems, gives you conviction it is one of a kind, and makes it impossible to compare with "the one down the street". [de5a81dc7f90] [e141a5bda4f3]

### 2.6 Supply, demand and the enhancers

42. **All marketing moves supply and demand.** More demand sells more units. Less supply sells units for more. "Perfect profit combination": lots of demand, little (perceived) supply. Condition: "assumes a regular business who is not trying to gain mass market penetration". [47afc4b21d24] [b83b87f23486]
43. **Demand is fractal (80/20).** "One fifth of the prospects are willing to pay five times the price (or more)." Keep supply below demand so unmet desire carries into the next round. Satisfy all demand and each later promotion sells fewer units. Condition: illustrative framing, not a demand law (card caveat). **[text: service/info]** (workshop example) [47afc4b21d24]
44. **"Hormozi Law: The longer you delay the ask, the bigger the ask you can make."** "The longer the runway, the bigger the plane that can take off." [47afc4b21d24]
45. **5 enhancers, each with a job.** Scarcity lowers supply to raise price (and raises demand through exclusivity). Urgency raises demand by lowering the prospect's action threshold. Bonuses raise demand and exclusivity. Guarantees raise demand by reversing risk. Names re-stimulate demand and widen awareness. The book says these outside forces "are often more powerful than your core offer". [65ce1e16b1ce]
46. **The person who needs the exchange less has the upper hand.** Hormozi turned down 2 offers of $50,000 for a day because he made more than $50,000 a day in profit. Rarity and value compound to set premium prices. [318cf8333224]
47. **Fear of loss is stronger than desire for gain.** Scarcity uses this. [b83b87f23486] [e456aab8c58a]
48. **Scarcity is quantity. Urgency is time.** They are often combined but are separate levers. [9264ceff1dcd]
49. **Never discount the core offer to close. Add bonuses instead.** Discounting "teaches your customers that your prices are negotiable". Bonuses put you in "a position of strength and goodwill". Condition: strong opinion; card notes it is not a ban on sound markdowns. Note the book itself uses discounts as a promotional "magnet" in names ("88% off", "$2,000-Off") and in pricing urgency (see section 9). [39853dac5920]
50. **A single offer is worth less than the same offer split into parts and stacked as bonuses.** Anchor price to the core offer, then widen the value gap with each bonus. Evidence: infomercials, "every second of air time costs money and must be justified with ROI". [39853dac5920]
51. **Risk is the single greatest objection.** Reversing it is "an immediate way to make any offer more attractive" and "the number one way to increase the conversion of an offer". Spend "a disproportionate amount of time" on the guarantee. Jason Fladlien said he saw conversion rise 2x to 4x from changing only the guarantee's quality (second-hand, no data). [4d72bb91baae] [eaf3e7847805]
52. **Guarantees are enhancers, not a business.** Used to cover a poor sales team or poor product, they backfire into refunds. [eaf3e7847805]
53. **Naming changes the wrapper, not the offer.** Offers fatigue over years, faster in local markets. Rename to refresh. Claim: good naming gets "the same offer 2x, 3x, or 10x the response rate" (no data). [f2d527d24cc0] [b261bc411259]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Screen a market (Chapter 4)

1. List what you are good at in health, wealth or relationships.
2. Name the subgroup in the most pain.
3. Check they can pay your required price (purchasing power).
4. Name where they gather: associations, mailing lists, social groups, channels they all watch (easy to target).
5. Check the market is not shrinking (growing or normal).
6. If 3 criteria are strong and the market is not shrinking, commit. Do not switch niches after 1 failed offer.
7. Rate market, offer and persuasion as great, normal or bad, in that order, to see where the weight falls.
Source is thin on how to measure each indicator. It gives no data sources or thresholds. [f1a3973e8921] [6fdb3e4dcd6e] [a0b03b52542e] `[needs brand data: category demand trend, customer spend, reachable audiences]`

### 3.2 Brick exercise (divergent warm-up, Chapter 8)

1. Set a 120-second timer.
2. Write as many uses for a brick as you can.
3. Then vary the parameters: size (a tab of gum, standard 3-5/8" x 2-1/4" x 8", 2 ft x 2 ft x 6 ft), material (plastic, gold, clay, wood, metal), shape (holes, divots for interlocking).
4. List again. Hormozi's list has 19 uses (paper weight, door stop, fish home, plant holder, trophy, Lego toy, flotation device, payment in gold, and more).
5. Apply the same to your offer: find the buyer's desire, then list every way your "brick" could create value.
[81b1872a99e6]

### 3.3 Build a Grand Slam Offer (Chapters 9 and 10)

**Step 1. Identify the dream outcome.** State the destination, not the vehicle. Example: "Lose 20lbs in 6 weeks" (big outcome plus short time). [adcf603d84ff]

**Step 2. List problems.**
1. List every core thing the customer must do, in the order they meet it (weight loss: buy healthy food, cook it, eat it, exercise).
2. Think about what happens immediately before and after using your product. What is the "next" thing they need help with?
3. For each core task, write the 4 negative beliefs, 1 per value driver:
   - Dream outcome: "This will not be financially worth it."
   - Likelihood: "It won't work for me. I won't be able to stick with it. External factors will get in my way." (the source says this bucket is "the most unique and service-specific")
   - Effort and sacrifice: "Too hard, confusing. I won't like it. I will suck at it."
   - Time: "Too much time. I am too busy. It will take too long. It won't be convenient."
4. Go to "insane detail". The example yields 16 core problems with 2 to 4 sub-problems each, so 32 to 64 problems. "The more problems you think of, the more problems you get to solve."
[d49905c44a5d]

**Step 3. Turn problems into solutions.**
1. For each problem ask "What would I need to show someone to solve this problem?"
2. Reverse the problem into solution language. Starter method: add "how to" and flip the problem. Example: "buying healthy food is expensive" becomes "How to buy healthy food for less than your current grocery bill". "Cooking takes too much time" becomes "How to cook meals in under 5 minutes".
3. Treat the list as a checklist of what you must deliver. "You will solve every problem."
4. "Don't be fancy. Just get all the problems down then turn them into solutions."
[c509fc4145c1]

**Step 4. Create delivery vehicles ("the how").**
1. For each solution, brainstorm everything you could possibly do, even things you would not actually do. Aim for "All that? Seriously? Yes, I'm in."
2. Group ideas by how many people you serve at once: 1-on-1, small group, one-to-many. Grocery example: 6 one-on-one ideas (in-store shopping, personal list, full-service shopping, orientation, text support, phone call while shopping), 4 small group, 6 one-to-many (live virtual tour, recorded tour, DIY grocery calculator, predetermined weekly lists, buddy system, pre-made Instacart carts).
3. Use the 6 "cheat codes" (section 6).
4. Do this for every problem before, during and after use. "You only need to do this once."
**[text: service/info]** The delivery-vehicle grid is built for services. One idea (pre-made Instacart carts) touches physical delivery. [aeea5b86423d]

**Step 5a. Trim.**
1. Estimate the cost to you of each delivery vehicle.
2. Remove high cost, low value first.
3. Then remove low cost, low value.
4. Test "value" with the 4 drivers: will they financially value it; will it make them believe they will succeed; will it cut effort and sacrifice; will it cut time.
5. Keep low cost/high value and high cost/high value.
6. If something is too costly (moving in with the client), step back 1 level at a time to a version you can live with, or raise the price enough to make it worth it.
[4febc6c2f078] [e141a5bda4f3]

**Step 5b. Stack.**
1. Write each set as: Problem > Solution wording > sexier bundle name > the actual delivery vehicles underneath.
2. Give each named bundle a benefit line and a stated value (example: "Foolproof Bargain Grocery System ... ($1,000 value for the money it'll save you").
3. Total the stated values and show the price (example: total $4,351 for $599).
4. How you present the stack depends on 1-on-1 or one-to-many selling (covered under bonuses).
[de5a81dc7f90] [a354c944e694]

### 3.4 Create scarcity (Chapter 12)

Pick 1 or more of 3 types: (1) limited supply of seats or slots, in general or per period; (2) limited supply of bonuses; (3) never available again.

**Physical products [text: physical]:**
1. Run limited releases by flavor, color, design or size. Example: "This month, we are releasing 100 boxes of mint chocolate cookie flavored protein bars."
2. Always sell out. "It's better to sell out consistently than over order and fail at creating that scarcity."
3. Repeat over time, "just not too often". Once a month "seems to be the sweet spot for most of the companies that I know who do this with regularity".
4. Tell everyone you sold out. It is social proof, and it raises desire for the next release.
Supporting example: Chanel sends only 1 to 2 of each piece to each store so every item looks like the last one. `[web check: Chanel allocation claim]` [b83b87f23486] [47afc4b21d24] [e456aab8c58a]

**Services [text: service/info]:**
1. Total business cap: "only accepting X clients" at this service level. Build a waitlist. Raise capacity 10% to 20% at times, then cap again. Best for highest tiers. Example: an agency that serves 25 customers total.
2. Growth rate cap: "We only accept 5 new clients per week and we already have the first 3 spots taken."
3. Cohort cap: "We take on 100 clients 4 times a year."
4. For high-ticket workshops and events: have fewer spots than you think you can sell, so people remember you sold out fast. "A compounding strategy."
5. Honest scarcity: define the number you can really take in a period and advertise it ("three-fourths of the way to capacity this week", "81% to capacity").
6. Extreme scarcity: a very small cap on 1-on-1 access (DM, email, phone, voice memo, Zoom), priced very high.
7. "Once you're out, you can never come back": works best with small groups; loses force as groups grow.
Lead magnet example: a free checklist capped at 20 downloads a week, with a waitlist that notifies the next batch. [e456aab8c58a] [b83b87f23486]

### 3.5 Create urgency (Chapter 13)

1. **Cohort-based rolling urgency.** Start clients on a fixed cadence (weekly, every other week, monthly, twice a year). Script: "If you sign up today, I can get you in with our next group that kicks off on Monday." The less often you start, the stronger it is. If someone wants to buy just after a cohort starts: offer speedy personal onboarding as a bonus, or (preferred) sell the wait as an advantage (more prep time, time to talk to staff or family, a longer payment plan). **[text: service/info]**
2. **Rolling seasonal urgency.** Put real dates on landing pages and copy. Rename the same promotion each month: "New Year Promotion ends Jan 30", "Valentines Lovers Promo", "Sexy By Spring Special ends March 31", "Fools in Love April Promo ends April 30". Takes "maybe five minutes of editing". Countdowns must be real or you lose credibility. Source calls this the number 1 strategy for local businesses. (The book's example "Ends Feb 30" is a typo in the source.)
3. **Pricing or bonus-based urgency.** Make the promotion, discount or bonus the thing that expires, not the service. Example script: "I'm not sure how long we will be running it as we change them every 4 weeks or so." Swap in a price promotion, discount, free install, free onboarding or an extra workshop ("valued at $1,000"). Before a real price rise, tell the pipeline "The price is going up! So get in now!" Never raise prices without telling people.
4. **Exploding opportunity.** For arbitrage-type offers that decay with time (buying on eBay to sell on Amazon, a new ad platform before competitors arrive, exploding job offers). Emphasize the decay.

### 3.6 Present bonuses (Chapter 14)

**1-on-1 selling [text: service/info]:**
1. Ask for the sale first, before any bonus.
2. If yes, reveal the bonuses after they sign ("wow", reinforces the decision).
3. If no, add a bonus that matches their stated obstacle, then ask again: "Fair enough?"
4. Repeat bonus by bonus. "People have a hard time rejecting reciprocity."
Group selling is "beyond the scope of this book".

**Bonus checklist (11 rules):**
1. Always offer bonuses (use the stack from Step 5b).
2. Give each a special name with a benefit in the title.
3. Tell them: (a) how it relates to their issue, (b) what it is, (c) how you discovered or created it, (d) how it makes their life faster, easier or less effortful.
4. Give proof: a stat, a past client, or personal experience.
5. Paint a vivid picture of life after using it.
6. Always put a price tag on it and justify it.
7. Tools and checklists beat more training (less time and effort, so more value).
8. Each bonus answers 1 specific reason they think they will fail, and proves that belief wrong.
9. A bonus can be the next thing they will need. "Solve their next problem before they even encounter it."
10. Total bonus value should exceed the core offer's value.
11. Add scarcity or urgency to the bonuses themselves. Scarcity version 1: bonuses "never for sale or available anywhere else". Scarcity version 2: "I have 3 tickets left to my $5,000 virtual event". Urgency: "If you buy today, I will add in XYZ bonus that normally costs $1,000."
[7d21455c5598] [39853dac5920] [cadfc89e4c1d]

**Other people's products as bonuses:**
1. Find adjacent, non-competing businesses that solve the next need.
2. Ask for free products or services for your customers in exchange for exposure.
3. Negotiate a group discount and a commission to you.
4. Optionally help partners build their own Grand Slam Offer so each bonus is more valuable.
[99765539fc85]

**Build a bonus vault:**
1. Create checklists, tools, swipe files, scripts and templates once.
2. Record every workshop, webinar, event and interview.
3. Keep them ready to "sprinkle into an offer to get the deal closed".
Good fits: info products (high perceived value, low cost, "zero operational effort besides giving an additional login"), tickets to virtual events, a month of VIP service (which doubles as an upsell). What should be a bonus rather than core: items with "wow factor" that could get lost in the core, especially short, high-value items (checklists, infographics, a product launch map). ### 3.7 Build a guarantee (Chapter 15)

1. Identify the client's biggest fears: "What do they not want to have happen if they pay you? What are they most afraid of?" Include time, emotion and outside costs.
2. Reverse the fear into a promise using the form: "If you do not get X result in Y time period, we will Z." Without the "or what" it "sounds weak and diluted". Bad: "We will get you 20 clients guaranteed." Better: "You will get 20 clients in your first 30 days, or we give you your money back + your advertising dollars spent with us."
3. Choose a type: unconditional, conditional, anti-guarantee, implied (section 4.1).
4. If fulfillment cost is high, prefer conditional or anti, because you would eat both the refund and the fulfillment cost.
5. Tie conditions to the actions that make customers succeed.
6. Consider stacking (section 4.1).
7. Name it (section 4.1, naming guarantees).
8. "Always hit your guarantee hard, even if you don't have one. Say it boldly and give the reason why."
9. Do the refund math (section 4) before deciding. "Don't be emotional, just do the math."
Source's starting advice: begin with service guarantees or performance partnerships (which make sales final, so no refund fear), then keep them or move to less restrictive guarantees for more volume. [eaf3e7847805] [0a29207c3138] [da1973ba6fc3] [264e18c4c7be] [4d72bb91baae]

### 3.8 Name the offer with M-A-G-I-C (Chapter 16)

1. **M, Magnet ("reason why"):** why you are making this offer, or why they should respond. "Think like a fraternity party planner"; the reason can be anything you believe. Examples: Free, 88% off, Giveaway, Spring, Summer, Back To School, Grand Opening, New Management, New Building, Anniversary, Halloween, New Year.
2. **A, Avatar:** who it is for (and not for). As specific as possible, "but no more". Locally, go hyper-local: "Not Baltimore but Towson, MD." Examples: Bee Cave Dentists, Rolling Hills Moms, Salon Owners, Brooklyn Busy Executives.
3. **G, Goal:** the dream outcome, as specific and tangible as possible. Examples: Pain Free, Celebrity Smile, Little Black Dress, Double Your Profit, First Client, 7 Figure.
4. **I, Interval:** the time to results ("4 Hour", "21 Day", "6 Week", "3 Month"). Compliance: "most platforms will not approve" a quantifiable claim (income, weight loss) with a duration because it implies a guarantee. Use duration freely where it is not a claim: "Make Your First Sale in 10 Days" rather than "$10,000 in 10 days". `[verify UI]` `[web check: current Meta and Google ad policy on outcome-plus-timeframe claims]`
5. **C, Container:** a word that says "bundle or system": Challenge, Blueprint, Bootcamp, Intensive, Masterclass, Program, Detox, Accelerator, Fast Track, Sprint, System, Reset, Solution, and more.
6. Use 3 to 5 components, in any order. "The shorter and punchier the better." Rhyme ("Six-Pack Fast Track") and alliteration ("Debt Detox") are optional; do not force them.
7. Name every sub-item and bonus in the stack with the same formula.
8. Write several names. Run the best 2 to 3 in ads. Note the winner, make it the control, test new names against it. "I honestly have no idea why some names win and others do not."
Marketing theory behind the letters: Attention (Magnet), Discrimination (Avatar), Purpose (Goal), Timeline (Interval), Method (Container). [f2d527d24cc0] [966feb3ae826] [a354c944e694]

### 3.9 Refresh a fatigued offer (Chapter 16)

Change things in this order. Go lower only after the earlier items stop working:
1. Change the creative (images and video in ads).
2. Change the body copy.
3. Change the headline, the "wrapper" (examples: "Free 6 Week Lean Challenge" to "Free 6 Week Tone Challenge"; "Holiday Hangover" to "New Year New You").
4. Change the duration of the offer (six weeks to 28 days or eight weeks).
5. Change the enhancer (the free or discount component).
6. Change the monetization structure, the series of offers and the price points (Volume II/III).
"The lower on the list you go, the more operationally heavy it is." "Once you've monetized an offer, rarely should you change it." Rotate among proven offers only if it does not create operational drag. Do not call an offer fatigued because the audience saw it once; "most people don't even notice an offer on the first mention". (The book numbers this list 1 to 8 with the 2 wrapper examples counted as items 4 and 5; the 6 steps above are the actual changes.) [b261bc411259] [1874fa3ba153]

**Stacking guarantees.** Examples: an unconditional 30-day no-questions guarantee plus a conditional triple-your-money-back 90-day guarantee; or 2 sequential conditional guarantees ("$10,000 by 60 days, $30,000 by 90 days as long as you do thing 1, 2, and 3"). **Naming guarantees.** Bad: "30 Day Money Back Satisfaction Guarantee". Good: "In 30 days, if you wouldn't jump into shark infested waters to get our product back, we will return every dollar you paid." "Great" (the source's word): the "Club a Baby Seal Guarantee". The card says keep memorability but stay on brand and clear; the source's shock wording is style, not required. [1890fdd95d42]

**Wording model (Fladlien, quoted in full in the book).** Frames the unconditional guarantee as a way to "make a fully informed decision" from "the inside", like looking inside a house before buying; "whether it's 29 min or 29 days from now ... if you ain't happy, I ain't happy"; refund by emailing support; backed by a stated support response average of 61 minutes, 24/7. ## 5. Diagnostics and checks

1. **Is the offer a commodity?**
   How: read comparisons in sales calls, reviews, comments and support. Bad: buyers say "these are the same, I'll buy the cheaper one", or price objections dominate. Good: buyers compare you with doing nothing. Next: rebuild with the 5-step process and the enhancers; do not cut price. [61f1c72af108] [78a797a2bcce] `[needs brand data: review and support text, competitor price map]`
2. **Is the market the problem?**
   How: rate market, offer, persuasion (great/normal/bad) in order. Check the 4 indicators. Bad: market shrinking (the newspaper case, -25% a year). Next: if the market is bad, no offer fixes it; if normal, fix the offer before the copy. Do not blame the market after 1 failed offer. [6fdb3e4dcd6e] [3e5ddfe3f852] [4efb9577e16a]
3. **Value Equation audit.**
   How: answer the 4 questions for your offer (what will I get, how will I know, how long, what is expected of me). Score each driver 0 or 1 for a rough comparison against rival vehicles. Bad: you only raised the claim (top side). Next: cut perceived time and effort; add proof and a guarantee for likelihood; make improvements visible. [0e0a8b7a8e72] [b314811091f7] [d3c0e5ca9001]
4. **Price-to-value gap.**
   How: compare what buyers pay with what they get. Bad: priced by averaging competitors and going slightly below. Next: add value first, then raise price. [d1d1f8ddde9a] `[needs brand data: price, COGS, fulfillment cost, conversion before and after]`
5. **Does the offer solve every perceived problem?**
   How: list objections heard in sales; check each has a named solution. Bad: 1 unsolved "one thing" keeps losing sales (the eating-out case). Next: make a reusable asset for it. [cd4c2dd6a329] `[needs brand data: objection log, support tickets, return reasons]`
6. **Is each stack item worth its cost?**
   How: run the trim test (cost to you vs the 4 drivers). Bad: high cost/low value items remain. Next: cut them; replace 1-on-1 items with one-to-many where value holds. [4febc6c2f078]
7. **Is the guarantee worth it?**
   How: compare net sales (sales minus refunds) before and after, as in the 95 vs 117 example. Bad: extra refunds fully offset extra sales, or refunds come from a weak product or sales team. Next: tie conditions to success actions, or switch to a conditional, service or anti guarantee. [264e18c4c7be] [4d72bb91baae] `[needs brand data: refund rate, fulfillment cost per order, conversion by guarantee version]`
8. **Is scarcity honest?**
   How: check the stated cap equals real capacity or inventory. Bad: fake countdowns, limits quietly reset. Next: use "honest scarcity" (your real capacity) and always sell out what you cap. [e456aab8c58a] [b83b87f23486]
9. **Is the offer fatigued, or just unseen?**
   How: check reach and frequency, not only time running. Bad: changing the whole offer after the audience saw it once. Next: follow the refresh order (creative, copy, wrapper, duration, enhancer, monetization). [b261bc411259] `[needs brand data: reach, frequency, CPM, CTR and CVR trend by creative]`
10. **Offer readiness before scaling traffic** (summary of the book's 11-point recap): not a commodity; normal or growing market with a niche; premium price; value drivers worked; 5-step build done; stack profitable; scarcity; urgency; bonuses; guarantee; name. [65ce1e16b1ce]
11. **Offer congruence** (from the extractor header and a card, not from Hormozi): ad, page, checkout and email state the same offer, and no expired promotion is visible. [511c3a0c4cf9]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Pricing by copying the market average and going slightly below. [d1d1f8ddde9a]
2. Cutting price to win customers when you lack a 1/10th cost advantage. [d1d1f8ddde9a]
3. Staying in a shrinking market; or the opposite, niche-hopping after 1 failed offer. [3e5ddfe3f852] [4efb9577e16a]
4. Selling to people who cannot pay (the unemployed resume example) or cannot be targeted (ads to nursing students for rich doctors). [f1a3973e8921]
5. Making bigger claims (top of the equation) instead of cutting time and effort. [0e0a8b7a8e72]
6. Improving the product without making the improvement perceived. [d3c0e5ca9001]
7. Leaving 1 perceived problem unsolved; insisting customers do it your way. [cd4c2dd6a329]
8. Keeping high cost/low value items in the stack. [4febc6c2f078]
9. Satisfying all demand at once, which kills pent-up demand ("pull the trigger too early"). [47afc4b21d24]
10. Over-ordering limited releases so they do not sell out. **[text: physical]** [47afc4b21d24]
11. Fake countdowns: "you'll lose credibility and just look like every other wannabe marketer". [4fc1b785cdaa]
12. Fearing lost sales from deadlines; the source says those buyers "were never going to buy". [9264ceff1dcd]
13. Raising prices without telling the pipeline first. [676632fcde7f]
14. Discounting the core offer to close. [39853dac5920]
15. A guarantee with no "or what". A generic "30 Day Money Back Satisfaction Guarantee" name. [eaf3e7847805] [1890fdd95d42]
16. Using a guarantee to cover a poor product or sales team. [eaf3e7847805]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Where the text addresses physical products, and where it does not**
- Physical products named in the text: the niche list ("physical products, software, brick and mortar stores" use the offer model); lotion gross profit; beauty vehicles (makeup, creams, serums, supplements, shapewear); golf club status copy; supplements versus gyms; pre-made Instacart carts; the "Physical Products" scarcity subsection (limited flavors, colors, designs, sizes; 100 boxes of protein bars; always sell out; monthly cadence; announce sellouts; Chanel; a musician's 100 limited hoodies); infomercial knives; Prestige Labs supplement partner discount; retail sales in the gym survey; jewelry, watches and auction items at the fundraiser; toilet paper and masks.
- Not addressed for physical products: the Value Equation examples, the 5-step build and delivery-vehicle grid, pricing cases, service scarcity caps, all 4 urgency methods, bonus presentation (1-on-1 selling), all guarantee types and examples, naming examples. The anti-guarantee lists "consumable" items and "a line of code to improve your checkout experience on a website" but gives no physical-goods example. Returns logistics, COGS, shipping, inventory risk and consumer return law are not discussed anywhere.
- Every card rewrites these into ecommerce steps (kits, PDPs, return windows, contribution margin, legal review). Treat those as card adaptations to test, not as Hormozi's advice.

**Evidence quality**
- The disclaimer says the numbers are "theoretical" and "illustrative". Most cases are the author's own, self-reported, with no controls. The client survey is voluntary and self-reported (158 gyms).
- Arithmetic errors in the text: the agency case (2.5 x 2.5 x 4 = 25, not 22.4; $112,000 / $10,000 = 11.2x); local reach (200,000 people at $20 per 1,000 = $4,000, not $10,000); wage guarantee (1,960 hours is 49 weeks, not a full year at 40 hours); "Feb 30". Use the calculated values if you cite these.
- Uncited studies: wine taste test, London Underground map, "backed by science" early wins, the market sizes (Grandview, IHRSA are named but not referenced). `[web check]` each before reuse.
- Second-hand claims: Fladlien's 2x to 4x guarantee lift and the $25,000 store buyback.

**Conflicts inside the book**
- "Never discount the main offer" versus discounts used as the "Magnet" in names ("88% off", "$2,000-Off Celebrity Smile") and as pricing urgency ("the discount you came in for"). The book defers "how to monetize free and discounted offers" to Volume III. Reading: discounts appear as time-limited promotional wrappers in lead generation, not as a concession to close.
- "Charge as much as humanly possible" versus "never charge more than your product is worth".
- Anti-guarantee: the text says "strong reason why is needed here. Just make one up that sounds compelling." This conflicts with the repo rule "no invented claims" and with the card [9413eb3d3ddf], which allows only truthful reasons. Do not follow the "make one up" line.
- "Always sell out" and "keep supply below demand" assume a limited-release model. The book itself notes this assumes a business not seeking mass-market penetration.

**Conflicts with common ecommerce advice** (flagged, not resolved)
- Common ecommerce practice runs sitewide % discounts (seasonal sales, first-order codes). The book says do not discount the core offer; add bonuses instead.
- Common ecommerce practice avoids stockouts on core SKUs. The book says under-supply and always sell out, but only for limited releases.
- Common ecommerce practice matches competitor prices on marketplaces and shopping ads. The book says being second cheapest has "no strategic benefit" and most businesses should raise prices.
- Common ecommerce practice offers free, easy returns as standard. The book pushes creative, named guarantees and allows "all sales final" with a reason.
- Scarcity and urgency tactics face consumer-protection rules on fake countdowns and false low-stock claims in many markets. `[web check: FTC, UK CMA/DMCC, EU UCPD rules on urgency, scarcity and reference prices]`
- Stated bonus "values" and total stack values may count as reference pricing claims. `[web check: reference price rules per market]`

**What is missing**
- No method to measure the 4 market indicators or the 4 value drivers.
- No test design, sample sizes or significance for any claim.
- No margin math for bundles beyond "cost to you" and the refund example. The contribution-margin gate for discounts, bundles, bonuses and guarantees is a card adaptation, not in the text [c8b36a7d028e]. The extractor note says offer advice without margin math should be skipped; this book's margin math is limited to gross profit, LTGP and the refund example.
- Group selling and presenting stacks one-to-many are out of scope ("beyond the scope of this book").
- Money models (upsells, downsells, payment terms, free and discount offers) are deferred to later volumes.
- Anchoring appears only as "we anchor the price we tell them to the core offer" and a passing mention that bonuses can "price anchor and product anchor (little known)". Tiered decoy layouts are a card adaptation, not in the text [e94ecf5a6435].

**Brand data needed before an agent applies this**
`[needs brand data: gross margin and contribution per order]` `[needs brand data: lifetime gross profit by cohort]` `[needs brand data: refund and return rate and reasons]` `[needs brand data: real inventory and production limits for any scarcity claim]` `[needs brand data: objection list from reviews, support, chat]` `[needs brand data: competitor price map]` `[needs brand data: sales by hour in past launches]` `[needs brand data: ad reach and frequency before calling fatigue]`
