## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator run a subscription program for a consumable brand (supplements, protein, coffee, skincare, toothpaste) across 4 stages: acquisition and offer design, onboarding, retention (portal, skip and delay, subscriber campaigns, sale periods), and cancel flow, win-back and failed-payment recovery. Its core claim is that growth is capped by who you acquire and on what offer, so offer testing and persona testing come before retention tinkering. It is strong on offer mechanics and cancel-flow logic. It is thin on test design, sample sizes and measured results.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 Acquisition and offer strategy

1. **Treat the subscription ceiling as an acquisition problem first.** Holman: "it doesn't matter how good you get at retaining somebody if you don't have somebody to retain." Every program hits a cap where new subscribers per month equal subscribers lost to churn. Growth then needs better acquisition, lower churn, or both. Condition: a resource-allocation heuristic for subscription and replenishment brands. Holman calls it deliberate "rage bait" and does not say retention work has no value. Not shown for one-time-purchase models. Evidence: illustrative math (section 4) and client names (Instant Hydration, Marsmen scaling "super fast" with a focus on offer testing; Grüns understanding LTV to CAC and payback). [a84f21107a98] [a79ffdb8c9a0]

2. **Finding the right customer fixes churn.** Most brands "worry too much about churn and not worrying about finding the right customer". With the right hook and the right offer, the buyer feels the value, so you do not have to talk them from month 1 into month 2. Onboarding and messaging then make it work. Condition: opinion, no test data given. [a84f21107a98] [7ff6a97aeea6]

3. **Offer testing covers both the offer and the persona.** Asked whether offer testing includes messaging and persona testing, Holman says "It's both." The same product serves different jobs, and each job has different conversion, upsell take and LTV. You can only learn which use case has the best LTV by testing at the offer. Example: Grüns reportedly runs 20, 30 or 50 funnels at once with different angles and hooks per product. Condition: consumables with several distinct reasons to buy. [c03757c10672] [9f5f2138c2b5]

4. **Hook the buyer's goal or identity, not the product category.** Protein powder: bone health and perimenopause (Holman's wife), muscle building (Holman), meal replacement, competition prep ("add 30 pounds of muscle"). Turmeric: longevity, reducing swelling. Sunscreen, toothpaste and other consumables have the same split. Write a separate hook and offer angle per job and run them as separate funnels so results stay clean. [9f5f2138c2b5] [c03757c10672]

5. **Never settle on 1 offer.** "As you grow you never find just one ... you use multiples." Examples he gives: a brand that is not a discount brand except twice a year; a gift-with-purchase path, with remarketing to people who declined it to come back and pick the subscription. A weaker front-end converter (for example gift with purchase) may have stronger LTV, so judge offers on cohort value, not front-end conversion alone. [7ff6a97aeea6]

6. **Build the offer-testing habit early.** "Even if you're a million a year brand you should be doing some of that now", on a funnel or on the PDP, plus CRO testing. [7ff6a97aeea6]

7. **Holman dislikes upfront subscription discounts.** "I kind of hate discounting upfront on subscriptions. I would like to see a world where there's so much value that the subscription costs more." He calls this a tangent and does not give a method for it. Condition: his opinion; it conflicts with standard subscribe-and-save practice (section 9). [2555be53857a]

8. **A flat subscribe-and-save discount on 1 SKU sells only "cheaper".** If the only offer is a discount for subscribing, all you communicate is that it is cheaper, and "you start to get people that just want things because they're cheaper". A low-ticket flat discount is also weak: on a $20 product, 10% is $2 and 20% is $4, "how motivating is that". [defb3074e4c1] [75c76ec85c29]

9. **Use buy more, save more (bulk tiers) as the first mechanic to test.** A bigger upfront quantity adds value, and it "dissuades somebody who's just cheap because they're not going to buy two or three bags at once. They probably can't afford to do that." It also uses anchoring: a buyer who does not want 3 bags may find 2 "just right" (Goldilocks). Condition: Holman calls it "a great place to start", especially for new single-SKU brands. Card caveat: assumes shelf-stable product; check inventory and fulfilment. [defb3074e4c1] [75c76ec85c29]

10. **Stack value that costs you less than the discount it replaces.** On a $100 product at 70% margin you "could give away" $70, but deeper discounts bring discount hunters. 10% off $100 is $10; 10% off $50 is $5. If you have more than 1 SKU, you likely have a product with a landed cost under $5 or $10, so giving that product is cheaper than a deeper discount and attracts "buyers who want value for their dollar, not necessarily just discounts". Condition: needs more than 1 SKU and real landed-cost data. [a4608f159609] [defb3074e4c1] [needs brand data: landed cost per SKU, gross margin per SKU]

11. **Next mechanics after bulk tiers, in the order he lists them.** (a) Add or bundle other products. (b) Free gift for subscribing, for example a 15-day electrolyte sample kit with a protein subscription. (c) Bundle builders. (d) With a wider catalog, frequently-bought-together bundles. (e) Advanced: tiered "gamified" unlocks, where Elevate (UK collagen) gives 8 or 9 items across 1, 2, 3 and 4 month supplies, "the higher up you get, the more stuff you unlock"; and visible gift roadmaps (Everyday Dose; Holman first said Magic Mind, then corrected himself). [defb3074e4c1] [a4608f159609] [d4bfd76ee183]

12. **Take rate is a trade between conversion rate and LTV, set by contribution margin.** Large brands test many discount depths to find "a maximum amount of CVR lift ... with the right amount of LTV long tail". Pushing take rate to 80 to 90% usually raises CAC, because people who do not want to subscribe get pushed. Holman says many brands at $5M to $20M do not have a strong grip on LTV and contribution margin, and you need contribution margin per order to pick the lever. [9099002370eb] [73cdef4101eb] [needs brand data: contribution margin per order, retention curve, LTV by cohort, CAC]

13. **2 valid profit shapes.** Front-loaded: get first-order profit and high first-order AOV (he names "AVI and Javi"; "if I can get like 80% of the LTV that I need on the first order, a lot of brands it makes sense for them. Not everybody can do that"). Back-loaded: take less upfront and earn it downstream. Choose by modelled total profit. The card adds a category match (fast-acting products lean front-loaded, slow-acting lean back-loaded); that mapping is the card's inference, not stated in the transcript. [73cdef4101eb]

14. **The right take-rate setting changes by phase.** While growing, you may make subscription very prominent and make one-time purchase (OTP) hard, "maybe it's just a little hyperlink you click instead of an actual button". Keep pushing CAC up only while strong, well-understood LTV supports it. When CAC becomes untenable at your size, test an OTP option, a gift with purchase, a quiz funnel, or another lever to lower CAC. "You do have to make the choice that works for your business right then", based on overall profitability. [9099002370eb] [73cdef4101eb]

### 2.2 Personas and research

15. **Collect persona data by self-selection and tag it.** Methods named: a pre-purchase popup quiz (Alia), a post-purchase survey (KnoCommerce), surveys inside onboarding emails, cancel-flow answers, founder or director calls and personal emails, and an AI calling tool for opted-in customers (Brian Stark's "Customer Contacts"). Ask 1 simple question such as desired outcome ("losing weight, getting in shape, training for something, doing a Hyrox") or identity. Tag as a profile property and run cohort analysis. Card ba925464ce52 lists KnoCommerce as a pre-purchase quiz; in the transcript it is the post-purchase survey tool. [33ff2c0b8e49] [ba925464ce52] [c03757c10672]

16. **Use the tags in every channel.** Ad team: more creative for the best persona. Email team: Holman's example, if 10% of protein subscribers say they train for a Hyrox, send them Hyrox content and a special offer, including Hyrox events in their area ("that's unlocking LTV"). Onboarding: aim the first 7 to 10 days at the stated goal. [9f5f2138c2b5] [33ff2c0b8e49]

17. **Talk to customers when data is thin.** "When you're small you should be calling as many customers as you can in a given week", from the founder or director level. Automate feedback where possible. "You have to kind of pick a few to start." [33ff2c0b8e49] [7ff6a97aeea6]

### 2.3 Onboarding and product adoption

18. **Onboarding exists to drive product adoption.** "Anything that's not driving product adoption is a problem." Billing reminders and the portal "do not matter if they love the product or they hate the product". The target is the large middle group who neither love nor hate it yet. Holman "cringes" at sales emails in the first couple of days. [df3fcc51281e]

19. **Nothing is obvious to a new customer.** Explain use, timing and the reason it works, even for creatine ("even if I don't work out today, do I have to take it?" is the host's example). Content types: why your version is more effective, when to take it, the science, how other customers use it; for skincare, where to keep it, how to use it, how to fit it in a routine. [df3fcc51281e]

20. **Use every first-order touchpoint.** Package inserts for first orders, especially for tricky products, devices, or rituals; the host notes packages have "100% open rate". Order and shipping confirmations are high-open slots that should also teach use ("criminally underrepresented"). [df3fcc51281e]

21. **Cross-sell as samples in the next box, not as onboarding sales emails.** Holman: "I don't see a lot of success with cross sales within onboarding"; the host adds they risk churn and brand affinity. Instead put cross-sell samples into the next order, where they double as a free gift, plus an insert. Holman allows that a pairing cross-sell ("pair this with another supplement") can support adoption. [a4608f159609] [df3fcc51281e]

22. **Send other flavors or variants with the first order.** A creatine gummy brand shipped 3 or 4 other flavors with Holman's watermelon order, "because they know that if I don't like the taste of watermelon, I'm never coming back". Holman: "anybody who's willing to switch flavors ... is a higher LTV customer." Low cost, and it is a surprise. [a4608f159609]

23. **Set the stage for renewal on slow-acting products.** For skincare and supplements without an immediate effect (testosterone, menopause and perimenopause hormones), communicate the timeline so the renewal "feels like the right next step". Share other customers' journeys and signal, "somewhat subtle", that it is "a three-month game to feel the impact". Holman: "If you're scared to message your subscribers I think it's the beginning of the end." [1493dc0bce84]

24. **For fast-acting products, focus on the first 7 to 10 days, then stop bugging.** Protein powder: make sure the first 7 to 10 days go well, then "unless I know that it's like a different type of protein and that you're new to it I don't want to bug you", except goal-based content (Hyrox training tips) if you know the goal. Card 1493dc0bce84 reads this as "wait 7 to 10 days before check-ins"; the transcript says the opposite order (see section 9). [1493dc0bce84]

### 2.4 Gifts

25. **Untargeted post-purchase gifts usually waste margin.** Holman is "a little contrarian": platforms push "gift a second order to drive retention", but in his tests the lift did not pay for the gifts (math in section 4). He admits "we could have spent a lot more time testing it maybe and it would have been better". [d4bfd76ee183]

26. **Gifts work in 2 places only.** (a) In the acquisition layer, as a promise the buyer knows about before buying: it adds value, lowers CAC and raises retention because it is teased (Everyday Dose, Elevate). (b) As a targeted save: a true surprise (samples, extra flavors), or aimed at a cohort, for example a SKU whose buyers are more likely to reach a VIP tier, or subscribers an AI tool can flag as at risk in month 2 ("I'll sign up"). Otherwise "you're losing time and you're losing margin." [d4bfd76ee183]

27. **If you gift, tell people at every step.** PDP at purchase; onboarding ("here's what you're getting over the next few months"); the host's method of a banner on every onboarding email ("free gift in your next order, valued at $X"); the billing reminder framed around the gift. If the gift is never mentioned, people churn anyway and those who stay "were staying anyways", so the gift has "no incremental benefit". [d4bfd76ee183]

### 2.5 Portal, skip, delay and pause

28. **Portal: findability first, sales last.** When someone is in the portal "it's almost too late most of the time". Table stakes: the subscription order is clearly visible, and the login-to-subscription path is easy. "If they were on the fence and they can't find it, they're cancelling." Most portal visits are to change frequency, swap a flavor, skip, or cancel. Holman works with 9-figure brands that do not earn much from portal add-ons; upsells work better via email and interactive email. Do not pick a subscription platform for how its portal looks. "Don't overengineer it. Focus on customer experience and spend tons of time on your cancel flow." [945cce30648f]

29. **Pause is the worst self-serve action.** Holman: "I hate pauses", because the subscriber "sits there forever and nothing ever happens to them"; reactivation from pause is put at "10% ... or something", said as a rough figure during the exchange. A "pause" that auto-resumes is a delay and should be named a delay. [34907451e7ef]

30. **Cap skip and delay; test the cap by product.** The host's rule: no pause, 1 skip is fine, delay at most 2 or 4 weeks (30 days), because heavy skip and delay use makes rebuild forecasting unreliable and "a lot of people who delay, they just cancel for their upcoming order anyways". Holman agrees and would test beyond 30 days by product: a giant bag of protein might justify letting someone skip 2 months. [34907451e7ef] [4f318efde260]

31. **Make frequency changes very easy.** Holman cites an Autoship Cloud study "a few years ago": subscribers who adjusted frequency 3 times had "200% higher LTV" than other subscribers, because they found the right fit. He stayed with Dollar Shave Club about 7 years on a 7-week cadence. Offer cadences like every 3, 5 or 6 weeks instead of 4. [34907451e7ef]

32. **A skip is delayed revenue, not a save.** Skippers may skip again or never return. Holman leans to guaranteed revenue now: push people into the portal to "get it now" with a bonus or better discount. [394bd4ca221e]

33. **Push chronic no-sayers out the door.** "If somebody's not telling you yes very often they're telling you no", so move them to cancel so they enter win-back and re-engagement. Otherwise they sit in limbo: skipping or paused, paying nothing, and possibly getting no marketing if subscribers are opted out of email. The card's "after N consecutive skips" rule is the card's suggestion; the source gives no number. [394bd4ca221e] [4f318efde260]

### 2.6 Subscriber campaigns and sale periods

34. **Segment subscribers as their own cohort when they are a large share of revenue.** Treat them like abandoned cart or browse abandonment: distinct offers and tests. The host adds lower send frequency plus a couple of subscriber-specific sends a month (subscriber newsletter, subscriber drops). Holman thinks most brands should opt new subscribers out of email campaign segments "at least for the first month or two". [525866e33d96]

35. **Every subscriber send needs an explicit intention.** Holman's repeated failure case, seen at $30M to $40M subscription brands: "we don't send billing reminders because it causes cancellations but we opt everybody into campaigns." [525866e33d96]

36. **A sitewide sale must not beat the subscriber deal.** If sitewide is 20% off and subscribers already get 20%, the sale gives them nothing. Around BFCM, big brands plan for cold and lukewarm audiences, then subscribers "ditch their offer to come back to the site and grab the better deal", which "happens all the time". [35bed5b98d94]

37. **Decide first whether you accept subscriber cancel-and-rebuy.** If yes, run the sale as you like (Holman warns contribution margin may suffer). If no, you "can't do 50% sitewide", because subscribers will see it on socials, ads, email and retargeting. [35bed5b98d94]

38. **Build the subscriber offer first, then trickle down.** Subscribers are "generally speaking more valuable". Example structure from Holman: 40% sitewide, but subscribers get 50% off any product they add during the sale, or a prepaid or 3-month bulk commitment earns 2 extra months. Then brief email and ad teams that the public offer should point to what subscribers get ("they could subscribe and still get all that too"). Other subscriber offers named: 30% off for subscribers, a free gift for subscribers who try a new product, 40% off to add an item to the next order. Deliver via email, portal (good for new product drops) and quick-action links or interactive email. [35bed5b98d94]

39. **Layer a subscription offer into every sitewide sale (host's rule).** The host's "biggest pet peeve" is a sitewide sale with no subscription layer. Stack sitewide 20% with subscribe-and-save 20% and lead with "save up to 40%" on the first subscription order, 20% for one-time buyers. To soften the drop after a heavy first-order discount, frame it as "40% off this order and then 20% off lifetime". [35bed5b98d94]

### 2.7 Cancel flow

40. **The cancel page "shouldn't exist".** Most effort belongs earlier so people have no reason to reach it. It is still where you learn why people leave and where last saves happen. [2555be53857a] [945cce30648f]

41. **First fix: a 1-minute founder video splash screen.** Holman: it "universally" improves save rate. Content: empathy and education; you can adjust, delay or change what fits you; "we still love you"; you can come back any time. With no clear founder persona, use a brand voice or spokesperson. Skio pioneered it, Recharge adopted it quickly, Stay's new portal supports it. [2555be53857a] [verify UI: video splash screen support in your subscription platform's cancel flow]

42. **Discounts are the strongest lever inside the cancel flow.** Despite disliking upfront discounts, Holman says "discounts in the cancel flow are the most effective lever you have". Gifting can work but is "not as effective as bigger discounts". Discounts on this order generally beat discounts over time; "you can test both to find out if it's true for your brand". On tight margins: going from a 20% to a 30% save offer costs 10% of the product price, "but otherwise, they're gone". [a006e3aa6f9c]

43. **6 or 7 cancel questions, rotated monthly.** No more; "Other" catches the rest. Each month retire the 1 or 2 least-picked answers into "Other" and add more specific ones. [a006e3aa6f9c]

44. **Cancel reasons are not literal; write copy per real cause.** "Too expensive" means either not enough value seen, or a real budget change. Both can get a discount, but copy differs: value education (his example: many cancel to pick it up at Walgreens, "let me tell you why that's costing you money") versus empathy ("times are hard ... we're willing to give you 40 50% off this next order"). "Too much product" means either never started using it, or wrong schedule; each gets different copy. [a006e3aa6f9c] [76cd3a77073a]

45. **Match the save action to the reason.** Expense: test a discount. Too much product: delay or skip. Holman tested skip on the splash screen: it raised save rate "but does that have the same impact on revenue because it can hurt revenue", so they moved to delay actions. [a006e3aa6f9c]

46. **Segment save offers as you mature.** By month in the subscription, product bought, LTV or basket size, if the platform allows. Flat dollars ("$20 off your order instead of 20%") only work if you can limit them to baskets over about $100. [a006e3aa6f9c] [verify UI: cancel-flow segmentation options in your platform]

47. **Cancel reasons are a feedback loop.** "Not enough value" is an onboarding email and experience problem. "Too much product" or wrong frequency means you are not explaining how easy it is to change quantity and cadence. Fix upstream. [1c5f9fce2bb2]

### 2.8 Win-back and failed payments

48. **No big offer in the cancel confirmation.** "If I have a great offer I should have used it in the portal or in the flow." Use the confirmation to leave amicably and set up a return, with education where relevant (a supplement needs time; "if you notice differences, please come back"). [42ece6bd5274]

49. **Win-back: broad and fast beats split by cancel reason.** Holman used to segment win-back by cancel reason and now pivots away: "if you have good offers, it doesn't really matter why they canceled." Test different win-back offers to everyone "every week or two weeks" and watch click rate and reactivation rate. Bigger groups give more conclusive results than a list split 7 ways. Reason-based segmentation is "not a bad strategy" if you have the bandwidth. [42ece6bd5274]

50. **Win-back offers can be aggressive and change the product relationship.** Examples: 50% off if you come back and buy product A and B together; free product B for 3 months if you subscribe to 3 months of product A. [42ece6bd5274]

51. **Failed payments leak real revenue; do not trust the platform's claim.** "Every subscription app is going to tell you that they're best at it." Holman: at a few million a year in subscription revenue "you're probably losing five to 10% of that revenue" to bad processing and dunning. Look at third-party providers or improve your own recovery emails. [9644aea14d42]

52. **Segment dunning by payment type and be aggressive.** Separate flows for PayPal, Shop Pay and card, each with its own reactivation steps, and card reactivation as a fallback for everyone. Let the platform retry; when it reaches a failure state, start email and SMS. SMS is "an amazing time" here because the order is not processing: "you have nothing to lose". [9644aea14d42]

### 2.9 Diagnosis habit

53. **Understand the lived experience before offer testing.** Holman's single takeaway: if you do not understand what customers experience with the product and platform, fix that first; "if you feel confident in that, then you should be really great at offer testing". [cfdb1e2cd594] [1c5f9fce2bb2]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Find your cap and choose where to focus

1. Pull monthly new subscribers and monthly churn rate. [needs brand data: new subscribers per month, monthly subscriber churn rate, active subscriber count]
2. Compute the cap: new subscribers per month divided by monthly churn rate. Source example: 2,000 a month at 10% churn caps at 20,000. Compare with the current base. [a84f21107a98]
3. Estimate 2 moves on your own numbers: (a) raising a retention step, for example month-3 cohort retention from 60% to 65 or 70%; (b) raising subscriber opt-in rate, for example 40% to 45%. Holman says (b) is less work, shows faster and compounds. The numbers are his illustration. [a79ffdb8c9a0] [a84f21107a98]
4. If near the cap or the 2 moves favour acquisition, put the main testing budget into offers, hooks and personas. Keep retention work running. [a84f21107a98]

### 3.2 Persona discovery and spend shift (host's case method)

1. Add a popup quiz with 1 question on identity or goal (example: "Are you an athlete or a coach?"). Tag answers as a profile property. [verify UI: popup tool and ESP property sync] [ba925464ce52] [33ff2c0b8e49]
2. Add a post-purchase survey to catch buyers who skipped the popup. [33ff2c0b8e49]
3. Run separate hooks or funnels per persona so results stay clean. [9f5f2138c2b5]
4. Wait (the host waited 1 week) and compare conversion by cohort. Then keep tracking AOV, renewal and LTV by tag; front-end lift is not retention lift. [c03757c10672] [needs brand data: conversion and LTV by persona tag]
5. Shift spend toward the better cohort, and align creative, onboarding and email to it. [ba925464ce52]
6. Card adds: re-run the quiz as new personas appear, and wait for enough volume before reallocating on LTV. [ba925464ce52]

### 3.3 Build a buy more, save more ladder (Holman's worked example)

1. Base: 1 bag at $50. Subscribe and save 10%: $45. [defb3074e4c1]
2. 2 bags plus subscription: add another 10%, so $20 off the pair: $80 for 2. [defb3074e4c1]
3. 3 bags: $120. (In his numbers 3 bags cost $40 each, the same per bag as 2; he gives no deeper 3rd tier.) [defb3074e4c1]
4. Test the ladder against the flat discount. Compare AOV, conversion and LTV by offer. [75c76ec85c29]
5. Optional: add a low-landed-cost product or sample kit at higher tiers instead of a deeper discount. [a4608f159609] [needs brand data: landed cost per SKU]

### 3.4 Model take rate and discount depth

1. Get contribution margin per order and the retention or LTV curve. [9099002370eb] [needs brand data: contribution margin per order, cohort retention by month]
2. Model scenarios: heavy first-order discount with small recurring discount (the host cites ARMRA at about 30% off first order and about 5% recurring) versus lighter first-order discount with a longer LTV tail. Compare total profit per cohort. Holman says a spreadsheet works and AI can now build it, but you must supply the inputs. Do the arithmetic in code or a sheet. [9099002370eb] [73cdef4101eb]
3. Copy the method, not the number: Holman guesses ARMRA tested 25, 35, 40, 45 and 50% before settling on 30%. [9099002370eb]
4. Choose OTP prominence for your phase (subscription prominent, OTP as a text link, while LTV supports rising CAC). [9099002370eb]
5. Recheck as you scale. If CAC becomes untenable, test an OTP option, a gift with purchase or a quiz funnel. [9099002370eb] [73cdef4101eb]

### 3.5 Onboarding for adoption

1. List what a new buyer must know: how, when, how long until it works, why yours is better. Run the team whiteboard exercise (3.11) to find gaps. [df3fcc51281e] [1c5f9fce2bb2]
2. Write onboarding emails on adoption only for the first days; no sales emails. [df3fcc51281e]
3. Add use tips to order and shipping confirmations. [verify UI: transactional email editing in your platform] [df3fcc51281e]
4. Add a first-order insert, most of all for tricky products or rituals. [df3fcc51281e]
5. Add other flavors or cross-sell samples to the first or next order. [a4608f159609]
6. Fast-acting product: make the first 7 to 10 days go well, then send only goal-based content. [1493dc0bce84]
7. Slow-acting product: set a multi-month expectation, share customer journeys, and run a before and after photo check-in (Holman: photo at start, another 30 days later; the host: day 14 and day 90, or a daily log sent in after 60 days, with a gift or discount for completing it). Frame the billing reminder as the next step. [1493dc0bce84]
8. If you run a gift roadmap, show it in onboarding. [d4bfd76ee183]

### 3.6 Gift roadmap done Holman's way

1. Decide the gift is an acquisition promise, not a hidden retention trick. [d4bfd76ee183]
2. Show it on the PDP and in ads before purchase. [d4bfd76ee183]

### 3.9 Cancel flow build

1. Add a 1-minute founder or brand-voice video splash screen. Holman's shortcut: take his script template, give it plus your website to an AI tool, rewrite in your voice. Measure save rate before and after. [2555be53857a] [needs brand data: current save rate]
2. On the splash screen, test offering delay (and possibly skip) or proceed. Watch revenue, not only save rate. [a006e3aa6f9c]
3. Write 6 or 7 cancel reasons. Split "too expensive" into value and budget branches and "too much product" into never started and wrong cadence, each with its own copy. [a006e3aa6f9c] [76cd3a77073a]

### 3.10 After cancel: confirmation, win-back, dunning

1. Cancel confirmation: amicable, door open, education. No big offer. [42ece6bd5274]
2. Win-back: rotate a new offer to all cancelled subscribers every 1 to 2 weeks. Track click rate and reactivation rate. Include product-relationship offers (A plus B at 50% off; free B for 3 months with 3 months of A). [42ece6bd5274]
3. Dunning: measure failed-payment revenue lost. Segment recovery by payment type (PayPal, Shop Pay, card), with a card fallback. Start email and SMS when the platform's retry reaches a failure state. Use SMS aggressively. Evaluate third-party dunning tools (ChurnBuster and others named). [9644aea14d42] [verify UI: failure-type segmentation, Skio is named as supporting it] [needs brand data: failed payment rate and recovered revenue]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Working mainly on churn while ignoring who you acquire and on what offer. [a84f21107a98]
2. A single flat subscribe-and-save discount that only communicates "cheaper" and draws discount hunters. [75c76ec85c29]
3. Deeper discounts when a low-cost product or gift would give more value for less. [a4608f159609]
4. Chasing 80 to 90% take rate without knowing contribution margin and LTV; CAC rises. [9099002370eb]
5. Not knowing contribution margin per order, even at $5M to $20M. [73cdef4101eb]
6. Sales and cross-sell emails in the first days of onboarding. [df3fcc51281e]
7. Assuming use is obvious; no timing or how-to instructions. [df3fcc51281e]
8. Being scared to message subscribers ("the beginning of the end"). [1493dc0bce84]
9. Untargeted second-order gifts nobody was told about. [d4bfd76ee183]
10. Choosing a platform for portal looks; turning the portal into a sales channel; over-engineering it. [945cce30648f]
11. Open-ended pauses, and calling a timed delay a "pause". [34907451e7ef]
12. Treating a skip as a save; leaving chronic skippers in limbo with no revenue and no marketing. [394bd4ca221e]
13. No billing reminders "because they cause cancellations" while opting all subscribers into campaigns. [525866e33d96]
14. Sitewide sale that matches or beats the subscriber rate; planning BFCM only for cold audiences. [35bed5b98d94]
15. Sitewide sale with no subscription layer (host). [35bed5b98d94]
16. Taking cancel reasons literally; long cancel surveys. [a006e3aa6f9c]
17. Judging a skip save by save rate alone; it can hurt revenue. [a006e3aa6f9c]
18. Putting the big offer in the cancel confirmation instead of the cancel flow. [42ece6bd5274]
19. Splitting a small win-back list 7 ways and getting nothing conclusive. [42ece6bd5274]
20. Trusting the subscription app's claim to be best at dunning. [9644aea14d42]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Conflicts with common ecommerce advice**
- Holman dislikes upfront subscription discounts and wants subscriptions to cost more through added value. Standard subscribe-and-save advice is a 10 to 20% recurring discount. He gives no method or evidence for a higher-priced subscription. [2555be53857a]
- Many subscription platforms push a gift on order 2 to lift retention. Holman says untargeted gifts rarely pay back. His own tests may have been too short. [d4bfd76ee183]
- Pause is a common save option in portals and cancel flows. Holman and the host want no open-ended pause. [34907451e7ef]
- Win-back split by cancel reason is common advice. Holman now prefers 1 broad offer track tested every 1 to 2 weeks, while saying reason splits are fine with bandwidth. [42ece6bd5274]
- Common advice says to email subscribers normally; Holman says most brands should opt new subscribers out of campaigns for 1 to 2 months, and admits this creates limbo for skippers. [525866e33d96] [394bd4ca221e]
- Holman dislikes upfront discounts yet calls cancel-flow discounts the strongest lever. Both can hold: discount only at the point of loss. [a006e3aa6f9c]

**Inside the source**
- Several numbers are the host's, not Holman's: the athlete and coach case, the ARMRA 30% and 5% description, the 80 to 90% take-rate question, the skip and delay caps, the "delayers cancel anyway" observation, the stacked 40% offer, the email-banner gift method, the company card exercise, and the 60-day log and day 14 and day 90 photo check-ins. The cards often attribute these to "the source".
- The "up to 40%" stack assumes additive discounts. If the platform applies the 2nd 20% to the already reduced price, the first order is 36% off, not 40%. [verify UI: discount stacking rules]
- In the bulk ladder, 3 bags cost the same per bag as 2 ($40). He does not say whether the top tier should get a deeper discount.
- "It jumps 10%" means 10 percentage points (5% to 15%).
- The 200% LTV for frequency adjusters is 1 old study at Autoship Cloud; correlation, not cause (people who adjust are engaged).
- The 5 to 10% dunning leakage is his estimate for brands at a few million a year, not measured.

**Card issues**
- [1493dc0bce84] says wait 7 to 10 days before check-ins. The transcript says make the first 7 to 10 days go well, then stop bugging fast-acting product buyers. Follow the transcript.
- [ba925464ce52] calls KnoCommerce a pre-purchase quiz popup; in the transcript it is a post-purchase survey tool.
- [75c76ec85c29] says flat discount buyers "churn when the discount ends" and to gift a "high-margin secondary SKU". The transcript says discount buyers "just want things because they're cheaper" and to use a product with low landed cost.
- [76cd3a77073a] claims generic cancel flows underperform specific ones and talks of "the mere presence of a film". The transcript gives no head-to-head; the card is largely a synthesis of [a006e3aa6f9c] and [2555be53857a].
- [35bed5b98d94] gives "200 to 300% sales spikes on BFCM"; the spoken line ("200x ... 300x sales, 300% sales") is a garbled hypothetical, not a result.
- [4f318efde260] title reads "Caveats and Delays"; it means skips and delays.
- [73cdef4101eb] maps fast-acting products to front-loaded profit; the transcript does not state this.
- [394bd4ca221e] suggests "after N consecutive skips"; the source gives no N.

**Missing**
- No sample sizes, test durations or significance rules for offer, persona or win-back tests.
- No win-back or dunning cadence (days, number of messages).
- No discount depths for the buy more save more tiers beyond the 1 worked example.
- No method for the "subscription costs more" idea.
- No numbers for how much OTP should be hidden or how CAC moves with take rate.
- No guidance for non-consumable or non-replenishment products.
