## 1. What this source is for

Gives an operator a framework for building a brand and a market, not just running campaigns: how to pick who you're for (the smallest viable market), how to position against a crowded field (the XY grid), how to use status, tension, and symbols to move people, how to price as a story rather than a spreadsheet line, how to earn permission and build a tribe instead of renting attention, and how to tell the difference between brand marketing (unmeasurable, cultural) and direct marketing (measurable, funnel-driven) so each is judged by the right yardstick. It is the single most detailed source in this library for positioning statements, status/affiliation-dominion messaging, semiotics, and pricing-as-signal — the four things the folder's own scope note calls out.

## 2. Principles

**Foundational orientation**

1. **Be market-driven, not marketing-driven.** A marketing-driven organization is "slick, focused on the offer, the surface shine, the ability to squeeze out one more dollar" — a dead end. A market-driven organization listens to, shapes, and invests in the culture of the people it serves. Condition: applies to any product-roadmap, positioning, or merchandising decision. Evidence: Godin's own framing, illustrated by the JCPenney case (see section 7). `[c496d321604d]`
2. **Run the five steps of marketing in order.** (1) Invent a thing worth making with a story worth telling; (2) design/build it so a few people will particularly benefit; (3) tell a story matching the smallest viable market's narrative and dreams; (4) spread the word; (5) show up regularly, consistently, and generously for years to earn permission and enrollment. Condition: sequencing rule for any launch or brand build. Evidence: stated twice in the book (once in ch1, restated in ch2, Godin's own experience at Spinnaker software co. in 1983 as a young brand manager who "wasted all that ad money" on ads that didn't work). Marketers habitually skip to step 4, which Godin calls "exactly backwards." `[1a1c714f09a8]`
3. **Empathy ("sonder") is the operating stance, not a soft add-on.** Assume every customer has an internal life as rich and conflicted as yours — they don't believe what you believe, want what you want, or know what you know, and they never will. Condition: applies before writing any positioning, offer, or message. Evidence: Godin's own concept borrowed from the "sonder" neologism; illustrated by the nonprofit-fundraiser example (a $1M ask feels crazy to the fundraiser but is "a bargain" to a donor who gets $2M of joy/status/satisfaction from giving it). `[409c6a8dbe35]`
4. **Plan for the irrational customer, not the rational one.** Don't assume people are well-informed, independent, long-term choice-makers, or that they're like you. Bet instead that they act on current irrational urges, ignore contrary information, trade long-term for short-term, and are driven by the culture they identify with. Condition: applies to pricing, promotions, bundles, and checkout design. Evidence: Godin's critique of the microeconomic "rational agent" assumption. `[c534c174189581]`
5. **The internet shifted marketing from ads to empathy.** TV and radio were built to hold ads; the internet is "a billion tiny whispers," not a place you can cheaply steal attention in. Condition: directional claim about media, not a ban on paid advertising. Evidence: Godin's framing of the internet as the first mass medium not invented for marketers. `[0793d186e0ce]`

**Smallest viable market and worldview**

6. **Define the smallest viable market by worldview, not demographics.** Choose the minimum number of people you can delight and still make the business worthwhile, then serve them obsessively. Condition: applies to initial target-customer selection for any launch or reposition; Godin explicitly says this precedes and outranks positioning-by-competitor-comparison. Evidence: Union Square Cafe (founder Danny Meyer knew the room could serve only 600 people/day, so he curated which 600); the "1,000 true fans" heuristic (Kevin Kelly, 2008 Wired essay: ~1,000 people who buy everything you release can sustain "a decent living, not a fortune"). `[cdc515a379b0]` `[f52853b6346f]` `[9696a0aeb3b7]` `[573fc71dee16]`
7. **Segment by psychographics (worldview, self-narrative, status driver), not demographics.** Group people by the stories they tell themselves, the way George Lakoff's "worldview" concept describes a shared lens/bias/stereotype set. Condition: for buyer-persona and targeting work. Evidence: Starbucks served a precise set of beliefs about coffee/time/money/community/luxury, deliberately not competing with Dunkin' Donuts for two decades on the same customer. Named illustrative personas: Bargain Bill (shops as a sport), Hurried Henry (wants shortcuts), Careful Karla (risk-averse). Named negative case: JCPenney's 2011 CEO Ron Johnson (ex-Apple) ended discounts/coupons based on his own luxury-retail worldview; sales fell more than 50 percent because he abandoned Penney's true fans — bargain hunters who liked the game of coupons and urgency. `[3a3aa51fd789]`
8. **"Find the lock before you fashion the key."** Build products for a group you've already identified, rather than inventing a product and hunting for customers afterward. Condition: applies to product selection and range planning. Evidence: Godin's own framing; explicit warning against reverse-engineering a "change" story to justify whatever you already sell (named mocking example: "Activation and engagement for TNT's new thriller that makes a meta-statement about viewer identity"). Contrast case done right: By the Way Bakery (Godin's wife's company, "the largest gluten-free bakery of its kind in the world"), whose stated change is "we want to make sure no one is left out... we change hosts from exclusive to inclusive, and guests from outsiders to insiders." `[ae66960f68e1]` `[8ed5982aa5df]`
9. **Chase depth in a micro-tribe over reach in a mass market.** A small, devoted, connected audience that supports you a lot beats a large, indifferent one that supports you a little. Condition: for niche brands, subscriptions, and community-driven stores, not for businesses genuinely positioned to be a "hit machine" (see principle 10). Evidence: Grateful Dead case (see section 7); a Roland Imhoff (Johannes Gutenberg University, Mainz) study found conspiracy believers were more enthusiastic when told only 19% of Germans shared a belief than when told 81% did — used as evidence that uniqueness-seeking, not consensus-seeking, drives belonging in small groups. `[b5df5fe9a17d]` `[810317bf6dda]`
10. **Some markets genuinely need a "hit machine" — most don't, and that's fine.** Someone will always be the hit-maker in a category (named example: Scott Borchetta/Big Machine Records and Taylor Swift, 200+ number-one singles, 30M+ records sold, tour revenue roughly matching the Grateful Dead's). Godin: "if you can find a playbook... to become a hit machine... go for it." For everyone else, the path is connection and smallest-viable-market depth. Even huge modern "hits" reach a small fraction of the population: Hamilton was seen by under 1% of the U.S. population even at full Broadway capacity; Mad Men, heavily covered by the New York Times, was regularly watched by only about 1% of Americans. Condition: strategic choice a brand must make deliberately, not a universal law that small always beats big. `[b3bfbcca02fe]`
11. **Prove traction in the small before spending to scale.** Ask: how many people outside the company use it daily; how often do they suggest improvements; are they insisting friends/colleagues use it right now; would they miss it if it were gone. "If you can't succeed in the small, why do you believe you will succeed in the large?" Condition: gate before increasing acquisition spend. Evidence: warning against "and then a miracle happens" thinking — the fantasy that PR/hype/ad spend turns an average product into a hit because "it's popular." `[1652f9bf8c62]`

**The promise and positioning (XY grid)**

12. **Write the three-sentence marketing promise.** Template (verbatim): "My product is for people who believe ___. I will focus on people who want ___. I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get ___." A promise is not a guarantee — "more like, 'if this works for you, you're going to discover...'" Condition: for any positioning statement, homepage hero, or PDP headline. Evidence: named promise-type examples from advertising history: status ("They laughed when I sat down at the piano..."), dominance ("Roll Tide!"), status+respect ("Choosy mothers choose Jif"), belonging ("I pledge allegiance..."), affiliation/justice ("The Earth needs a good lawyer"). `[25d09eedd419]` `[e5b4497dc979]`
13. **Position on an XY grid of customer-relevant extremes, not on competitors' feature claims.** Pick two axes people actually care about (not necessarily price/features) — Godin's own axis menu: Speed, Price, Performance, Ingredients, Purity, Sustainability, Obviousness, Maintenance costs, Safety, Edginess, Distribution, Network effect, Imminence, Visibility, Trendiness, Privacy, Professionalism, Difficulty, Elitism, Danger, Experimental, Limited, Incomplete. Plot every alternative (not just direct "competitors") on the grid; a crowded middle quadrant needs an advertising budget to win, an overlooked corner doesn't. Condition: core positioning exercise, re-run periodically since a category can be re-mapped many ways. Evidence: diamond transport (speed vs. security: armored car, postal service, bike messenger, a stamp, each valid for a different customer); a local music teacher choosing "rigor + competitions won" as two axes, creating a teacher worth paying extra for while a different teacher a few blocks away occupies a whole-student, non-competitive quadrant without either one "competing." Historical grounding: David Ogilvy and Rosser Reeves (1950s) invented claims/features to fill a market "hole" (one soap = purity, another = no dry skin, same product); Jack Trout and Al Ries pushed further toward positioning competitors "into a corner." Godin explicitly says this old approach "doesn't hold up... in a hyper-competitive world"; claims must instead be true and generous, doubled down on in every action. `[cdddb1268ba4]` `[93e68a951096]`
14. **Build a generous beacon/signal flare, not a selfish USP.** "Better" isn't transitive for subjective attributes (an Hermès bag isn't "better" than Louis Vuitton just because it's pricier — that's a taste and status claim, not an absolute one). Frame the position as "we're this, not that" as a service to the right customer, not a boast that maximizes market share. Condition: for homepage/About-page core claims. Evidence: Beer Advocate case — 250 beers each have 3,400+ ratings, each someone's favorite; "taste matters, everyone else is wrong." `[bc1ce3b6482]` `[de650f79961f]` `[ee75e1138f82]`
15. **Position one step ahead of today, not ten.** Meaningful positioning sits just beyond current culture; leap too far and the tribe won't follow. Condition: for creative/aesthetic refreshes and category-defining launches. Evidence: entrepreneur Alex Samuel's observation — JetBlue only had to be hipper than American and Delta; Virgin America, six years later, had to be hipper than JetBlue (the bar keeps rising). `[2f504f814390]`
16. **Grow via "roots and shoots," not an acorn expecting a crowd.** Deep roots in a specific niche's dreams/desires let a brand grow into the dominant "tree" in its neighborhood, crowding out shallow-rooted commodity competitors — "the market likes a winner." Condition: framing for long-term category-dominance planning, not a launch-week tactic. `[dfd28e61b812]`
17. **Use "it's not for you" deliberately.** Respecting a smallest-viable-market choice requires explicitly excluding others. Condition: applies to review handling, ad targeting, and copy tone. Evidence: 12% of ~21,000 Amazon reviews for Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone gave 1-2 stars — bimodal audiences are normal for anything that matters to someone; the "comedian's dilemma" story (a comic bombs, then learns the audience was an Italian tour group who didn't understand English) — the work may have failed, or it may simply have been for the wrong room. `[f97dfa662068]` `[e2ffdd118cf5]` `[9a3e3103d764]`
18. **Avoid the two positioning "stumbles."** Stumble one: picking a grandiose, unresourceable change ("change the face of music education nationally") that becomes a comfortable excuse for being stuck. Stumble two: reverse-engineering a "change" story to defend what you already sell, loaded with meaningless buzzwords. Condition: when drafting a brand mission or campaign brief. `[8ed5982aa5df]`

**Status, affiliation vs. dominion, and tension**

19. **Status has six properties (Godin's list, verbatim structure).** (1) Status is always relative — no absolute ceiling ("6 is bigger than 4 but lower than 11"). (2) Status is in the eye of the beholder — a person can be low-status externally and high-status in their own narrative, both true at once. (3) Only status that's attended to matters in a given interaction. (4) Status has inertia — people work to maintain their current level (high or low) more than to change it. (5) Status is learned, though a new cohort can shift someone's perceived status quickly. (6) Shame is the status killer — it works because it undermines a person's relative-status narrative. Condition: applies to product tiers, imagery, influencer partnerships, loyalty perks. Evidence: framework credited to Keith Johnstone's book Impro (illustrated with the opening scene of The Godfather — Bonasera the undertaker, low status, versus Don Corleone, high status); Frank Sinatra case (per Gay Talese) — externally the definition of high status, internally saw "a low-status skinny kid," illustrating that you cannot know from the outside whether a person believes their own external status. `[963941c44199]`
20. **Segment messaging by affiliation vs. dominion — the customer's worldview decides which, not yours.** Dominion is vertical (winning/losing, power, "who eats first"; self-talk: "this is mine, not yours," "who has more power," "I did this myself"). Affiliation is horizontal (insiders/outsiders, being in sync; self-talk: "who knows you," "who trusts you," "where do you stand within the tribe"). The same person can hold both frames in different contexts (a 12-year-old cares intensely about winning on the field, but also cares a lot about who sits next to him on the bus). Condition: for acquisition creative, influencer briefs, and community mechanics. Evidence: Tom Hanks ("nicest guy in Hollywood," affiliation) vs. Don Corleone (dominion); named dominance-branding case: early Uber's contentious relationships with regulators/competitors/drivers, which let aligned investors/employees/users double down on a winning-and-losing story; affiliation-signal tactics named: trade-show booth traffic, launch-party guest lists, book blurbs, investment-bank "tombstone ads" listing the "right" firms, B2B referrals, a local craftsperson building one-neighborhood reputation. Affiliation tolerates abundance (network effects help everyone); dominion leans on scarcity. Fashion is usually affiliation-driven — the goal is being in sync, not winning. `[bbfb0fef7597]` `[b87302554feb]`
21. **The status quo resists you because of status, not because your idea is wrong.** "If all it took to upend the status quo was the truth, we would have changed a long time ago... the status quo doesn't shift because you're right. It shifts because the culture changes. And the engine of culture is status." Condition: applies when planning competitive entry against a market leader or an entrenched category belief. `[24bbaad92a51]`
22. **Seed a small number of respected insiders to trigger cascading adoption ("the standing ovation").** As few as ~3 respected people standing at TED, or roughly 15 strangers spread through a Broadway theater, can trigger a full-room cascade — but only where trust/recognition among attendees already exists (a jazz club audience at Mezzrow, per the source, essentially never gives a standing ovation because the venue's own norm forbids it). Condition: applies to launch seeding and social-proof design in high-trust communities; the specific headcounts are illustrative of the mechanism, not a formula. `[99309de7a12b]`
23. **Classify every offer as a pattern match or a pattern interrupt, and market accordingly.** A pattern match slots into an existing routine (a new cereal brand added to a family's rotation) — cheap, low-friction. A pattern interrupt requires breaking an established habit (a first-time gardener hire) — most people say no, because patterns represent safety. Best pattern-interrupt targets are people whose life context just broke their existing pattern and has no incumbent to unseat: new dads, engaged couples, people who just moved, or anyone encountering a brand-new platform at launch. Condition: shapes go-to-market sequencing for a new category or a subscription meant to replace an incumbent habit. Evidence: Slack case (see section 7). `[8c7a16df0f13]`
24. **Create tension deliberately; never create fear.** Tension is the pull of "this might work / this might not," or the discomfort of being left behind — necessary for forward motion and present in good education, a jazz concert, or a thrilling movie. Fear paralyzes and is "a dream killer." If a message coerces or scares rather than creating a resolvable pull, Godin says "you're probably doing it wrong." Condition: applies to acquisition and cart-recovery copy, launch scarcity, and community pressure. Evidence: named tension triggers — a going-out-of-business sale's FOMO, a new social app's early-adopter advantage, a home-sale-urgency narrative, Supreme limiting a drop to 250 units, a TV finale airing Sunday. Every launch also breaks something for someone (a second hotel ends the first's "only one" status; a new efficiency extreme displaces the prior extreme-holder) — that displacement is itself the incumbent's source of tension. `[e1cc02e44a65]` `[8d76a2fd3089]`
25. **Change the ritual, not the underlying human need, when a target behavior is culturally load-bearing.** When a behavior serves deep status/community/rite-of-passage needs, replace the specific act while honoring the underlying desire, rather than arguing against the belief head-on. Condition: for culturally or identity-sensitive category or product-behavior shifts. Evidence: Lion Guardians (Dr. Leela Hazzah) case — Maasai adolescent males historically proved bravery by killing a lion solo (lion population fell from an estimated 200,000 a generation ago to ~30,000); Hazzah's team built a new rite of passage around finding/naming/tracking/saving lions instead, preserving the same community-binding, bravery, and status functions. `[f1188b0479f4]`
26. **Peer pressure and "cash for status" can outperform a fundraising or premium-tier tactic on its own.** Condition: for high-status cohort fundraising, premium tiers, or exclusive purchase experiences. Evidence: Robin Hood Foundation's 2015 gala raised $101,000,000 in one night — the single most effective fundraiser of its kind in history at the time. Godin's explicit argument: the mechanism was not the gala format but "the extraordinary peer pressure of people like us do things like this" among Wall Street/hedge-fund donors, cultivated over a generation and normalized as "what we do," not an unintentional side effect of a well-run event. `[8e5f86a776a2]` `[2b7413eac695]`

**Semiotics, symbols, and brand definition**

27. **People scan, not study — design for "what does this remind me of?"** Busy customers don't know your competitive drama or how hard you worked; they read logos, page styling, photography, room setup, and even a phone call's click-and-pause cadence for pattern matches to things they already trust or distrust. Condition: applies to storefront, PDP, packaging, and ad-creative audits. Evidence: a GeoCities-style flashing-GIF site or a robocall's telltale pause reads as a scam and is hard to undo; big companies' logos often look alike on purpose (signaling "solid company"), not from laziness; a summer blockbuster "gives itself away in four frames," instantly distinct from a teen-vlogger video. The symbol lives in the viewer's mind, not the marketer's: a Silicon Valley hoodie signals "too busy to shop" status there, but could read as a threat in a different context in East London. Named deliberate-signal case: Nigerian scam emails are sloppy on purpose — the bad grammar filters IN only the credulous and filters OUT the skeptical, saving the scammer's time. Named vestigial-signal case: driving gloves with a hole in the back (originally so a wristwatch could poke through; the watch is gone, the hole survives as pure symbol), and aftermarket SUV wheel "flares" with no function beyond status flag — overdoing them can lower status, "like plastic surgery." `[b1ae1eab635d]` `[b4d995b1ac08]`
28. **A brand is the customer's shorthand for a promise — not the logo.** If you switched a customer from Verizon to AT&T and they wouldn't notice or care, that "brand" is functionally worthless — a commodity has no brand at all (wheat by the ton, coffee by the pound, bandwidth by the GB). Logos matter less than designers claim and more than committees realize: pick a workable one, don't over-invest, and keep it as long as you keep your name. Condition: for brand-identity and logo decisions. Evidence: named "unmemorable logo, strong brand" examples: Google, Sephora, Costco. Named semiotics-as-bias case: Witchsy.com founders Penelope Gazin and Kate Dwyer invented a fictional male co-founder "Keith" whose emails got faster, more respectful vendor/developer replies than the real founders' own emails (per Fast Company) — evidence that symbols can encode bias against you, not just for you. `[177aa8d4c957]` `[dfa4179985351]`
29. **You cannot build a brand for everyone — be specific, then overdo it everywhere.** "You definitely, certainly, and surely don't have enough time and money to build a brand for everyone... Be specific. And then, with this knowledge, overdo your brand marketing." Every touchpoint should let a customer make a smart guess about all of you. Condition: applies to brand-marketing budget allocation once a specific audience is chosen. Evidence: Apple's 1984 Super Bowl ad (art directed by Lee Clow, referencing Orwell) — only needed the "media-savvy talking heads" and "nerds," roughly a million people out of ~100M viewers, to get the reference and talk about it; took ~30 years for the idea to spread from that million to "everyone." `[dfa4179985351]`
30. **Define "us" explicitly and narrowly in every "people like us" statement.** A vague "us" (e.g., "Americans," "everyone") is useless; the tighter and more connected the group, the more effective the change. The correct order is not "the people who give are people like us" (backwards) but a specific, named identity you invite people into. Condition: for brand manifestos, community language, and internal pitches alike. Evidence: "The Blue Ribbons" case (see section 7). `[f3fcf887a035]` `[1381647866a6]`
31. **Build an exclusive cohort, not just an elite one.** Elite is external respect that outsiders can revoke overnight (Rhodes Scholarship). Exclusive is an internal, controllable us-vs-them identity (Hell's Angels). Harvard Business School and the Navy SEALs are both. Exclusive institutions actually change behavior because belonging is something the group itself controls. Condition: for loyalty tiers, member communities, and gated-access programs. `[0917788c7038]`

**Pricing as a story**

32. **Price is a signal that shapes marketing, and marketing shapes what a price means — treat them as one decision.** Price must align with the XY-positioning extremes claimed elsewhere; the choice between cheapest and priciest wine on a menu says nothing about the wine itself, only about the story the buyer wants to tell. Condition: applies whenever setting or restructuring price points. Evidence: worked baker margin example (numbers below); Porsche Cayenne cited as pure status signal with no proportionate utility. `[070a742b6c8b]` `[19792f53199d]`
33. **Ask the "ten times" question before adding a premium tier.** What would justify charging ten times more? Reject "more of the same" answers (more words, bigger portions, a louder stereo don't multiply willingness to pay) — look instead for a different story or a different kind of scarcity (access, curation, exclusivity, relationship, transformation). Condition: for premium-tier or luxury-bundle pricing. Evidence: Fiona's ice cream shop at the Opinicon resort near Ottawa — a waffle cone under 3 Canadian dollars, deliberately kept cheap as "a symbol, a beacon, a chance to engage" rather than priced at the ~$8 an MBA profit analysis might suggest. `[b48af12fdcd2]`
34. **The hard part of variable/dynamic pricing is the storytelling, not the mechanics.** History: Quakers invented the fixed price tag on moral grounds (equal price for everyone); Macy's and Wanamaker's adopted it for operational efficiency at scale (couldn't train enough staff to haggle). Condition: for discount, member-pricing, and dynamic-pricing decisions. Evidence: Tesla's fixed, no-haggle pricing reassured luxury buyers; Uber's demand-based surge pricing "cost their brand billions in trust" (Godin's claim; `[web check: current status/perception of Uber surge pricing]`). `[32584c80f7c7]` `[ba1499ab1e10]`
35. **"Cheap" is another way to say "scared."** Racing to be the cheapest promises "the same, but cheaper," not change — "the last refuge of a marketer who has run out of generous ideas" (Godin's own words, stated as an opinion with an explicit exception for "an extraordinary new way to deliver your service"). Condition: applies before defaulting to a discount strategy. `[19792f53199d]`
36. **Structure a free-to-paid ladder as "free ideas that spread, expensive souvenirs that are worth paying for," not as one undifferentiated free tier.** Two reasons not to make everything free: (1) a purchase creates scarcity/tension/enrollment that a giveaway forfeits; (2) no cash flow means no ability to reinvest. Free "scales to infinity" precisely because it isn't a small price — a free idea spreads far faster than a priced one. Condition: for content marketing, sampling, and community-to-purchase funnels. Evidence: a chef's free recipes/podcast vs. her $24 plated restaurant dish; a free radio song vs. an $84 concert ticket (figures illustrative, from music/restaurant contexts, not ecommerce). `[2ee5aff46dcf]`
37. **A high, risky price can build trust rather than erode it, via cognitive dissonance.** People sometimes convince themselves a costly commitment must be trustworthy ("I'm smart, so I must trust this") — the same mechanism behind boot-camp bonding and why high-end restaurants can survive bad reviews. Explicit rule: "Lowering your price doesn't make you more trusted. It does the opposite." Condition: for high-ticket items and premium subscriptions facing buyer hesitation; not a license to raise price without supporting quality. `[e8ed1256772e]`
38. **When you change price or policy, name explicitly who it's for and who it's not, and how status shifts.** A change can't be for everyone — say so ("it's not for you, sorry") rather than pretending otherwise. Condition: for pricing, tipping-policy, or loyalty-tier changes affecting existing customers. Evidence: USHG (Union Square Hospitality Group, the company behind Union Square Cafe, which also spun off Shake Shack) eliminated tipping in 2016, raising menu prices ~20% to fund parental leave and fair, professional-style wages. Big-tippers lost a status ritual; fairness/dignity-minded diners gained a story they could tell friends — deepening loyalty precisely because it's worldview-aligned, not a discount play. `[7c300cb947347]`

**Permission and the tribe**

39. **Own a permission asset instead of renting one.** Real permission means: "if you stop showing up, people are concerned and ask where you went" — not a legally-obtained email address or fine-print consent. Using a social platform for reach makes you "a sharecropper": the platform owns the permission, monetizes it by charging you to reach people you thought you already had, and can change the rules anytime. Condition: applies to email/SMS strategy and any reliance on platform-native reach. Evidence: Godin's own company Yoyodyne reportedly had 70%+ open rates and ~33% response rates (his own 1990s figures, `[web check: not a modern benchmark]`); DailyCandy (Dany Levy) — a city-focused email newsletter — sold for "more than a hundred million dollars"; Spotify's RapCaviar playlist (Tuma Basa, hired 2015) grew to 3M+ subscribers within months and 9M within 3 years, described as "the most important asset in the music business... bigger than any radio station." `[b39a993e6adc]` `[7589bd4ba273]` `[565ac57d818d]`
40. **Design tension and relief into every step of the customer's journey, not just the sale.** Condition: for PDP flow, onboarding, and content sequencing. Evidence: Penguin Magic's trick-demonstration videos show the outcome without revealing the method, creating tension that drives purchase (over a billion views, at zero distribution cost); the Open Heart Project's 8-point growth system (below). `[25f6ebc5c073]` `[db8055815e43]`
41. **Reframe "prospects/customers" as "students."** Shift from a compliance-testing power dynamic to one of enrollment, choice, and care. Condition: for onboarding emails, content, and retention touchpoints. `[16af7cf20f56]`
42. **The Open Heart Project's eight-part community-growth system (Susan Piver's online meditation sangha).** (1) Start from empathy for a real need, not "how do I start a business"; (2) focus on the smallest viable market — "how few people could find this indispensable and still make it worth doing"; (3) match the audience's worldview and language; (4) make spreading easy — one new member per existing member compounds; (5) earn and keep attention/trust; (6) offer ways to go deeper — do work FOR members, don't just recruit them; (7) create and relieve tension at each step of the member's journey; (8) show up often, with humility, focused on what's working. Result cited: grew from a small following to 20,000+ members with a single full-time staffer, "not in one fell swoop... in thousands of small swoops." `[85c6164c8044]`
43. **Remarkability is decided by the user, never by the creator.** "If they remark on it, it's remarkable." Build talkability into the product/experience itself with intent; avoid stunts (offensive, juvenile, urgent, selfish attention grabs), which the source explicitly distinguishes from genuine remarkability. People spread word mostly to say something about themselves ("look at my taste"), not to do the marketer a favor. Condition: for packaging, unboxing, and post-purchase moment design. Evidence: Fight Club's "first rule is you don't talk about Fight Club" became an invitation to talk about it once the right worldview-matched readers heard of it; Alcoholics Anonymous, "hardly anonymous" in practice, treats talking about membership as a generous, shame-reducing act baked into its culture. `[5f5e55f7d191]` `[f315eb5389c3]`
44. **Design evangelism into the offer — people share because it serves their own goals, not yours.** Two bridge questions for any sharing mechanic: "What will I tell my friends?" and "Why will I tell them?" — the "why" must be a real benefit to the friend group joining, not a favor to the brand. Condition: for referral, group-buy, and gifting mechanics. `[f315eb5389c3]`
45. **Aim to be "famous to the smallest viable audience," not to maximize follower count.** "Everyone is famous to fifteen hundred people. Some to three thousand." For a consultant, designer, or niche brand, being known to the right 3,000 people is enough. Distinguish Public Relations (deliberately telling your story to the right people) from Publicity (chasing ink "as long as they spell my name right"). Condition: for PR and community-reach strategy. `[b75dfff1cc41]`
46. **Use Marshall Ganz's "Story of Self, Story of Us, Story of Now" structure for founder/community narratives.** Story of Self establishes "I am people like us, I did things like this" (your own transformation, not catastrophizing or fake vulnerability). Story of Us explains why the audience should care and invites empathy ("together, not apart"). Story of Now is the urgent, peer-pressure-backed call to action. Condition: for About pages, launch emails, and founder-led video/content. Evidence: Ganz worked with both Cesar Chavez and Barack Obama; a worked example (a figure-skating weight-loss narrative) demonstrates the exact 3-paragraph structure in the source. `[1d9f9a55d030]`
47. **Reject manipulative organizing tactics even when they "work"; use their generous mirror-image instead.** Saul Alinsky's Rules for Radicals gives 13 zero-sum tactics (e.g., "ridicule is man's most potent weapon," "pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, and polarize it") that Godin explicitly labels tribe-killing when used for manipulation. He offers 13 reversed, generous principles instead (e.g., "treat others the way you'd want to be treated," "stick with your tactics long after everyone else is bored with them, only stop when they stop working," "disagree with institutions, not with people"). Condition: for community organizing, activist-style marketing, or any campaign built on an us-vs-them frame. `[no direct card — context/discussion in ch20, not summarized as its own card]`

**Direct vs. brand marketing, the funnel, and measurement**

48. **Separate unmeasurable brand marketing from measurable direct marketing — judge each by its own yardstick.** Direct marketing is action-oriented and measurable (a Facebook ad you click- and convert-track); brand marketing is culturally oriented and not measurable (a billboard hoping to be remembered someday). Explicit operating rule: "If you're buying direct marketing ads, measure everything... if you can't measure it, it doesn't count. If you're buying brand marketing ads, be patient, refuse to measure... if you can't afford to be consistent and patient, don't pay for brand marketing ads." Condition: for budget allocation between paid acquisition and brand-building. Evidence: credited to Lester Wunderman ("the father of direct marketing," built American Express and the Columbia Record Club; Godin put him on Yoyodyne's board in 1995). Numeric claim: "$100 of online advertising generates $125 in profit for the advertiser" is why Google/Facebook ad revenue grew so fast (2017 figure: the two platforms took in "more than a hundred billion dollars," about half of global online ad spend). Contrast: legacy brand advertisers (Ford, Absolut Vodka, Palmolive named; also P&G's Tide/Crest on TV) can't simply swap to direct-response tactics without their business model breaking. `[4a787df73c0a]` `[025ead1f1433]`
49. **Work the funnel with six explicit levers.** (1) Attract the right people; (2) align the entry promise with the actual destination; (3) remove decision steps (but not so many that trust collapses); (4) support people along the way (reinforce dreams, address fears); (5) use tension to create forward motion; (6) hand successfully-engaged customers a megaphone to tell others. Condition: diagnostic framework for any leaky landing-page or purchase funnel. `[03bc266e06a4]`
50. **Establish customer lifetime value (LTV) before buying any ads, and don't buy ads you can't trace.** Explicit rules: "If you can't see the funnel, don't buy the ads. If you can measure the funnel and it costs too much for you to afford ads, don't buy the ads. Fix the funnel first." Condition: for setting acquisition budgets and target CAC. Evidence: worked numeric example (see section 4); old-school stamp math example (see section 4); Slack cited as an extreme-LTV case once coworker lock-in and equity value are counted. `[4c18bce6cf22]` `[f10089962425]`
51. **Expect most "magical, self-running" funnels to be fantasy.** LTV rarely exceeds acquisition cost once trust and web clutter are priced in; most brands that matter are "primed by advertising but built by good marketing" — organic evangelism and community, not a perpetual-motion ad machine, which "almost always ends in bitterness" when pushed too hard. Condition: applies whenever evaluating a promised "passive" acquisition system. `[f10089962425]`
52. **Build frequency before declaring a message dead.** People remember what they rehearse, not what they merely saw once; "the familiar is normal, and the normal is trusted." There's a real gap ("the dip") between when the marketer gets bored and when the audience actually absorbs the message. Named quote (Jay Levinson, Godin's own mentor): "Don't change your ads when you're tired of them. Don't change them when your employees are tired of them. Don't even change them when your friends are tired of them. Change them when your accountant is tired of them." Condition: for deciding whether to refresh creative, retire a tagline, or abandon an unproven channel. `[440b65714a23]`
53. **Reject generic-term SEO as a durable strategy; build a name worth searching for instead.** The math can't support every business imagining it will be the first match among "a thousand pages of results" for a generic term. Reframe the goal: get people to search for your name, not a category term (Godin's own example: search "Seth," not "blog"). Condition: for SEO investment decisions on a limited budget. `[e3dbb41cbcdb]`
54. **Trust is earned through action, not statements — track what you did, not what you said.** In a low-trust culture, three paths exist for a marketer: Ignored, Sneaking around, or Trusted — only Trusted compounds (trust → attention → kept promises → more trust → word of mouth). Prompts to test yourself: what did you do on a refund request, a data breach, a plant closure? Condition: for service-recovery policy and crisis response. `[e4fcc6c178a1]`
55. **Expect a "chasm" between early adopters and the mass market, and build a bridge on network effects, not hype.** Geoffrey Moore's point (from the Rogers adoption curve): satisfying early adopters may require annoying the mass market, because the very thing that thrilled adopters (breaking a pattern) is what the mass market resists. The bridge across is network effects — early adopters have a selfish incentive to recruit others only if the product genuinely improves when friends join (a beloved chocolate doesn't improve when others eat it; Snapchat/Instagram/Twitter do). Two bridge questions: "What will I tell my friends?" and "Why will I tell them?" Condition: for category-entry and expansion sequencing. `[894eb8e3217061]` `[bc48055c28ea]`
56. **A local chasm crossing can matter without a global one.** Low entry cost + a quick, visible payoff can flip a whole local group fast (a schoolyard yo-yo fad reaching ~30 kids within a week); durable adoption additionally needs identity/persistence (contrast: yo-yos faded fast; Uggs, black backpacks, and penny skateboards persisted). Condition: for seeding a new product in one community, campus, or region before a broader rollout. `[bc48055c28ea]`
57. **Escape the long-tail trap by dividing one giant market into many small "short heads," not by chasing a mass hit.** Chris Anderson's long-tail shape: hit #1 sells roughly 10x hit #10 and 100x hit #100; half of Amazon's book sales are titles outside the top 5,000 — yet a lone creator selling one obscure item can't live on it ("the internet can live on that... you can't"; "becoming an outlier isn't a strategy, it's a wish"). The escape: create the single definitive contribution for a narrowly-defined niche, and connect that niche's members to each other so they recognize it as their shared "hit"/glue. Condition: for product-selection and assortment-breadth strategy on a large marketplace. `[2b78cfe849b3]`
58. **Prioritize identified superusers over trying to serve everyone equally.** "Everyone is a restaurant critic now" doesn't mean you can treat everyone better — fixed resources mean you can't. Watch behavior, not stated intent, and "adopt back" whoever is already advocating, talking, or trying to lead. Fixed marketing costs spread across the customer base mean a small minority of customers are usually the only profitable ones ("the whales pay for the minnows"). Condition: for reviewer/UGC/ambassador program design and support-tier prioritization. `[b1d6d86a7bc5]` `[a4515f778cd0]`
59. **Separate goal, strategy, and tactics — and don't abandon a working strategy when one tactic underperforms.** Goal = the change sought in the world; strategy = the durable, long-lasting approach; tactics = the many replaceable steps executing it. Test: telling a competitor your tactics costs you (instantly copyable); telling them your strategy usually doesn't matter (they lack the guts/persistence to adopt it). Condition: for annual marketing planning and channel-kill decisions. Evidence: Coca-Cola (goal: more people drinking more Coke; strategy: mass advertising tying Coke to happiness and universality; tactics: the ever-changing ad content) vs. Patagonia (goal: get a small group of outdoor enthusiasts to care about the environment; strategy stable for 30+ years — redefine how that group thinks about environmental impact, give them a label and evangelism tool, create insiders/outsiders; tactics: clothing recycling, brick-building retail stores, materials/selection/price choices). `[8d1d09b4c8e5]`

## 3. Procedures

**A. Write the three-sentence marketing promise** `[25d09eedd419]` `[e5b4497dc979]`
1. Fill in: "My product is for people who believe ___. I will focus on people who want ___. I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get ___."
2. Fill the first blank with the customer's worldview or belief, not a demographic trait.
3. Fill the second blank with the aspiration or desired outcome the customer is already pursuing.
4. Fill the third blank with the concrete emotional transformation your product enables (belonging, status, connection, peace of mind, respect, dominance).
5. Test the finished sentence against your PDP headline, ad hooks, and email onboarding copy — flag any touchpoint with no clear connection to X, Y, or Z.
6. Rewrite any weak touchpoint until it carries the same promise.

**B. Run the XY positioning exercise** `[cdddb1268ba4]` `[93e68a951096]`
1. List every alternative your customer could actually choose — not just direct competitors, but other formats, marketplaces, DIY, or doing nothing.
2. Pick two axes people genuinely care about in this category (draw from Godin's menu in principle 13, or invent your own).
3. Plot every alternative on the grid.
4. Identify the crowded middle quadrant — this is where undifferentiated brands compete on price and need an ad budget to win.
5. Choose a corner that is extreme on at least one axis, confirming you can actually deliver on it operationally (sourcing, service, returns).
6. Rewrite your homepage, PDP, and lead ad around that single claim.
7. Re-run the exercise periodically with different axis pairs; the same category maps differently depending on what you choose.

**C. Diagnose affiliation vs. dominion for a segment** `[bbfb0fef7597]` `[b87302554feb]`
1. Ask affiliation-diagnostic questions of the segment: who do they want to know them, who do they want to trust them, what do they want their circle to think?
2. Ask dominion-diagnostic questions: do they talk about winning, power, "doing it myself," or their side beating another side?
3. Note that the same person can flip frames by context — check the specific purchase moment, not the person's general personality.
4. Write two draft messages, one for each worldview, rather than one averaged, bland message.
5. Route the affiliation draft toward community/belonging cues (who else uses this, shared identity) and the dominion draft toward competitive/status cues (winning, exclusivity, being first).
6. Test against actual customer language in reviews, support tickets, or UGC before committing spend.

**D. Design tension and relief into a customer journey step** `[e1cc02e44a65]` `[25f6ebc5c073]`
1. Identify what becomes less true for the customer if they don't act (what they'd miss, lose status on, or fall behind on).
2. State the tension honestly — check it isn't actually fear (coercion, manipulation, or manufactured scarcity that isn't real).
3. Make the tension resolvable with one simple, immediate action.
4. Deliver the promised relief the moment the action is taken — don't let tension linger unresolved past the point of frustration.
5. Repeat the create-and-relieve cycle at each subsequent step of the journey (Open Heart Project pattern), not just at checkout.

**E. Fix a leaky funnel** `[03bc266e06a4]` `[4c18bce6cf22]`
1. Map every stage from first touch to loyal customer explicitly (ad → click → landing page → cart → purchase → repeat).
2. At each stage, ask: are the right people arriving here? Does the promise that brought them match this stage's actual content?
3. Count the decision steps; remove any that aren't earning their cost in trust or conversion — but stop removing once trust starts to visibly drop (don't strip so many steps that buyers stop trusting the process).
4. At each remaining step, add support: reinforce the dream, answer the specific fear.
5. Add a tension-and-relief moment at the step with the highest drop-off.
6. After a successful purchase, hand the customer an explicit way to tell others (referral link, shareable result, an invite).
7. Compute lifetime value (LTV) using the supermarket-style method: (average order value) x (purchase frequency) x (expected customer lifespan) x (margin %); add a referral-value estimate if repeat customers demonstrably bring others.
8. Compute cost per order from your actual ad/click data (cost ÷ clicks = cost per click; clicks ÷ orders = cost per order).
9. Compare LTV to cost per order. If LTV clearly exceeds cost per order, scale spend. If it doesn't, stop buying ads and fix the funnel (steps 1-6) before spending more. `[needs brand data: current CAC, conversion rate by stage, and LTV by cohort]`

**F. Write a Story of Self / Story of Us / Story of Now narrative** `[1d9f9a55d030]`
1. Story of Self: describe a real transition — who you were, what changed, what you learned — establishing "I am people like us, I did things like this." Avoid catastrophizing or performed vulnerability.
2. Story of Us: explain why that experience is relevant to the specific audience being addressed — what you share, why they should care, invite their empathy.
3. Story of Now: state a concrete, immediate next action, using the group's own peer pressure/momentum as the reason to act together, without delay.
4. Check the three sections appear in that order and each does its distinct job — self establishes standing, us builds the tribe, now creates the tension that moves people.

**G. Write a five-part business plan (or store-launch brief)** `[cc2b6038e9ea]`
1. Truth: describe the market as it is — needs, competitors, technology standards, others' successes and failures. No positions taken, just facts; include data, spreadsheets, and visceral customer stories. Get the team to agree on this before moving on.
2. Assertions: state "we will do X, and then Y will happen" — the specific market served and the expected market response. Accept this section will be partly wrong.
3. Alternatives: state what happens if an assertion misses — how flexible is the product, supply chain, or team? This is what lets you test instead of bet the whole business on one assertion.
4. People: describe attitudes, abilities, and shipping track record (not resumes) — both your team and the customers you serve, including their status beliefs and worldview.
5. Money: capital needed, spend plan, cash flow, P&L, margins, exit — written last, after the story is clear.
6. Replace a vague mission statement with the specific answer to: who is this for, what tension does it create, what status roles does it engage, and what story makes the change happen.

**H. Adapt a culturally load-bearing behavior instead of fighting it** `[f1188b0479f4]`
1. Identify the underlying human need the current behavior serves (status, community-binding, empowerment, rite of passage) — not just the surface action.
2. Confirm rational argument alone won't shift it (it usually won't, if the behavior is genuinely status-linked).
3. Design a new behavior that satisfies the same underlying need and confers equivalent status.
4. Anchor the new behavior inside the existing cultural system (its language, its milestones, its community) rather than importing an outside framework.
5. Make the new behavior visible and shareable so it can become the new normalized story ("people like us do this now").

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Smallest-viable-market check.** Ask: who would genuinely miss this brand if it disappeared? Good: you can name a specific, bounded group. Bad: the honest answer is "no one in particular" or "everyone." Fix: narrow the target using worldview, not demographics, and re-run the three-sentence promise. `[cdc515a379b0]` `[9696a0aeb3b7]`
- **XY-crowding check.** Plot every real alternative on your chosen axes. Good: your position sits on an edge with few neighbors. Bad: you're clustered in the same quadrant as most competitors. Fix: pick a different axis pair, or move toward a genuine extreme you can operationally deliver. `[cdddb1268ba4]` `[93e68a951096]`
- **Commodity-swap check.** Ask: if a customer were switched from you to your nearest competitor without being told, would they notice or care? Good: yes, clearly. Bad: no — you have a commodity, not a brand. Fix: identify and commit to a specific promise the competitor doesn't make. `[dfa4179985351]`
- **Worldview-vs-yours check.** Before shipping a positioning or policy decision, ask whether it reflects the customer's worldview or your own (JCPenney's Ron Johnson mistake). Good: backed by customer language from reviews/support/UGC. Bad: backed only by internal taste or the founder's own preferences. Fix: pull direct customer language before finalizing. `[3a3aa51fd789]`
- **Tension-vs-fear check.** Read the copy aloud and ask whether it creates a resolvable pull toward action or a coercive, scary push. Good: reader feels "I don't want to miss this." Bad: reader feels manipulated, pressured, or scared. Fix: remove manufactured scarcity; make any real scarcity/deadline true and disclosed. `[e1cc02e44a65]`
- **Affiliation-vs-dominion mismatch check.** Compare your creative's language (winning/power vs. belonging/trust) against how your actual customers talk about the category in reviews and support tickets. Good: matched. Bad: brand talks dominion, customers talk affiliation (or vice versa). Fix: rewrite the mismatched creative; consider running two variants for two segments rather than one blended message. `[bbfb0fef7597]`
- **Semiotic-scan check.** Have someone unfamiliar with the brand look at the storefront, PDP, and packaging for 5 seconds and say what it reminds them of. Good: the association matches the intended promise. Bad: it reads as generic, dated, or (worst case) scam-adjacent. Fix: audit typefaces, photography style, and page design against category "trust codes," and consider hiring a professional designer rather than relying on in-house taste. `[b1ae1eab635d]` `[b4d995b1ac08]`
- **Funnel LTV-vs-CAC check.** Compute LTV (per procedure E) and compare to actual cost per order. Good: LTV clearly exceeds cost per order — safe to scale spend. Bad: LTV is close to or below cost per order. Fix: don't scale; fix funnel steps or improve retention/repeat-purchase rate first, per the explicit rule "if you can't see the funnel, don't buy the ads." `[4c18bce6cf22]` `[03bc266e06a4]`
- **Frequency-before-death check.** Before killing a message or channel, ask how many times a typical customer has actually seen/heard it, and compare against your own internal boredom threshold. Good: the audience has had meaningful repeated exposure and still shows no signal. Bad: internal team fatigue, not audience fatigue, is driving the decision to change. Fix: apply the Jay Levinson rule — change it only "when your accountant is tired of it." `[440b65714a23]`
- **Superuser-identification check.** Pull actual behavior data (repeat purchase, UGC, referrals, reviews) rather than relying on stated loyalty. Good: you can name specific customers who are already advocating. Bad: no one stands out, or you're guessing based on order size alone. Fix: build a lightweight "who's already doing our marketing for us" tracking habit before investing in a formal ambassador program. `[b1d6d86a7bc5]` `[needs brand data: repeat-purchase and UGC/referral data by customer]`
- **Brand-vs-direct measurement check.** For every marketing line item, ask: is this being measured as direct-response (clicks, conversions, CAC) or brand (patience, consistency, no measurement expected)? Good: each line item is judged by its own correct yardstick. Bad: brand spend is being killed for lacking a CAC, or direct spend is being kept alive on "awareness" grounds with no conversion tracking. Fix: reclassify the line item and apply the matching rule from principle 48. `[025ead1f1433]` `[4a787df73c0a]`

## 7. Examples and case results

All figures below are as stated in the source; none are independently verified in this file.

- **Penguin Magic** (magic-trick ecommerce retailer): trick-demonstration videos that show the result without revealing the method — over a billion combined views at zero distribution cost; targets amateurs (who constantly need new tricks) over professionals (who need only 10-20); 82,000+ product reviews from fellow magicians, driving fast quality-cycling; 300+ hosted lectures, ~100 live conventions, an email list "of tens of thousands"; 16,000+ items carried. `[db8055815e43]`
- **VisionSpring** (social enterprise selling reading glasses in Indian villages): manufacturing cost ~$2/pair, sold for ~$3/pair. Initially, offering a full try-on/choice experience from 10 styles converted only ~1/3 of people who needed and could afford glasses. Reframing the transaction as loss-aversion ("here are your new glasses — pay $3 to keep them, or give them back") roughly doubled the conversion rate. `[d1c228542d6b]`
- **By the Way Bakery** (Godin's wife's company): "the largest gluten-free bakery of its kind in the world"; stated change: "we want to make sure no one is left out... we change hosts from exclusive to inclusive, and guests from outsiders to insiders." `[8ed5982aa5df]`
- **JCPenney (2011)**: new CEO Ron Johnson (ex-Apple) ended discounts/coupon culture based on his own luxury-retail worldview; sales fell more than 50 percent because it abandoned the bargain-hunting customers who were Penney's actual true fans. `[3a3aa51fd789]`
- **Union Square Cafe / Union Square Hospitality Group (USHG)** (Danny Meyer): could serve only 600 diners/day, so its founding strategy was curating which 600, not chasing volume. Later, in 2016, USHG eliminated tipping across its restaurants and raised menu prices ~20% to fund parental leave and fair, professional-style wages — explicitly framed as a change "not for everyone" (some big-tippers lost a status ritual) that deepened loyalty among fairness/dignity-minded diners. `[f52853b6346f]` `[7c300cb947347]`
- **Stack Overflow vs. Experts Exchange** (Joel Spolsky, cofounder Jeff Atwood): Experts Exchange tricked Google's crawlers into indexing its answers, then hid the answers from human visitors until they paid a $300/year subscription ("profit via frustration" — a named anti-pattern). Stack Overflow instead made all Q&A free and visible, monetizing through job-board ads, and served three distinct audiences differently (fast-ranked answers for askers, reputation/status systems for answerers, self-service hiring for job posters). Now 250+ employees, millions of weekly visits, profitable. `[ee75e1138f82]` (context)
- **Trident Booksellers and Café** (Boston): survived competition from Amazon by adding coffee — "a coffee shop that sells books," where the book itself becomes "a souvenir of the personal connections made" in the store. `[e2e793b2cda6]`
- **"The Blue Ribbons"** (Godin's own town): a school budget vote failed once amid a tax revolt; with 8 days before the revote, activists tied 100 blue ribbons to a tree at the middle school instead of campaigning directly. The visible "people like us support this" signal spread to dozens of trees townwide within days; the budget passed 2-to-1 on the revote. `[1381647866a6]`
- **Robin Hood Foundation gala (2015)**: raised $101,000,000 in one night — described as the most effective fundraiser of its kind in history at the time, attributed to peer-pressure/status-trading among Wall Street donors cultivated over a generation, not the gala format itself. `[8e5f86a776a2]` `[2b7413eac695]`
- **Lion Guardians / Maasai lion conservation** (Dr. Leela Hazzah): Maasai adolescent males historically proved bravery by killing a lion solo, contributing to a population decline from an estimated 200,000 a generation ago to ~30,000. Hazzah's team introduced a new rite of passage — finding, naming, tracking, and saving lions via radio telemetry — preserving the same community/bravery/status functions without the underlying harm. `[f1188b0479f4]`
- **Facebook's mass-market chasm crossing**: launched at Harvard (high-status, status-anxious students), spread Ivy League school by school via a status ratchet (more friends on Facebook = higher status, and early users were already high-status peers), then to other colleges, then the public — described as one of very few brands (alongside, arguably, none fully, since even Starbucks and Heineken "haven't made it all the way") to cross the full mass-market chasm. `[bc48055c28ea]`
- **HugDug.com** (Godin's own failed venture at Squidoo): let users build a 4-minute Amazon-affiliate page for a loved product, splitting the royalty with a chosen charity. Thesis: authors and Pinterest-style users would evangelize at scale. Result: thousands of pages built (real trial), but fewer than 6 people built more than a dozen pages each (no "whales" emerged); tension dissipated after one visit; some pages sold zero books a month. Named causes: insufficient incentives, no tension dynamic turning early users into ambassadors, no status story, and genuine emotional awkwardness in pushing friends toward a shopping link even when mechanically easy. Explicit lesson: 4 months isn't enough time to force an "overnight success." `[9db7234804f8]`
- **Casey Neistat funnel** (YouTube creator, 10M+ views/video): drove ~1,000,000 video viewers to a Twitch livestream, yielding ~18,000 clicks (~1-in-50), ~1,000 comments (~1-in-18), and ~5 further sign-ups — offered as a normal, steep funnel shape, with the creator's real advantage being an already-enrolled top-of-funnel audience, not funnel optimization. `[6f6fc2d540e9]`
- **Grateful Dead**: one Billboard top-40 hit ever; grossed $350M+ in touring revenue while Jerry Garcia was alive (plus $100M+ since his death), mostly at ~$23 average ticket price; a typical 1972 show drew only ~5,000 people; success required over a decade of "significant patience" plus "the guts to be quirky" while contemporaries (the Zombies, the Doors, the Turtles, named) outsold them at the time. `[b5df5fe9a17d]`
- **The NRA**: ~5 million members (under 2% of the US population) shifted "the attitude and focus of thousands of lawmakers," via focused smallest-viable-market mobilization and comfort saying "it's not for you" to outsiders — presented neutrally by Godin as a proof of the underlying mechanism, with an explicit personal disclaimer ("the NRA isn't my version of 'better'"). `[context, no single dedicated card]`
- **Tesla Model S launch**: framed as breaking the story of existing luxury-car ownership rather than meeting an unmet functional need (early buyers already had "perfectly fine cars"). Positioned on three simultaneous extremes: fastest, safest, and most efficient car of its size, "ever." Named contrast: Ford, GM, and Toyota had the technology and resources to attempt something similar but didn't, because (per the book's own logic) "car companies like us don't take risks like this," and their existing customers shared that risk-averse worldview. `[1fe8a3dca718]`
- **LEED certification** (US Green Building Council): launched at only ~2 buildings/day certified; at that pace it would have taken "a hundred years" to reach the volume actually reached within 12 years, driven by developers' need to answer "what will I tell my boss/board/investors" with a status narrative rather than "I bought the cheapest option." `[context, discussed under the chasm-crossing card]`
- **Water Health International (WHI)**: sells purified water via village kiosks with a distinctive branded Jerry can (a visible status/conversation badge); sends reps with microscope projectors into local schools so children see live parasite/germ images in their own household water samples, then pressure parents at home — crossing the local adoption chasm within months via proximity-based social status pressure rather than digital network effects. `[bc48055c28ea]` (context)
- **Slack**: grew fastest of its category despite near-zero individual desire to switch workplace tools, by first pattern-matching to neophiliacs (marketed as new/cool software) and then triggering a peer-to-peer pattern interrupt via network effects ("Slack is better when your coworkers use it," "you're missing out" social pressure). `[8c7a16df0f13]` `[2a2f3f7c6950]`

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Skipping straight to "how do I get the word out" (step 4 of 5) before doing the earlier work of understanding the customer's worldview and building something worth talking about. `[1a1c714f09a8]`
- Imposing your own worldview on customers whose worldview differs (JCPenney's Ron Johnson, sales down more than 50%). `[3a3aa51fd789]`
- Reverse-engineering a "change" story to defend a product you already decided to sell, dressed in meaningless buzzwords. `[8ed5982aa5df]`
- Picking a grandiose, unresourceable change as a comfortable excuse for staying stuck. `[8ed5982aa5df]`
- Trying to build a brand, a position, or a price for "everyone" — mass positioning forces boring, average compromises that satisfy no one enough to talk about. `[dfa4179985351]` `[cdddb1268ba4]`
- Assuming "all publicity is good publicity" — publicity grows reach but doesn't reliably grow trust or push perception in a positive direction. `[context, discussed alongside brand-definition cards]`
- Confusing brand marketing with direct marketing and applying the wrong measurement standard to either (killing patient brand work for lacking a CAC, or excusing unmeasured "awareness" ad spend). `[025ead1f1433]` `[4a787df73c0a]`
- Buying ads before the funnel can be measured, or scaling ad spend before LTV is known to exceed cost per order. `[4c18bce6cf22]`
- Lowering price to try to earn trust on a big-ticket item — the source's explicit claim is this does the opposite. `[e8ed1256772e]`
- Racing to be the cheapest as a substitute for having a generous, differentiated story — "the last refuge of a marketer who has run out of generous ideas." `[19792f53199d]`
- Chasing generic-term SEO rankings as a primary acquisition strategy when the math (thousands of competing pages) can't support most businesses winning it. `[e3dbb41cbcdb]`
- Treating a promised "self-running, passive" ad funnel as realistic; most brands that matter are "primed by advertising but built by good marketing." `[f10089962425]`
- Killing a message or channel out of internal team boredom before the audience has had meaningful repeated exposure to it. `[440b65714a23]`
- Building a referral/UGC program with no real incentive, no tension dynamic, and no status story for the sharer — the HugDug failure pattern. `[9db7234804f8]`
- Treating stunts (offensive, juvenile, urgent, selfish attention grabs) as equivalent to genuine remarkability; audiences reciprocate by shunning and censuring, not sharing favorably. `[5f5e55f7d191]`
- Trying to serve every customer equally well with fixed resources, instead of identifying and over-investing in actual superusers. `[b1d6d86a7bc5]`
- Assuming early-adopter enthusiasm will automatically carry a product across the chasm to the mass market without a deliberate network-effect bridge. `[894eb8e3217061]`
- Using status-based marketing carelessly — you cannot know from the outside whether a person believes their own external status or craves more of it (Frank Sinatra example); status marketing is explicitly called "walking on thin ice." `[963941c44199]`
- Copying a famous tactic (a gala format, a viral campaign) without recreating the underlying cohort dynamics, history, and status economics that actually drove the result (Robin Hood Foundation misread). `[2b7413eac695]`

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **This is one author's synthesized framework, not a research study.** Nearly every number in this file (the 1,000 true fans heuristic, the Yoyodyne open/response rates, the $100→$125 ad-profit ratio, the baker margin math) is Godin's own illustrative figure or a single named company's self-reported history, not a controlled measurement. Several cards explicitly flag this ("the source's opinion," "not a benchmark," "unsourced"). Treat every number in section 4 as a worked example of the underlying logic, not a target to hit.
- **The book gives almost no ecommerce-specific content.** There is no PDP-copy template, no ad-platform mechanic, no Shopify/Amazon-seller example anywhere in the source text. Every ecommerce application in this file's Principles, Procedures, and Diagnostics sections (funnel math applied to a store, XY grid applied to product pages, semiotics applied to packaging) is this file's own adaptation of Godin's general marketing framework — not a claim Godin makes about ecommerce specifically. The cards in `data/library-v3/cards.json` for this source already do a version of this adaptation; this file follows the cards' lead but the underlying source is a general marketing book, not a retail playbook.
- **The book explicitly conflicts with itself on "hits" vs. smallest viable market**, and Godin flags this himself: chapters 4, 8, and 21 argue hard for smallest-viable-market focus, but chapter 8 also explicitly says "someone is going to make hits, and it's probably not going to be you... if you can find a playbook... go for it." Treat "chase depth, not mass" as the book's default advice for most operators, not a universal law — some categories genuinely reward a hit-machine strategy (Taylor Swift/Big Machine Records is the book's own counter-example).
- **The "refuse to measure brand marketing" instruction is a strong opinion that conflicts with common modern practice.** Most current marketing-measurement guidance (including brand-lift studies, incrementality testing, and MMM) explicitly tries to make brand spend at least partially measurable. Godin's binary (direct = measure everything, brand = measure nothing) is presented as his own rule, not as consensus, and should be weighed against a brand's actual measurement capability rather than applied as written.
- **Godin's ethics discussion (chapters 22-23) is his own moral argument, not empirical.** His claim that failing to market a genuinely valuable offering is itself "stealing" from people who would have benefited is a strong, contestable opinion with no supporting data; it is included in this file's notes but not elevated to a "principle" with a card citation, because no card in this source treats it as an actionable rule.
- **Several case studies are dated or context-specific and need a live check before repeating to a client**: Uber's "surge pricing cost billions in trust" claim, USHG's no-tipping policy status, and the exact mechanics of Spotify's RapCaviar or Facebook's early growth loop should all be verified before use in current client-facing material. `[web check: Uber pricing model and public trust perception since 2018; USHG tipping policy status; current Spotify playlist curation model]`
- **Thin on "how" in places.** The book gives almost no procedural detail on: how to actually build a semiotic style guide, how to run an A/B test on positioning, or how to calculate LTV with anything beyond the simple multiplication shown in chapter 19 — several cards note the source "does not supply a numeric benchmark" or "gives no measurement method." Where this file's Procedures section adds structure (e.g., the step-by-step LTV formula in Procedure E), that structure is this file's own operationalization of the source's narrative example, not a verbatim method from the book.
