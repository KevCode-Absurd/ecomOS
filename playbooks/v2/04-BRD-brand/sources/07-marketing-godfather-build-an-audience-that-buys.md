## 1. What this source is for
Gives a philosophical baseline for organic, word-of-mouth brand building: a definition of marketing, a test for whether a product deserves organic spread, a method for choosing who to build for, a way to shape the story, a way to design for customers to spread the word themselves, and a rule for how long to persist. It is a mindset and framing source, not a channel how-to or a platform tutorial. Use it to judge whether a product, a positioning, or a story is worth pursuing organically before spending on paid interruption; do not look here for funnel mechanics or ad-platform tactics (see 06-MEA and 11-ADC for those, per the folder header).

## 2. Principles

1. Marketing is creating the conditions for an idea to spread, not interrupting people to push an average product. It spreads because the people you serve benefit from telling their friends, not because you push harder. Godin frames this as his own definition, deliberately separating himself from "hustle or system" sellers — treat it as a positioning stance he holds, not an industry standard. [f36e1af62b85]
2. The remarkable benchmark: a product is remarkable only if it is literally worth a customer making a remark about it, not merely good or "neat." Evidence: TOMS shoes (visible, worn in public, conversational) succeeded at word-of-mouth where TOMS coffee (consumed privately, label unseen) did not. [800ade0c28a2]
3. Choose acquisition channels for personal and brand fit, not for the size of the algorithm's audience. Godin's own case: a blog with about a million readers, no TikTok, Facebook, or Instagram use — other people talk about him there instead. He calls social platforms' creator relationship "unpaid employees ... doing what the social media companies tell you to do." This is his personal strategy built on an already-established audience, not a blanket rule against social media; he frames it as a fit decision. [6ff737f56ea5]
4. Vanity reach is a false proxy. Large view counts that don't convert are a trap: he cites people who got 40 million TikTok views and sold about $200 of product — "if you need 40 million views every time you want to make 200 bucks you're in really big trouble." Judge organic content by revenue and owned-audience growth, not views or follower counts, while not discarding all early-stage awareness value outright. [35873b25b23b]
5. Copy a proven business model; spend originality on the product's story and experience, not the operating structure. Example given: franchising already worked for McDonald's and Subway, so don't try to invent a new business structure. Once basic needs (food, shelter, healthcare) are covered, most purchases are really a story about status and affiliation — what kind of person the buyer is and who they want to be seen with. [1e1b6ad921c2]
6. Sell the story, not the object, once tension is present. Using the Birkin bag: every buyer already owned a purse, so what they bought was the ability to be seen carrying it and to have a conversation about owning it. Build tension into the narrative (what happens next, can I do it, will I be left out, will they sell out) because tension causes forward motion; without it, the customer takes no action. [eb0068016eec]
7. Most people are already good at telling stories socially (his example: inviting four friends to dinner and all of them coming is driven by the story of the occasion, not obligation). The blocker to telling a good story about your own offer is fear of being seen and judged, not a lack of storytelling skill. Apply the same structure to an offer: what's the occasion, who else is in, what's at stake, where's the possibility, who is this for. [846111480ab9]
8. Radical/practical empathy: you can sell something you would never personally buy without being a fraud, the same way an oncologist need not have had cancer or a toy inventor need not be a child. The method is practical, not imaginative — observe real people directly (his example: reading the room at small talks) rather than guessing what they want. [22aa44d0250f]
9. Design for the smallest viable market, not for everyone. His hypothetical: an agency built only for pediatric orthodontists, refusing all other clients; after 4 proven clients, demand outstrips capacity because specialists want the best-known specialist for their exact problem. Viability has two bounds: too narrow to sustain a business (his joke example: left-handed red-haired people with a monocle) and too broad to avoid being a commodity (headphones for everyone with ears). [0bc28a1a4edf]
10. Separate decision quality from outcome. Attributed to his friend Annie Duke (identified as a former World Poker Champion): winning a lottery ticket is still a bad decision because no math supports buying one; a good decision (flying, statistically the safest way to travel) can still end in a bad outcome (a crash). Decide from available evidence and history, not from a feeling of confidence, since confidence implies knowing a future you cannot know. [893fba99c769]
11. Run small, real-world tests and watch who responds, then double down on that. His example: his first talks were to six people; he judged them by watching listeners' reactions, not by revenue, and did more of what made a "light switch" turn on. He notes standup comics improve by learning to please open-mic audiences specifically, and may later choose a different venue (weddings) once that skill is proven. Hedging across many customer segments at once, in his read of the interviewer's own story, is a symptom of low confidence, not a strategy. [6ef98e0e935d]
12. Match the story to the audience's existing worldview and context; changing what someone believes is much harder than affirming what they already believe. Core question: what did this person believe before you got here, and what assumptions have they used to get by? Examples: fashion-forward buyers can be told "here's the new fashions," but Lands' End catalog buyers would first need to be trained into a different kind of person; Joshua Bell (an acclaimed classical violinist, not pianist as the transcript's earlier paraphrase suggests) playing in a DC subway was ignored by people who'd pay to see him at the Kennedy Center, because the subway context signaled "not worth listening to." [33a9a29e4bd2]
13. Consistency is the brand promise; authenticity is for amateurs, consistency is for professionals. Customers don't want your authentic bad day; they want the version of you (or the brand) that you promised them — his examples are a doorman or housekeeper having a bad day, and a touring musician who is sick of their hit song but plays it anyway because that's what the audience signed up for. He preempts the obvious pushback: humans have not been fully "authentic" since early childhood (we already moderate behavior for context, e.g. driving at a safe speed instead of our authentic urge to speed) — so this is a service standard, not a demand to fake your identity. He draws a boundary: don't do work you're not proud of, and don't do work that burns you out. [e1a3cf352d92]
14. Make the customer the word-spreader by design; you as the marketer do not spread the word, the customer does, so the real question is what conditions make the customer want to spread it. Visible, social artifacts create the spread loop; private consumption does not (TOMS shoes' visible back-of-shoe logo drove conversation; TOMS coffee, consumed alone with no visible label, did not). [141441a7be8f]
15. Show up regularly, consistently and generously for years to earn permission to follow up and to earn enrollment (the audience's willingness to be taught). His own example: he wrote his blog daily for 5 years before meaningful readership. Attributed rule from his late writing partner Jay Levinson: you don't change your work when you get bored with it, your spouse gets bored, or your employees get bored — you change it when your accountant gets bored (i.e. when the numbers force it, not when motivation dips). [6a08abe16376]
16. The Dip: locate the hardest point of a venture before you start, and if you lack the resources or stamina to get through it, don't start at all. He states plainly that "most people quit too late, they don't quit too soon" — most projects should never have been started in the first place, given the resources committed. This directly contradicts the common encouragement to "just keep pushing through" as universal advice; his qualifier is that it applies to under-resourced or unprepared projects, not to every setback. [0f5eadcb74ad]
17. Apply the Dip check operationally to store-level portfolio decisions: before launching a demanding initiative (new product line, new channel, long content commitment), map where the hardest stretch will fall and estimate the budget, team attention and founder energy needed to get through it. If you're already mid-dip without a plan, this framing supports stopping rather than grinding blindly; his rule for killing something is to quit early, at the low-commitment point, not mid-dip when sunk cost and exhaustion cloud judgment. [4408b6534f31]

## 3. Procedures

**The five steps of marketing** (stated directly by Godin as his own sequence; the interviewer frames step 4 as "the one everyone gets excited about"):
1. Invent a thing that's worth making, with a story worth telling and a contribution worth talking about. Test: does the product pass the remarkable benchmark (principle 2)? Do not invent a new business structure; copy one that already works (principle 5).
2. Design and build it in a way that a few people will particularly benefit from and care about. This is the "smallest viable market" step (principle 9): pick a group narrow enough to serve exceptionally, wide enough to sustain the business.
3. Tell a story that matches the built-in narrative and dreams of that tiny group (principle 12): start from what they already believe, weave in status, affiliation, community and tension (principle 6).
4. Spread the word — reframed: you don't spread the word, your customer does. Design the conditions (visible artifact, social script, a reason to mention it) that make the customer want to tell others (principle 14).
5. Show up regularly, consistently and generously for years to organize, lead and build confidence in the change you want to make, to earn permission to follow up and to earn enrollment to teach (principle 15).
No further sub-steps or settings are given for any of the five; the source is thin on tactical "how" beyond the examples cited under each principle. Do not add implementation detail the source did not give.

**Practical-empathy test procedure** (for founder discomfort or unclear audience fit):
1. Start with a small, real audience instead of guessing (his own first talks were to six people).
2. Observe reactions directly rather than judging by revenue at this stage — watch for the "light switch on" response.
3. Do more of whatever produced that response; let the audience "train" you as much as you train them.
4. Once a specific venue or angle is proven at small scale, consider moving to a different venue that suits the proven skill (his standup-comic example: open mic first, weddings later), instead of scaling the same room bigger.
This is presented as a general method (speaking, standup comedy, food service) with no ecommerce-specific steps in the source; card 22aa44d0250f offers an ecommerce translation (read support tickets, watch session recordings, run post-purchase surveys) as an adaptation, not something Godin said.

**The Dip pre-launch check**:
1. Before starting, identify where the dip will be: the stretch where it feels hardest and least likely to succeed, which is when most people quit.
2. Honestly assess whether you have the resources and stamina to survive that stretch.
3. If not, do not start; there is nothing wrong with staying at a day job or freelancing instead.
4. If you do start, plan in advance for "where to put the tired" (his mile-22 marathon framing): decide before the dip hits how you will keep going.
5. Size the project to your actual runway — his instruction is to "find a small problem, make a promise, do it for the people in that audience, keep the promise, and then do it again," not to attempt something requiring resources you don't have (his example: you don't have $40 billion to build an AI engine that changes the world).

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Remarkable test**: for each candidate product, write the exact sentence a customer would say about it to a friend. If you cannot produce a specific, non-generic sentence, the product is not remarkable as defined here; either redesign it (visible design, packaging, ritual, story) or plan to sell it through paid interruption instead, since organic spread will not carry it. [800ade0c28a2]
- **Vanity-metric check**: for any content or creator campaign, compute revenue attributable to the traffic, not views, likes or follower counts. Flag any metric that doesn't connect to purchases, repeat purchases or owned-audience growth (email list, community) as a false proxy, while keeping some allowance for early-stage awareness value. [35873b25b23b]
- **Niche viability check**: confirm the target group clears two bounds — not so narrow that even total market capture can't sustain the business, and not so broad that you're competing as an interchangeable commodity. [0bc28a1a4edf]
- **Worldview check**: before writing creative or picking a channel, ask what the audience already believes and what assumptions they use to get by. If the campaign requires first changing what they believe, expect it to be much harder than a campaign that affirms an existing belief; if a campaign underperforms despite good execution, check whether it fought the audience's frame instead of using it. [33a9a29e4bd2]
- **Decision-quality check** (not outcome): when reviewing a past experiment at a retro, ask whether the reasoning and evidence were sound at the time it was made, separately from whether revenue went up or down afterward. Don't let a lucky win validate a bad process, and don't let an unlucky loss condemn a good one. [893fba99c769]
- **Visible-artifact check**: before expecting a product to generate referrals, check whether it is consumed or used in a way others can see (like a TOMS shoe logo) or privately with no visible trace (like TOMS coffee). No visible artifact means design a different, visible trigger for conversation (packaging, unboxing moment, public use) or don't expect the word-of-mouth loop to fire on its own. [141441a7be8f]
- **Dip check**: before starting a demanding initiative, ask where the hardest stretch will fall and whether you have the resources and stamina to get through it. If not, the source's answer is not to start (or to keep it a side project/day job), not to start anyway and hope. [0f5eadcb74ad]

## 7. Examples and case results

All figures below are Godin's own claims or the interviewer's paraphrase, unaudited, and are marked as examples, not proof:
- **Google and Facebook**: per Godin, neither ran ads "for years and years and years" during their early growth; presented as evidence that word-of-mouth can outperform paid interruption for a genuinely remarkable product. [f36e1af62b85]
- **iPhone**: claimed to have spread because people heard a distinctive ringtone and asked about it, not because of advertising. [f36e1af62b85]
- **TOMS shoes vs. TOMS coffee**: shoes (visible logo, seeded with a small number of fashion-forward women who wanted to be seen as philanthropic) scaled into what Godin describes as a large company; coffee (private consumption, no visible label) did not work, because no conversation trigger existed. [141441a7be8f, 1e1b6ad921c2]
- **Fesol/Festool $220 jigsaw**: sold at roughly 10x a commodity jigsaw's price by being the best available on every dimension (design, battery life, feel, packaging, case) rather than competing on price. [0bc28a1a4edf]
- **Hermes Birkin bag**: cited as an example of buying status and a story, not utility — pricing detail is unreliable (see section 4) and should not be reused as fact. [eb0068016eec]
- **By the Bakery (New York)**: Godin's wife's business, gluten-free and dairy-free baked goods; framed as succeeding because guests with dietary restrictions give hosts a natural reason to talk about the product at a dinner. [f36e1af62b85]
- **Joshua Bell subway experiment**: an acclaimed violinist played in a Washington DC subway station and was largely ignored by people who would pay for tickets to see him at a concert hall, used to show that context (not talent) determines whether something is perceived as worth attention. [33a9a29e4bd2]
- **Buddhist-monk-robe street scam**: scammers used the appearance of monks to trigger reciprocity and trust norms in a modern US city; Godin notes the same approach would have failed in 1784 Abilene, Texas, because the cultural belief didn't exist yet there. Presented as a (negative) example of exploiting existing worldview, not an endorsed tactic. [33a9a29e4bd2]
- **Volunteer fire department roadblock collections**: collects small individual donations (about $5 each) by standing in traffic; contrasted with a named-but-unidentified nonprofit client whose average gift is $50,000, to show that the size of the ask changes the story needed. [0bc28a1a4edf]
- **Humane Society / SPCA shelter photo project (hypothetical/illustrative)**: instead of a phone-bank donation ask, inviting local high-school kids to photograph shelter animals for Pet Finder gives the kids status and purpose, and some may adopt while there — framed as a better-designed word-of-mouth mechanism than direct solicitation. - **Pediatric orthodontist agency (explicitly hypothetical)**: Godin says "if I had to start an agency today" he would serve only pediatric orthodontists exclusively; after 4 proven clients he expects a line out the door, and after 20 clients he'd never need to prospect again. ## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Chasing algorithmic reach or vanity metrics (views, followers) that don't convert to revenue. [35873b25b23b]
- Trying to invent a wholly new business structure instead of copying one that already works. [1e1b6ad921c2]
- Selling an average or commodity product and expecting a growth tactic to fix it — "if the plan is to sell average products to average people, no growth hack will fix the underlying problem." [f36e1af62b85]
- Hedging across many customer segments at once instead of committing to a smallest viable market; Godin's read is that this comes from low confidence, not from strategy. [6ef98e0e935d]
- Confusing personal authenticity with the brand's promised consistency, and letting a bad day degrade the customer's experience. [e1a3cf352d92]
- Designing a product for private, invisible consumption when the goal is word-of-mouth (TOMS coffee's failure mode). [141441a7be8f]
- Starting a demanding project (his marathon analogy) without the training, resources or time to survive its hardest stretch, then quitting mid-way from lack of preparation rather than lack of effort. [0f5eadcb74ad]
- Buying a Super Bowl ad expecting it to "work" as direct response; Godin calls this "almost always a terrible decision" that mainly sends a signal to certain audiences, not a performance-marketing move — stated as his opinion, flagged for verification against your own scale and category. [33a9a29e4bd2]
- Trying to change what an audience already believes instead of starting from their existing worldview. [33a9a29e4bd2]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Self-reported and unverified**: every number in this source (blog readership, 5-year daily posting, speaking history, TOMS/Google/Facebook/Apple claims) is Godin's own account or a named-but-unaudited third-party story. Treat all of it as anecdote, never as a benchmark to hit. [applies across all cards]
- **Conflicts with common startup advice**: "most people quit too late, they don't quit too soon" runs against widely repeated advice to persist through every setback. Godin's own qualifier is that this applies to under-resourced or unprepared projects specifically, not to every hard moment — the playbook should preserve both his claim and this qualifier rather than flattening it into a universal rule. [0f5eadcb74ad]
- **Conflicts with common "be authentic" brand advice**: many brand-strategy sources push founders and brands to "be authentic" as a differentiator; Godin explicitly argues the opposite ("authenticity is for amateurs, consistency is for professionals") and anticipates the pushback in the transcript itself. Both positions exist in the broader marketing literature; this file states Godin's side only, marked as his framing. [e1a3cf352d92]
- **Conflicts with paid-acquisition-first playbooks**: the source's channel-fit advice (don't play the algorithm game) reflects Godin's own already-large earned audience; a new or small store may need paid or platform reach before any word-of-mouth mechanism has a base to amplify. This is called out in card 6ff737f56ea5 itself, not added here. [6ff737f56ea5]
- **Thin on "how"**: the five-step framework and most principles state what to aim for (remarkable, smallest viable market, story, spread, persistence) without giving a repeatable, general-purpose method for producing any of them — the "how" is almost entirely inference from analogies (jigsaws, Birkin bags, pizza shops) rather than a stated procedure.
