## 1. What this source is for

It gives one Chief Design Officer's blow-by-blow account of rebranding an existing, already-selling physical product company using the Double Diamond framework (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver). It helps an operator sequence a full rebrand: what research to gather before touching visuals, how to turn that research into a small number of positioning themes, how to explore and pick a visual direction without committing to custom design too early, and what to actually ship at the end (guidelines, site, packaging, content capability, product). It does not cover how to run the underlying customer interviews (routed to 02-AVT) or how to wireframe and build the website (routed to 07-LPG); this source only covers the identity and process layer around those.

## 2. Principles

1. A rebrand is a decision about what to keep as much as what to change. State explicitly why you are changing anything; the source's framing is that even a healthy, already-growing business updates its brand because its opinions, values and market understanding sharpened over time, not because growth stalled. Condition: applies when redesigning a business that already sells, not a from-scratch launch. Evidence: Mode's own guiding questions ("Where have we been... what's worth keeping and what needs to change for our business to grow?"). [5a833673f348]

2. Treat brand as the customer's gut feeling about the company, shaped by every touchpoint, not as logo, colors and type. Condition: use this framing before starting any visual work, to keep changes tied to how they will be perceived rather than to taste. Evidence: source's stated definition, "A brand is a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or company." [5a833673f348]

3. Audit brand equity before replacing any asset. Condition: applies to logos, product names and packaging with existing recognition. Evidence: Mode kept its existing logo unchanged because it already worked and had built-in equity; effort went into refined usage rules, not replacement. [5a833673f348] [a913f3dfc742]

4. A rebrand naturally forces catalog decisions, not only visual ones. Condition: expect this when brand positioning sharpens; some products may no longer fit. Evidence: Mode's Define phase ended in discontinuing certain products to sharpen focus on the ones that remained; no revenue figure is given for what was cut. [5a833673f348] [ee667d0641e8]

5. Competitive positioning is found by plotting opposing qualities on an X/Y map, using both direct competitors and out-of-category brands. Condition: use once brand attributes are already defined, since attributes supply the axis choices. Evidence: Mode's map used axes like understated vs. outspoken, opaque vs. transparent and human, warm and inviting vs. stark and industrial, curated vs. raw and unedited, premium vs. budget. Plotting non-competitor brands revealed space that category-only comparison hid. [84c08bf1872c]

6. Read the map for crowded pools, then ask what must change to carve out unique space. Condition: applies wherever the map shows the brand clustered with competitors on both axes. Evidence: Mode found itself "swimming in a crowded pool" in some areas with little to differentiate it, which produced its final positioning themes, "Warm Tech" and "creating joy in everyday experiences." [84c08bf1872c]

7. Build three stylescapes (wide-layout, cross-touchpoint mood boards) before commissioning any custom design. Condition: use once strategy is agreed but the look is not; skip if there is no time or budget to explore multiple directions. Evidence: Mode built "Serious Fun," "Crafted Narrative" and "Bits of Joy," evaluated them against team resonance and against the previously defined brand values, then merged elements of all three into a final direction, "The Story of Craft." Three is the source's working number and a stated opinion, not a measured optimum. [3d381bacc1a4]

8. Assign typography by the job the copy does, not by a single family for everything. Condition: applies whenever a brand mixes expressive copy with technical or spec copy. Evidence: Mode used serif and sans-serif for headers and body, a monospace font specifically for technical copy, and bitmap-style type used selectively as a retro nod. Combinations were tested in a type specimen and then in context on real sample pages, because a pairing that works in isolation can fail against real layout, images and buttons. [acd8f5a64996]

9. Anchor the color palette with one memorable core color plus a small set of designated accent colors, not a flat palette of equals. Condition: applies wherever the palette must work across print (packaging) and screen (website) simultaneously. Evidence: Mode anchored on a deep green chosen because it felt "bold yet calming" through its tie to nature, expressing the "Warm Tech" theme; the source calls color review "one of the most intense parts of the process." No contrast or accessibility threshold is given. [9b48715e763f]

10. Set photography as a short list of explicit, written constraints, paired with reference images, not a loose folder of inspiration. Condition: applies when briefing photography or UGC selection for a store, packaging or social creative. Evidence: Mode's four rules: strong directional lighting; warm and natural tones with subtle film grain; scenes that feel lived-in but not messy; people featured in a way that does not detract from the product. The source's own caveat: these are one brand's chosen style, not a universal rule, and film grain plus warm grading can misrepresent true product color. [8a3a065898f8]

11. Strip technical jargon and marketing buzzwords from brand voice even in a technical product category. Condition: applies when copy has drifted into insider language that alienates non-expert buyers. Evidence: Mode's target balance was explicitly "professional but not corporate, friendly but not fake," drafted collaboratively in a shared Notion document over multiple passes; simplifying voice does not mean removing the deep technical specs research-heavy buyers still need. [3cac34e1c33d]

12. Segment buyers by behavior and required treatment, not by demographics alone, and concentrate research time on segments you do not already understand. Condition: applies when the audience spans expert hobbyists to busy first-time buyers who would need different site and marketing treatment. Evidence: Mode built "Tim" (experienced UX manager, researches extensively, values community and quality, would enjoy kits) against "Jay" (busy software engineer, relies on influencer and publication recommendations, wants a simple purchase path and ready-to-use products); Mode already knew its current customers by name, so exercises focused on prospective new ones. [a04096752c25]

13. Sequence a site redesign as sitemap first, then two placeholder-content sample pages, then full build and gradual rollout. Condition: applies to a major template overhaul that must serve more than one type of user journey. Evidence: Mode's sitemap mapped every page and route before design started; two sample pages tested components (menus, typography, buttons, galleries) and the overall aesthetic; the rest of the site then took weeks and rolled out gradually through the year, with early work revisited as the team learned. [c46421294324]

14. Define brand attributes through four separate, sequential brainstorming lenses before positioning work: values and beliefs, features and benefits, voice and tone, look and feel. Condition: run this before competitive mapping, since attributes supply the map's axes. Evidence: Mode used focused prompts, free brainstorming, then grouped and prioritized the resulting word clusters into themes; the session included the CEO, head of marketing, the design lead and an outside facilitator, deliberately included so the design lead could focus on vision while someone else guided the process and challenged assumptions. [a85cb5eecad5]

15. Convert discovery synthesis into a small number of "How might we" design challenges that then filter every downstream decision. Condition: use once discovery findings converge on repeated themes. Evidence: Mode's three: thoughtfully crafted rather than mass-produced products that bring joy and warmth; a brand identity that feels high-end and curated yet humble and human; a website that guides both newcomers and enthusiasts. A single customer line ("a nice keyboard can be like a piece of furniture that ties the room together") became a stated guiding product principle. [ee667d0641e8]

16. Package the finished identity into a brand book with logo usage, one cohesive visual language, explicit language rules with ready messaging samples, and a separate "brand expressions" showing the brand applied across surfaces as thought-starters, not final assets. Condition: use once the identity work is otherwise settled, to keep future creative consistent without re-deciding basics each time. Evidence: Mode also formalized a secondary "alter-ego" visual language (lo-fi bitmap, retro-computing imagery) as a named, contained layer driving custom iconography, a logo variant and a photo treatment, kept separate from the core system. [a913f3dfc742]

17. Measure rebrand progress by shipped delivery artifacts, not by strategy documents. Condition: use when scoping what must ship for a rebrand project to count as done. Evidence: Mode's delivery set was finalized brand guidelines, a website redesign, updated packaging (foam inserts replaced by recyclable molded pulp starting with the next Encore run), a built content-capture studio, and a next-generation product in progress. No revenue or conversion outcome is given for any of it. [1d643039b4fd]

18. Reaching roughly 90% completion is comparatively fast; the final 10% takes the most time and effort. Condition: applies to the Deliver phase of any rebrand; use it to set schedule expectations, not as a literal percentage. Evidence: stated directly by the source at the four-month mark, with rollout continuing for months afterward. [5a833673f348] [a913f3dfc742] [1d643039b4fd]

19. Let the specific moment customers say they connect with the brand dictate the marketing channel investment, not a generic "be customer-centric" statement. Condition: applies when research surfaces one recurring "aha moment." Evidence: Mode's customers connected most when they experienced keyboards firsthand, so the plan was to invest in more in-person creative and tech events plus a transparency push (more story, behind-the-scenes content, team introductions). The source explicitly flags the ecommerce-only equivalent (hands-on unboxing content, community showcases, low-risk returns policies) as an inference, not a tested result, since Mode itself sells a tactile product. [a9b87b90a092]

## 3. Procedures

**A. Discover phase**
1. Assemble a small cross-functional team: CEO, head of marketing, design lead, plus an outside creative facilitator if available. [a85cb5eecad5]
2. Before any brand exercise, align as a team on top-line business goals in writing. Mode's four: establish a distinct brand voice and point of view; deepen understanding of current customers while planning for new ones; update and refine the product catalog; increase revenue over 5 years to fund harder projects. [5a833673f348]
3. Gather quantitative and qualitative research over several weeks before the team meets in person: analyze website traffic and sales data; run customer surveys within the community; conduct in-depth video interviews with specific community members to learn why they buy or pass. [a04096752c25]
4. From that research, segment current and prospective customers into personas with names, constraints, desires and stories. Concentrate this work on segments the team does not already understand by name. [a04096752c25]
5. Map each persona's journey from discovery to purchase; record what influences their decision and what holds them back, so hurdles become a concrete to-do list. [a04096752c25]
6. Run four separate brand-attribute brainstorming sessions with focused prompts: values and beliefs; features and benefits; voice and tone; look and feel. Group similar ideas into clusters and prioritize what resonates most. [a85cb5eecad5]
7. Using the resulting attributes, build an X/Y competitive positioning map with opposing-quality axes; plot both direct competitors and brands from outside the category. Identify crowded pools and the questions they raise about differentiation. [84c08bf1872c]

**B. Define phase**
1. Synthesize Discover-phase findings into a small number of repeated patterns and themes the whole team recognizes (Mode's: "Warm Tech," "creating joy in everyday experiences," "making products that deserve to exist"). [ee667d0641e8]
2. Make the hard call on product lineup: decide what to discontinue to sharpen focus, alongside the visual and messaging overhaul. [5a833673f348] [ee667d0641e8]
3. Write the emerging themes as explicit "How might we" design challenges covering both physical product and digital experience; use these as the acceptance test for every later decision. [ee667d0641e8]

**C. Develop phase**
1. Build stylescapes: identify key themes from Discover and Define, gather matching visual references, adjust them into visual harmony, and arrange each direction on a wide layout that previews the brand across touchpoints (packaging to digital screens). Produce three distinct, named directions. [3d381bacc1a4]
2. Present the three directions; evaluate on two tests: what resonates with the team, and how well each direction matches the already-defined brand values. Ask explicitly which elements people want to borrow across directions. [3d381bacc1a4]
3. Combine the winning elements into one final stylescape rather than picking a single finalist outright. [3d381bacc1a4]
4. Start the largest identity build, the website, because it forces resolution of typography, imagery and color together. First build a comprehensive sitemap of every page and route. [c46421294324]
5. Build two key pages with placeholder content to test components (menus, typography, buttons, galleries) and the overall aesthetic. [c46421294324]
6. Test typeface combinations in a type specimen, then re-test them in context on the two sample pages, before committing. Assign typefaces by job: serif/sans-serif for header and body, monospace for technical copy, bitmap selectively for a retro nod. [acd8f5a64996]
7. Choose the color palette in parallel: pick one anchor color tied to the brand theme, then a small set of secondary accent colors. Review swatches and combinations across multiple dedicated sessions, checked against both packaging (print) and website (screen). [9b48715e763f]
8. Set photography principles as a written short list, paired with reference images: strong directional lighting; warm, natural tones with subtle film grain; lived-in-but-not-messy scenes; people present without detracting from the product. [8a3a065898f8]
9. Draft brand voice collaboratively in a shared document (Mode used Notion), refining over multiple passes against the stated target: professional but not corporate, friendly but not fake, free of jargon and buzzwords. [3cac34e1c33d]
10. Once the two sample pages are approved, build and refine the rest of the site over several weeks. [c46421294324]
11. Carry the "Warm Tech" design challenge into product design: explore forms, colors, materials and finishes, then begin designing the next physical product using that exploration as input. [ee667d0641e8]

**D. Deliver phase**
1. Finalize brand identity guidelines: logo usage rules (keep an equity-carrying logo, document detailed usage rather than replace it), one cohesive visual language for typography/color/imagery, explicit language rules with ready-to-use messaging samples, and a separate "brand expressions" showcase presented as thought-starters, not finished assets. [a913f3dfc742]
2. Formalize any secondary visual language that emerged organically (Mode's lo-fi bitmap "alter-ego") as a named, contained layer, kept separate from the core system, driving iconography, a logo variant and a photo treatment. [a913f3dfc742]
3. Ship the concrete delivery artifacts with owners: website redesign, updated packaging, a content-capture studio setup, and next-generation product design. [1d643039b4fd]
4. Change materials where the brand promise requires it: Mode moved from foam inserts to recyclable molded pulp packaging starting with its next product run. [1d643039b4fd]
5. Roll out gradually rather than as a single launch, and expect to revisit early work as the team learns; budget disproportionate time for the final stretch after reaching about 90% completion. [1d643039b4fd] [a913f3dfc742]
6. Translate the strongest customer-research insight (the specific moment people say they connect with the brand) into a channel decision: Mode chose more in-person events plus a transparency push in content (behind-the-scenes, team introductions) because its "aha moment" was hands-on product experience. [a9b87b90a092]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- Brand equity check: before touching a logo, name or packaging asset, ask whether it already works and has built recognition. Good: keep and refine usage. Bad: if it actively blocks the new positioning, replace it. [5a833673f348]
- Positioning crowding check: on the X/Y map, look for zones where the brand sits on top of competitors on both axes. Good: open space exists on at least one combination of axes. Bad: clustered with competitors; the fix is to ask what must change to carve out distinction, not to keep the same position. [84c08bf1872c]
- Stylescape fit check: score each direction on two axes only, team resonance and match to previously defined brand values. Do not pick a single "winner" outright; check for elements worth merging across directions first. [3d381bacc1a4]
- Typography-in-context check: a pairing that reads well in a type specimen must be re-checked on real sample pages with real layout, images and buttons before it is finalized. Bad: approving type from the specimen alone. [acd8f5a64996]
- Color cross-surface check: review the palette on both physical packaging and the on-screen website; a swatch approved on one medium only is not validated. [9b48715e763f]
- Photography compliance check: hold every shot against the written rule list (lighting, tone/grain, lived-in-not-messy, people-don't-detract-from-product). Good: same feel across vendors and channels. Bad: inconsistent look that forces re-shoots; the source's own caveat is to also check the warm grading and film grain against product color accuracy. [8a3a065898f8]
- Voice check: read copy against the stated poles, professional vs. corporate, friendly vs. fake, and check whether a non-specialist buyer could understand it without help. Bad: jargon or buzzwords remain; good: clear language with the necessary technical specs still available for research-heavy buyers. [3cac34e1c33d]
- Persona differentiation check: confirm at least two personas require genuinely different site or marketing treatment, not just different descriptions. Good: concrete, different requirements (e.g., deep specs and visible community vs. a simple purchase path with ready-to-use options). [a04096752c25]
- Site sequencing check: do not begin full site production before the sitemap and two placeholder pages are approved. Good: components, aesthetic and typography validated on 2 pages; bad: production starts on assumptions untested by placeholder content. [c46421294324]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Treating brand as only logo, colors and type, rather than as the customer's gut feeling shaped by every touchpoint. [5a833673f348]
- Replacing a logo or asset that already carries equity, instead of auditing what to keep first. [5a833673f348] [a913f3dfc742]
- Investing in custom design work before validating direction with stylescapes. [3d381bacc1a4]
- Choosing typefaces from a specimen alone, without testing them in context on real pages, since a pairing can fail against real layout, imagery and buttons. [acd8f5a64996]
- Overusing a specialty or decorative typeface (e.g., bitmap); the source treats it as strictly selective, since overuse dilutes the contrast that makes it meaningful. [acd8f5a64996]
- Approving a color palette on only one medium (screen or print), when it must work on both. [9b48715e763f]
- Letting people in product photography detract from the product itself. [8a3a065898f8]
- Assuming that simplifying brand voice means removing necessary technical specs for research-heavy buyers. [3cac34e1c33d]
- Skipping catalog decisions during a rebrand; assortment is part of the brand decision, not a separate merchandising task. [5a833673f348] [ee667d0641e8]
- Underestimating the final stretch of delivery: reaching ~90% is comparatively easy, the last 10% takes the most time and effort. [a913f3dfc742] [1d643039b4fd]
- Starting full site production before a sitemap and two validated placeholder pages exist. [c46421294324]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- This is a single, first-party case study (Mode, 2025), not a study across brands. Every number in section 4 (weeks of strategy, ~4 months to first deliverables, 3 stylescapes, 2 sample pages, 4 photo rules) is this one company's documented experience, explicitly flagged by several cards as not a benchmark to copy directly.
- No outcome metrics are given anywhere in the source, no revenue, conversion, or brand-lift numbers for the rebrand itself. The source states directly that impact "takes time to see." Treat every delivery artifact as a process output, not proof of a result.
- The video is Shopify-sponsored with two sponsor reads and an explicit Shopify Capital pitch. Per this folder's own scope note ("Skip: Shopify Capital, business funding mechanics"), those passages are excluded from this distillation as promotional, not evaluative, content.
- Axis choice on the X/Y positioning map is explicitly subjective; the source calls it a discussion and alignment tool, not a quantitative scoring model. [84c08bf1872c]
- The specific fonts, the deep-green anchor color, and the four photography rules are this one brand's chosen style, not recommended defaults; the source's own caveat notes a clinical or high-tech brand might deliberately choose the opposite look. [acd8f5a64996] [8a3a065898f8]
- Possible tension worth flagging for the pass-2 folder playbook: the source's photography rule of "subtle film grain" and warm color grading is paired with its own caveat that such grading can misrepresent true product color. This sits against the general ecommerce principle that generated or stylized product imagery should not replace accurate real-product photography where color or fit accuracy matters. [8a3a065898f8]
- The source is thin on how: it names "focused prompts" for the brand-attribute brainstorm but never shows them; it describes video interviews and community surveys but not their script or sample size; it describes sitemap creation but not the sitemap's structure. These mechanics are explicitly routed elsewhere by the folder's own header (interviews to 02-AVT, wireframing and digital journeys to 07-LPG) and are not detailed here because the source itself does not detail them.
- Facilitator use (Ben Burns) is a staffing/vendor decision specific to a funded team; the source's own caveat says a solo operator can substitute a structured written exercise but should expect less outside challenge to assumptions. [a85cb5eecad5]
- The ecommerce-only translation of the "hands-on aha moment" channel finding (unboxing content, community showcases, low-risk returns) is explicitly labeled by the source's card as an inference, not a tested outcome, since Mode's own finding was about in-person, tactile experience. [a9b87b90a092]
