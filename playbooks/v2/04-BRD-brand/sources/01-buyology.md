## 1. What this source is for

This source gives a popular-science account of one commissioned neuromarketing research program (fMRI + SST/EEG) plus a wide catalogue of brand anecdotes, organized around ten psychological mechanisms the author argues explain why people buy: unreliable self-report, product placement, mirror neurons, subliminal cues, ritual and superstition, religion-like brand structures, somatic markers, sensory branding (smell, touch, sight/color, sound), neuromarketing's predictive power, and sex/beauty in advertising. It is useful to an ecommerce brand-building agent as a source of named mechanisms, illustrative brand cases, and a small number of transferable numbers and rules — not as a peer-reviewed evidence base. It does not cover pricing strategy, paid-media mechanics, landing-page layout, or CRO testing methodology in any operational depth; those live in other folders (06-MEA, 07-LPG, 09-CRO, 10-MCR, 11-ADC). Section 9 of this file adds a short, clearly marked block on how each finding might apply to ecommerce brand-building (PDP, packaging, ads, unboxing) — that mapping is this playbook's own adaptation, not a claim the book itself makes about ecommerce specifically (the book never mentions ecommerce, PDPs, or online stores as such).

## 2. Principles

All of the following restate the book's own claims. Every principle traces to a specific chapter/study described in Section 7; treat the underlying evidence exactly as strong (or weak) as that section documents, since the book itself gives no independent statistical detail beyond what's quoted below.

### 2.1 Self-report is an unreliable guide to what people will actually buy

1. **What people say they do and what they actually do diverge, so treat stated preference and revealed behavior as different data sources.** Condition: any time you are deciding what to build, stock or advertise based on survey, interview or focus-group input. Evidence: the book's own cigarette-warning-label study (Section 7.1) found smokers said warnings reduced their smoking while their brains showed the opposite; the Pepsi/Coke taste-test history (Section 7.1) found blind-taste preference for Pepsi did not translate into market share for Pepsi once the brand was revealed. `[0e4c9c6ae0f8]` `[1c02b00e5cfd]` `[b6e70f8909c7]`
2. **Roughly half of purchase decisions are made unconsciously at the point of sale, per one cited study.** Condition: applies to decisions made while physically or visually browsing a shelf or a page. Evidence: the book cites "a recent study conducted by German brand and retail experts, Gruppe Nymphenberg," finding "over 50 percent of all purchasing decisions by shoppers are made spontaneously — and therefore unconsciously — at the point of sale." No sample size, method or publication is given for this figure in the book. `[web check: Gruppe Nymphenberg point-of-sale study methodology and sample size]` `[f19c742bb5e3]`
3. **The author's own summary claim: about 90 percent of buying behavior is unconscious.** Condition: this is Lindstrom's own headline conclusion in the book's conclusion chapter, not a single study's finding — it is a synthesis across all the chapters. `[web check: this specific "90 percent" figure has been publicly challenged as an overstatement not derived from a single controlled measurement]` `[a3640e21d284]`
4. **The "Wanamaker problem" — not knowing which half of ad spend works — persists because companies rely on self-report and aggregate sales data rather than causal tests.** Condition: any brand relying only on sales tracking ("the view from the cash register") or survey research to judge marketing effectiveness. The book quotes John Wanamaker's line and frames it as still true a century later. `[c4890e00c06a]`
5. **New-product failure rates are high, so predictive testing (of any kind) should precede scaling spend.** Condition: any product or SKU launch decision. Numbers as given in the book: "eight out of ten new product launches fail within the first three months" in the US; in Japan the figure is "9.7 out of every ten"; "52 percent of all new brands, and 75 percent of individual products" fail (consumer products); ~156,000 new products launched globally in 2005 (one every three minutes); ~21,000 new brands introduced worldwide per year (IXP Marketing Group). `[web check: these figures are attributed to unnamed or loosely cited sources in the book's endnotes — verify before quoting externally]` `[cce38d2b0f78]`

### 2.2 Product placement and passive branding

6. **Product placement only builds memory and preference when the brand is integrated into the emotional narrative, not merely present on screen.** Condition: any paid placement, sponsorship, or brand-cameo decision. Evidence: the American Idol SST study (Section 7.2) found Coca-Cola (60% of show running time, narratively integrated) gained recall while Ford (traditional 30-second spots only, same $26M annual spend) lost recall versus baseline. `[14e9e290ba3e]` `[0fdfce48d0e0]`
7. **A branded placement that works can actively suppress recall of unbranded/competitor items shown alongside it.** Condition: any environment where your brand and competitors' logos both appear (a shared media buy, a comparison page, a multi-brand event). Evidence: the same American Idol study found post-viewing recall of Coke and Cingular crowded out recall of unrelated logos like Pepsi and Verizon. `[3bdf718b5a61]`
8. **Simple, high-frequency exposure without narrative integration produces indistinguishable, forgettable creative.** Condition: category advertising that repeats the same visual trope across competitors. Evidence: Lindstrom's own small experiment splicing 60 car ads from 20 brands into a single reel, in which he could not tell which brand was which. `[8311a6869eb4]`
9. **Sex, extreme beauty, or highly incongruous imagery can "eat" the very brand message it was meant to carry (the "Vampire Effect").** Condition: any ad or PDP hero image using sexual or shock content. Evidence: the MediaAnalyzer eye-tracking study and the Sex-and-the-City recall study (Section 7.9). `[73f6e00507f3]` `[15242b826b8b]` `[43f966448348]`

### 2.3 Mirror neurons

10. **Observing (or reading about) someone perform an action activates the same brain regions as performing it yourself, which drives imitation of buying behavior.** Condition: any product demo, unboxing video, lifestyle/aspirational imagery, or UGC content. Evidence: Rizzolatti's 1992 macaque studies (Section 7.3) and the UCLA fMRI reading-comprehension study. `[8a5b7d650559]` `[e9c09bb38c14]`
11. **Repeated visual exposure to a product/style can convert dislike into desire purely through imitation, independent of the product's intrinsic merits.** Condition: applies to trend-driven categories (footwear, accessories). Evidence given is anecdotal (the book's own Crocs example) rather than a controlled study. `[3c6a5508abe9]`
12. **Smiling cues increase reported satisfaction, loyalty intent, and even name recall, and should be treated as a real (if small) lever, not just etiquette.** Condition: any customer-facing interaction, imagery, or support touchpoint. Evidence: the "Smiling Study" (55 volunteers, imagined travel-agency scenario) and the 2008 Duke University fMRI face/name study. `[web check: sample sizes and publication for both]`
13. **Aspirational, in-group imagery paired with sensory reinforcement (a signature scent, a "clubhouse" store design) compounds mirror-neuron and dopamine effects into a repeat-visit loop.** Condition: applies to lifestyle/status brands. Evidence is the book's own narrative account of Abercrombie & Fitch's model displays plus scent marketing — not a controlled study. `[e0305647c836]` `[a12162ab18df]`

### 2.4 Subliminal and logo-free cues

14. **A brand's associated imagery (color, scenery, shapes) can trigger the same "craving" brain response as its explicit logo — sometimes a stronger one — once the association is established.** Condition: applies in regulated or logo-fatigued categories, or wherever repetition has already built the association. Evidence: the cigarette subliminal-imagery fMRI study and the Silk Cut "purple silk" case (Section 7.4). `[d80a660bafff]` `[b5a2ca86be10]`
15. **Explicit branding may work less well than a logo-free but recognizable "brand code" once that code is established — but building the code requires prior repetition to work.** Condition: mature brands with strong, already-learned visual codes (shape, color, pattern). Do not apply to a new or low-awareness brand, which has no code yet to be "free" of. `[web check: this is Lindstrom's own inference from a single tobacco study generalized to "the logo is dead" — treat as his opinion, not a proven universal]`
16. **Historical claims of literal subliminal advertising (words/images flashed too fast to consciously perceive) are mostly debunked (Vicary's 1957 "hoax") or unverifiable, but "primes"/short exposures below full attention (16ms face-emotion flashes, 2-second radio "blinks") have shown measurable effects on stated willingness to pay in at least one cited experiment.** Condition: distinguish outright fabricated subliminal-advertising claims (which the book itself debunks) from genuine sub-attention priming (which it treats as real and effective). `[web check: the smiling/frowning-face 16ms willingness-to-pay study — authors, sample size, publication not given in the book]`

### 2.5 Somatic markers

17. **Purchase decisions run on fast, emotional "bookmarks" built from past reward/punishment associations (Antonio Damasio's "somatic marker" concept), not on deliberate feature comparison.** Condition: any low-involvement, fast, repeat-purchase decision (packaged goods, personal care, snack foods). `[a340e6a9c132]` `[2bdfd34d3777]`
18. **A memorable, even shocking, association (a mascot, a stunt, a strange claim) can create a durable somatic marker cheaply, faster than rational feature messaging.** Condition: differentiating a commodity-like product where features are hard to differentiate. Evidence: Andrex's puppy mascot (UK toilet paper, ~2:1 sales lead over Kleenex on equal spend, per Professor Robert Heath's account) and Blendtec's "Will It Blend?" viral videos. `[80f3f6bcd431]` `[5ef563bcc38e]`
19. **Fear-based somatic markers are among the most powerful and most abused; use them cautiously.** Condition: any messaging that ties non-purchase to a negative outcome (health, safety, social standing). The book names this pattern (Johnson's No More Tears, Colgate gum-disease copy, ADD/Ritalin marketing) and predicts fear-based advertising will increase, while also flagging it as ethically fraught. `[00978aa1938e]` `[18a609b6840b]` `[844b2776ac81]`

### 2.6 Rituals and superstition

20. **Products and brands with an attached ritual are "stickier" and command loyalty beyond functional performance.** Condition: repeat-purchase categories, especially food/drink and personal care. Evidence: Corona-and-lime, Guinness's engineered "slow pour," Oreo dunking, Magners-on-ice, Mallomars' seasonal scarcity. `[9c5d9ee989d0]` `[3b56299b42e4]` `[f8ee55b77565]`
21. **Manufactured scarcity (seasonal withdrawal, invitation-only access) increases anticipation and perceived value.** Condition: applies when a brand can credibly withdraw and reintroduce a product or access tier. Evidence: Mallomars (production halted April-September), Gmail's invite-only launch, American Express Centurion Black Card. `[9ee51a3f9c3d]` `[8723c0ddc0dd]`
22. **Stress and uncertainty increase superstitious and ritualistic behavior, including around brands and money.** Condition: applies more strongly during periods the target audience perceives as high-stakes or uncertain (job security, health, major purchases). Evidence: the Gulf War/Scud-missile superstition study, Friday-the-13th accident-rate claims, Chinese-American heart-attack-rate claims tied to the number four. `[502ec2ab644a]` `[afc9e3363d43]`
23. **A culturally specific ritual or lucky number/color must be adapted per market, not transplanted.** Condition: any localization decision for numbers, colors, or symbols (e.g., 4 vs. 8 in Chinese-influenced markets, 13 in Western markets). `[b43f8160f55f]`
24. **Collecting/completism functions as a control-seeking, stress-response behavior and a genuine retention mechanic.** Condition: applies to categories with expandable ranges, limited editions, or badge/achievement mechanics. Evidence: Hello Kitty's 22,000+ SKU range, eBay-era collectibles-market figures. `[0a83b57e9364]` `[fa843a0610e6]`

### 2.7 Religion-like brand structure

25. **Strong brands can activate the same brain regions as religious/spiritual imagery, more so than sports imagery, in at least one cited study.** Condition: applies mainly to already-strong, high-loyalty brands (the book's examples are Apple, Guinness, Ferrari, Harley-Davidson — "smashable" brands, defined below) — not to weak or new brands, which showed different, less engaged brain activity in the same study. `[992d9678d728]` `[2f826d11d900]`
26. **Ten traits the book claims most major religions share, and which it argues strong brands also exhibit: a sense of belonging, a clear vision, power over enemies (us-vs-them), sensory appeal, storytelling, grandeur, evangelism, symbols, mystery, and rituals.** Condition: use as an audit checklist for an already-strong or aspiring premium/lifestyle brand, not as a literal instruction to imitate religious practice. Evidence base: the author's own interviews with "fourteen prominent leaders from various religions," a self-selected, non-random sample with no stated selection method. `[2f826d11d900]`
27. **Overt (not implicit) use of religious symbolism in a general-market campaign risks backlash, even when well-intentioned.** Condition: campaigns not specifically targeted at a single faith community. Evidence: Lindstrom's own Lego "virtual advent calendar" anecdote, which drew complaints for perceived religious promotion. `[f951cfe57e4e]`
28. **"Smashable" brands (recognizable from a fragment, with no visible logo) tend to be the strongest, most emotionally loaded brands.** Condition: applies to physical product/packaging design decisions for an already-distinctive brand. Test given: "smash" the product (a Guinness pour, a Ferrari red paint chip, a Harley wheel, an iPod's shape) — if a fragment alone signals the brand, it is "smashable"; a plain fabric swatch from an unbranded shirt is not. `[b7da48474acd]`

### 2.8 Sensory branding

29. **Smell is processed via the limbic system with minimal conscious deliberation and can be a stronger purchase driver than sight in some categories.** Condition: any physical retail environment, packaging-open moment, or direct-mail piece. Evidence: the "Pete's" fast-food fragrance/image congruence fMRI study, the lemon-scented-room cleaning-intent study, the Nike-shoe floral-scent study (Section 7.8). `[20fbba1a4005]` `[de8a70a0af11]` `[d96ee4fa4d8b]`
30. **Image and smell (or image and sound) presented congruently are rated more pleasant and are better remembered than either alone; incongruent pairings actively hurt pleasantness (activating an aversion-linked brain region in the cited study).** Condition: any multi-sensory execution (in-store scent + visual merchandising, a video ad's sound design + visuals). Test congruent combinations against incongruent ones before scaling either. `[598a56fddd0e]` `[c197d7564abe]`
31. **Touch and perceived weight signal quality, sometimes independent of actual functional quality.** Condition: physical product/packaging design where "premium" perception matters. Evidence: Lindstrom's own Bang & Olufsen remote-control weight test (100 consumers) and the (unreleased) Duracell bullet-shaped battery concept. `[501ed81d0942]`
32. **Color materially shifts attention, recall, price perception and even physiological arousal, and can outweigh product substance in a blind comparison.** Condition: packaging, PDP hero imagery, and site theming decisions. Evidence: the Tiffany-blue-box heart-rate test (600 women, no logo shown), the Heinz EZ Squirt green-ketchup sales spike, the Seoul International Color Expo brand-recognition figure, phone-directory colored-vs-B&W attention-time figures. `[2e704af4c88b]` `[830ad040b9b1]`
33. **Signature sounds paired with a recognizable image increase both liking and long-term recall versus either alone — but a beloved product can still carry a signature sound that has gone negative through overuse/annoyance, and that negative sound can drag down the visual brand too.** Condition: any brand with (or considering) a sonic identity (notification sound, jingle, unboxing sound). Evidence: the Nokia ring-tone fMRI study is the book's central, most detailed cautionary case here — see Section 7.8. `[3c9b9d4f6d00]` `[2c808611a9bb]` `[e519920226cf]` `[774df5ca9ac9]`

### 2.9 Sex, beauty and controversy in advertising

34. **Overtly sexual creative does not reliably improve brand/product recall and can actively suppress it (the "Vampire Effect"); this held in at least two cited studies (recall study and eye-tracking study).** Condition: any ad or PDP image trading on sexual content as the primary hook. `[15242b826b8b]` `[43f966448348]`
35. **Extreme/supermodel-level beauty in a spokesperson can also suppress product/brand recall, and "wholesome"/relatable imagery tested better with women in at least one cited study.** Condition: casting decisions for lifestyle/apparel/beauty categories, especially when targeting women. `[47ec35b97f3d]`
36. **Controversy (not sex per se) is the more reliable attention/memorability driver, and can succeed commercially even amid public backlash — but can also fail (Dolce & Gabbana pulled ad) or draw legal/regulatory scrutiny (Calvin Klein).** Condition: high-risk creative strategy for brands that can tolerate reputational volatility; not advisable for brands needing to protect trust with a broad or risk-averse customer base. `[8046d8d903e6]` `[382d5f7fae30]`
37. **"Real"/user-generated, non-model imagery increasingly outperforms polished models for authenticity signaling, especially post-2005 as reality TV and UGC content normalized it.** Condition: campaigns targeting authenticity-sensitive audiences. Evidence given is example-based (Dove Real Beauty, Axe UGC contest, Heinz/KFC UGC contests), not a controlled study. `[3fd73a09748d]`

## 3. Procedures

The book is a narrative, not a how-to manual; it gives few explicit, repeatable operator steps. The closest things to procedures are (a) the research methodology the author used, and (b) two or three concrete testing recipes he describes performing for clients. All are listed as the book states them, with no missing steps invented.

### 3.1 The book's own research method (fMRI + SST combined study)

1. Form a hypothesis from consulting experience (e.g., "cigarette warnings encourage rather than deter smoking"; "product placement is largely useless"; "brands function like religions").
2. Design stimuli (images, sounds, fragrances, video clips) to isolate the variable being tested, and choose a benchmark/control condition (a second, unrelated TV show; unbranded logos alongside branded ones; a generic tune alongside a brand's signature tune).
3. Recruit volunteers through commercial recruitment companies, across the study's target countries (the flagship study used five: US, Germany, UK, Japan, China — chosen respectively for advertising-industry centrality, "most advanced country . . . as far as neuromarketing is concerned," the lead researcher's location, launch difficulty, and market size).
4. Submit the protocol to an ethics committee for fMRI work (UK Central Ethics Committee, per the appendix); the commercial SST provider (Neuro-Insight) followed market-research industry codes of practice instead, since it did not require university facilities.
5. Brief volunteers fully, obtain consent, pay a per diem; allow withdrawal at any stage (the book states none withdrew).
6. Run stimuli through fMRI (Dr. Gemma Calvert's team) and/or SST (Professor Richard Silberstein's team, up to 13 readings/second across ~12 brain regions), presenting elements alone and then in combination (image alone, sound/smell alone, then combined) to isolate congruence effects.
7. Cross-check brain-scan results against a written questionnaire from the same subjects, specifically to compare stated preference against measured brain response.
8. Report findings as a directional read for the sponsoring company's future strategy (Christian Dior's J'adore, an unnamed Latin American singer's lyric-language mix via MindCode, Microsoft's UX/EEG testing, Unilever's Eskimo ice-cream taste study), without necessarily publishing the underlying data. `[web check: none of these client cases includes a public replication or published methodology beyond the anecdote in the book]`

### 3.2 Testing image/sensory congruence before combining channels (as performed in the "Pete's" fragrance study)

1. Select the candidate sensory elements to pair (an image and a fragrance, or an image and a sound).
2. Present each element alone to a panel and record pleasantness ratings (the book used a 9-point scale, unpleasant to pleasant).
3. Present the same elements together and re-measure pleasantness.
4. Compare congruent pairings (a fish sandwich image with a lemon scent) against incongruent pairings (a soap image with a fried-food scent) — the book's finding was that congruent combinations scored more pleasant than either element alone, while incongruent combinations scored worse than either element alone.
5. Use the winning, congruent combination in the final creative; discard or fix the incongruent one before wide deployment.
`[20fbba1a4005]` `[598a56fddd0e]`

### 3.3 Diagnosing a "sick, stuck, stumbling" brand (Lindstrom's own consulting framing, not a formal study)

1. Identify the product's actual secret/differentiator, if any.
2. Look for existing stories, rituals or mysteries consumers already associate with it, or that could plausibly be built.
3. Check whether the product could appeal to an underused sense (smell, touch, sound) beyond the two-dimensional constraints of standard advertising.
4. Assess whether current creative is distinctive or "as boring and forgettable as every other company's."
This is presented as the author's own diagnostic habit, not a validated methodology, and the book gives no scoring system or decision thresholds for it. `[23653225623e]`

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

The book is weak on explicit diagnostics; most of what follows is inferred from how the author frames his own consulting checks, not a formal checklist he lays out as such.

- **Check: does your stated customer-research feedback match your revealed behavior data (repeat purchase, order data, abandoned-cart, search terms)?** How: split any research input into stated vs. revealed preference and compare. Good: they broadly agree, or disagreements are explainable by price/availability confounds. Bad: they diverge with no confound explanation — in this case the book's position is to weight revealed behavior more heavily and treat survey language as vocabulary input only, not a behavior predictor. `[b6e70f8909c7]` `[1c02b00e5cfd]`
- **Check: is your brand/placement narratively integrated, or just visually present?** How: ask whether removing the brand name would change the story/content at all (Coke's cups and set design vs. Ford's interchangeable 30-second spots). Good: the brand is load-bearing in the narrative. Bad: it's swappable wallpaper — expect weak or negative recall lift. `[14e9e290ba3e]` `[0fdfce48d0e0]`
- **Check: does your hero creative pass the "smash test"?** How: mentally (or actually) break the product/packaging into a fragment and ask if it is still identifiable without the logo. Good: yes (a paint chip of a distinctive proprietary color, a distinctive silhouette or texture). Bad: no — you are relying entirely on the logo, which the book argues is a comparatively weak brand-recognition anchor once other cues exist. `[b7da48474acd]`
- **Check: are your sensory channels congruent?** How: test image+smell or image+sound pairings against each element alone and against a deliberately mismatched pairing. Good: the congruent pairing scores above either element alone. Bad: a mismatched pairing scores below either element alone — fix or drop it before scaling. `[598a56fddd0e]`
- **Check: has a signature sound/asset gone negative through overexposure?** How: separately measure sentiment/engagement for the visual brand alone vs. the visual+sound/asset combination. Good: combination lifts engagement over visual alone (3 of the book's 4 tested brand pairings). Bad: the sound suppresses engagement below the visual-alone baseline — this is exactly what the book's Nokia case found, and it recommends investigating rather than assuming a beloved brand always benefits from ubiquity. `[e519920226cf]` `[774df5ca9ac9]`
- **Check: is your creative sexual/shock content actually pointing at the product, or competing with it?** How: after exposure, test brand/product recall specifically (not just attention or ad recall). Good: recall holds or improves. Bad: recall drops versus a non-sexual control — this is the book's own definition of the Vampire Effect and its recommended failure signal. `[43f966448348]` `[382d5f7fae30]`

## 6. Tools, settings and named features

- **fMRI (functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging):** measures blood-oxygenation changes tied to neural activity; used in the book's core study for high spatial resolution (down to ~1mm) but with a several-second lag. Run by Dr. Gemma Calvert's team (Neurosense/Warwick).
- **SST (Steady-State Topography):** an advanced EEG-type technique developed by Professor Richard Silberstein, measuring electrical brain activity up to 13 times/second across ~12 discrete brain regions; faster but spatially coarser than fMRI. Run by Neuro-Insight.
- **Button box:** a handheld console volunteers used to rate stimuli (typically a 1-9 pleasantness scale) in real time during scanning.
- **"Sensory Branding™"** — a term/trademark the author uses for his own agency's (BRAND sense) practice of designing multi-sensory (not just visual) brand cues.
- **MindCode** — an outside consulting firm (unrelated to the author's own companies, per the text) specializing in "indirect signals" brands send, cited in one client anecdote (the unnamed Latin American singer's lyric-language case).
- **Neuroco** — a brain-scanning company cited for a 20th Century Fox in-game-advertising attention study (billboards in a virtual Paris).
- **BUYOLOGY INC.** — the author's own neuromarketing company, founded/growing alongside this book's publication; a direct commercial beneficiary of the book's thesis. Treat any claim sourced only to "my company's work" as vendor-interested. `[web check]`

## 7. Examples and case results

All items below are the book's own reported studies and brand cases, organized by chapter. Every sample size, method and result is stated exactly as the book gives it; none of this has been independently re-verified for this file, and several methods (especially "vendor's own claim" or single-anecdote items) are explicitly weaker evidence than the controlled studies. `[web check]` applies globally to this section.

### 7.1 Warnings, cravings and the core research program (Ch. 1)

- **Cigarette warning-label fMRI study.** Method: fMRI on smokers (32 in the London leg described in detail; part of a 2,081-subject, 5-country study: US, UK, Germany, Japan, China). Cigarette warning labels were projected while subjects rated craving via button box. Result: warning labels produced **zero** suppression of craving and instead activated the nucleus accumbens ("craving spot"); the effect held across the smoker sample. Brand named: Marlboro and Camel smokers were recruited by brand loyalty. `[f3b801517b67]`
- **Pepsi/Coke blind taste test, historical and 2003 fMRI replay.** 1975 original "Pepsi Challenge": blind taste test in malls/supermarkets; >50% preferred Pepsi's taste, yet Coke retained market dominance. 2003 replication by Dr. Read Montague (Baylor College of Medicine), fMRI, 67 subjects: blind test matched original (>50% preferred Pepsi; ventral putamen activated). When brand was revealed before tasting, **75%** then preferred Coke, and medial prefrontal cortex (higher-thinking region) activated alongside the taste region — interpreted as brand/emotion overriding taste preference. `[web check: full study citation]`
- **Princeton delayed-gratification study.** Four Princeton psychologists, fMRI, subjects choosing between a $15 Amazon gift voucher now or a $20 voucher in two weeks. Both options activated the lateral prefrontal cortex; the immediate $15 option additionally activated limbic (emotional) regions more strongly, and greater limbic activation predicted choosing the smaller, immediate reward over the objectively better deal. `[3b9792ac8011]`
- **FKF Applied Research political ad study (Tom Freedman, 2003).** fMRI study during the Bush-Kerry campaign; subjects viewed campaign ads, candidate photos, 9/11 imagery, and LBJ's 1964 "Daisy" ad. Result: 9/11 and "Daisy" imagery activated the amygdala broadly; Democrats' amygdalas activated more strongly than Republicans' on the 9/11 imagery, per lead researcher Marco Iacobini's interpretation. No sample size given.
- **Daimler-Chrysler car-image fMRI studies (Ulm, Germany, 2002).** Study 1: subjects viewing a Mini Cooper image showed activation in a face-recognition brain region (interpreted as the car being perceived as "a face"). Study 2: 12 men shown images of 66 different cars; sports cars activated a "reward and reinforcement" region, interpreted by psychiatrist Henrik Walter via a peacock/mate-display analogy.
- **Oxford infant-face study (Morten L. Kringelbach).** Magnetoencephalography, 12 adults, shown infant and adult faces during a computer task. Result: a distinct, fast (~1/7 second) response to infant faces specifically, in the medial orbitofrontal cortex.

### 7.2 Product placement (Ch. 2)

- **American Idol SST/logo-recall study.** ~400 subjects wore SST caps; shown 20 branded logos (Coke, Ford, Cingular — Idol's paid sponsors) and 20 unbranded logos (e.g., Fanta, Verizon, Target, eBay) before and after watching a 20-minute Idol segment plus a benchmark unrelated show. Baseline: no recall difference between the two logo sets. Post-viewing: branded-logo recall rose significantly and **suppressed** recall of unbranded logos. Among the three sponsors, Coke (~60% of show running time via props/set design/judges' cups) showed the strongest recall gain; Cingular (voting mechanic, on-screen branding) a smaller gain; Ford (only 30-second spots, no narrative role) showed recall **below its own pre-study baseline** despite a reported $26M/year spend. `[14e9e290ba3e]` `[0fdfce48d0e0]` `[3bdf718b5a61]`
- **Historical product-placement cases (author's reporting, not controlled studies):** Reese's Pieces in E.T. (1982) — Hershey's deal after Mars/M&Ms declined — sales tripled within a week, 800+ cinemas added the product within two months. Ray-Ban in Risky Business (1983) — sales +50%; in Top Gun (1986) — sales +40%, Navy recruitment +500% (per the book); in Men in Black II (2002) — sales tripled, ~$25M "free ad" value per a company representative. Die Another Day (2002) — 23 brands in 123 minutes; Driven (2001) — 103 brands in 117 minutes; Transformers (2007) — 68 companies. Casino Royale (2006) — Ford supplied every vehicle, Sony placed multiple product lines; author states he personally recalls only the Aston Martin, attributing that to prior Bond-brand association, not the film itself.
- **Lindstrom's own car-commercial montage experiment.** 60 TV car commercials from 20 automakers, each using a nearly identical desert/dust-cloud driving scene; when spliced together, the author could not identify which brand was which. No formal subject panel — this was the author's own viewing.

### 7.3 Mirror neurons (Ch. 3)

- **Rizzolatti's macaque mirror-neuron discovery (Parma, Italy, 1992).** Method: single-neuron recording in macaque premotor cortex (region F5) during grasping tasks. Finding: neurons fired both when the monkey grasped an object and when it merely observed a human grasping an object (e.g., a grad student eating an ice cream cone) — but only for "targeted gestures" involving an object, not random movement.
- **Human mirror-neuron evidence.** fMRI/EEG studies (unnamed in detail) of inferior frontal cortex and superior parietal lobule show activation both when performing and observing an action.
- **Tania Singer pain/empathy and schadenfreude studies.** Subjects' brains scanned while watching others experience pain: fronto-insular and anterior cingulate cortices activated (empathy). Follow-up: subjects watched cheaters and non-cheaters receive mild electric shocks; when cheaters were shocked, male subjects' pain-empathy regions activated less and reward regions activated more; female subjects retained empathy for cheaters. No sample size given.
- **UCLA reading-comprehension fMRI study.** Subjects read action phrases ("biting the peach," "grasping a pen"), then later watched videos of the same actions; the same cortical regions activated for reading and for watching. No sample size given.
- **Duke University smiling-face/name-recall fMRI study (2008).** Professors Takashi Tsukiura and Roberto Cabeza; subjects shown smiling vs. non-smiling faces paired with names. Result: orbitofrontal cortex (reward-processing region) more active when learning/recalling names paired with smiling faces. No sample size given.
- **"Smiling Study."** 55 volunteers imagined a travel-agency interaction with a smiling, despondent, or fed-up agent. Result: the smiling-agent group reported more positive experience and greater likelihood of repeat patronage.
- **Nick Baily unboxing anecdote.** A Detroit teenager's Wii unboxing video (Nov. 2006) reportedly viewed ~71,000 times in its first week on YouTube — offered as an anecdotal illustration of vicarious mirror-neuron pleasure, not a study.

### 7.4 Subliminal messaging (Ch. 4)

- **James Vicary's 1957 "Drink Coca-Cola"/"Eat Popcorn" hoax.** Claimed 1/3000-second flashes during screenings of Picnic in Fort Lee, NJ (45,699 moviegoers over 6 weeks); claimed 18.1% Coke sales increase and 57.8% popcorn increase. Vicary later admitted (1962, after Dr. Henry Link's challenge) that the entire experiment was fabricated — the book treats this explicitly as a debunked hoax, not evidence subliminal advertising works.
- **1999 Harvard subliminal aging-stereotype study.** 47 subjects aged 60-85; subliminal word flashes (positive: "wise," "astute," "accomplished"; negative: "senile," "dependent," "diseased") during a computer game; walking speed/gait measured afterward. Result (per lead researcher Jeffrey Hausdorff): gait of the positive-word group improved by "almost 10 percent."
- **16-millisecond smiling/frowning face and willingness-to-pay study.** Two unnamed researchers; brief (16ms) face-emotion exposure, too fast for conscious identification. Result: subjects exposed to smiling faces poured more of a beverage and were willing to pay roughly twice as much for it than those exposed to frowning faces. No sample size given.
- **Marlboro/Camel subliminal-imagery fMRI study.** Extension of the Ch.1 cigarette study: logo-free images (Western scenery, cowboys, red Ferraris, F1/NASCAR imagery) shown to smokers alongside explicit logo/pack images. Result: logo-free images triggered nucleus-accumbens (craving) activity at least as strong as, and in some comparisons **stronger than**, explicit branded imagery; the only consistent difference was more visual-cortex activity for the more complex logo-free images. Held for both Marlboro and Camel smokers.
- **Silk Cut "purple silk" case (UK, 1997).** Ahead of a UK tobacco-ad ban, Silk Cut paired its logo with a purple-silk background in every ad; after the ban, it ran logo-free purple-silk billboards. A cited research study found 98% of consumers correctly identified the billboards as Silk Cut despite no text or logo.
- **Anti-smoking "fat globule" ad backfire.** A US ad showing fat oozing from cigarettes (meant to depict arterial damage) instead activated craving regions in test subjects, whose mirror neurons reportedly responded to the convivial social scene rather than the health-warning content — no subject count given for this specific sub-test.
- **Sean Polyn memory-retrieval fMRI study (2005, University of Pennsylvania).** ~90 images across 3 categories (famous faces, famous places, everyday objects); subjects' recall-phase brain activity matched their encoding-phase pattern, and subjects could identify an image's category before recalling the specific image/name.

### 7.5 Rituals and superstition (Ch. 5)

- **Corona-and-lime ritual.** Anecdotal origin story: a 1981 bartender bet, not a cultural or hygienic tradition (the book explicitly rules out the other two common explanations). Credited (unverified) with helping Corona overtake Heineken in the US market.
- **Guinness "slow pour" ritual.** Manufactured deliberately in the early 1990s in response to customer impatience with the ~10-minute pour; supported by ad lines "Good things come to those who wait" and "It takes 119.53 seconds to pour the perfect pint," per brewmeister Fergal Murray.
- **BBDO Worldwide 2007 ritual study.** 26 countries surveyed; identified four recurring daily ritual categories: "preparing for battle" (morning routine), "feasting" (eating together), "sexing up" (grooming), "protecting yourself from the future" (nighttime routine). No per-country breakdown or sample size given.
- **Bruce Hood's "Fred West sweater" demonstration (British Association Festival of Science, Norwich).** Live demo, not a controlled study: offered £10 to try on a blue sweater; most hands went up, then dropped when told (falsely) it belonged to serial killer Fred West.
- **Giora Keinan / Tel Aviv University superstition study.** Questionnaires sent to 174 Israelis after the 1991 Iraqi Scud missile attacks; higher self-reported stress correlated with greater endorsement of magical/superstitious beliefs.
- **Kahneman and Tversky "hot hand" study (1985).** Analysis of Boston Celtics free-throw statistics, 1980-1982. Result: making the first free throw did not change the probability of making the second (~75% either way) — the popular "hot hand" belief was not supported by the data. Named Nobel laureates; this is the book's most methodologically explicit citation.
- **Product-ritual cases (all anecdotal/example-based, not controlled studies):** Oreo dunking (partnered with "Got Milk?"; quote from Oreo's Mike Faherty); Magners cider served over ice (originated from lack of refrigeration in Tipperary pubs, became "Magners on Ice" branding, cited as driving a UK popularity surge without any reformulation); Mallomars' seasonal production halt (April-September) framed as manufactured scarcity; Subway's fixed sandwich-build order; Bacardi-and-Coke ("Cuba Libre," dated to 1898 Spanish-American War) as a ritual the brand is now "trapped" by.
- **Collecting/hoarding figures.** A 1981 New York Times article estimated ~30% of Americans hoard; 1995 (the year eBay launched) collectibles-industry sales reached $8.2B; at time of writing, eBay had ~49M registered users. Hello Kitty: 22,000+ licensed products in circulation across Asia and globally.

### 7.6 Religion and brands (Ch. 6)

- **Carmelite "nun study" (2006).** 15 cloistered Carmelite nuns, age 23-64; fMRI; Dr. Mario Beauregard and Dr. Vincent Paquette, University of Montreal. Nuns asked to relive their most profound religious experience: caudate nucleus (joy/serenity/self-awareness/love) and insula (feelings tied to a sense of the divine) activated. A different activity pattern appeared when nuns recalled a profound experience with another human being. Conclusion (researchers' own): no single "God Spot" exists, but religious and interpersonal experience produce distinguishable brain-activity patterns in this group.
- **Author's own religion-traits interview.** 14 religious leaders across Catholicism, Protestantism, Buddhism and Islam interviewed (no stated sampling method); author extracted 10 common traits: belonging, clear vision, power over enemies, sensory appeal, storytelling, grandeur, evangelism, symbols, mystery, rituals.
- **Brand-vs-religion fMRI study.** 65 male subjects (self-rated spirituality 7-10/10), fMRI; shown alternating images of strong brands (Coca-Cola, iPod, Ferrari, Guinness/Harley-Davidson-type icons), weak brands (Microsoft, BP), religious icons (the Pope, rosary beads, Mother Teresa, a church pew), and sports imagery (David Beckham, the World Cup). Result: strong brands produced brain-activity patterns "not just similar" but reported as **almost identical** to religious-icon patterns; weak brands activated different regions; sports imagery activated a reward region (middle inferior orbitofrontal cortex) similarly to religious icons but did not match strong/weak brand imagery's stronger activation of memory and decision-making regions.
- **Named brand cases (anecdotal):** Steve Jobs' 2004 Apple Newton-discontinuation demo (audience members reportedly wept/stomped their own Newtons); "Smash Your Brand" concept traced to a 1915 Coca-Cola bottle-redesign brief; Coca-Cola's secret-formula-theft attempt (June 2005, an undercover sting caught a man attempting to sell Coke trade secrets to a Pepsi mole for $1.5M); Unilever's fictitious "X9 Factor" shampoo label in Asia (an employee's unauthorized addition went undetected for a production run; when removed on reprint, customers complained the product no longer worked); Lego's "virtual advent calendar" backlash (overt Christian-holiday framing drew complaint volume the author personally handled); Holy Land Earth (Brooklyn company importing Israeli soil for religious use, founder Steven Friedman) and "Official Irish Dirt" (Alan Jenkins, ~9 years to secure import approval, $3M+ sold in 12oz bags, cited individual purchases up to $148,000).

### 7.7 Somatic markers (Ch. 7)

- **Gruppe Nymphenberg point-of-sale study.** Cited finding: >50% of purchase decisions made spontaneously/unconsciously at point of sale. No method, sample size, or publication given in the book. `[web check]`
- **Andrex vs. Kleenex case (Professor Robert Heath's account).** UK toilet-paper market; Andrex outsells Kleenex ~2:1 despite equal ad spend, price and quality, attributed to Andrex's puppy mascot creating richer "conceptual links" (family, puppies, toilet training) than Kleenex's advertising.
- **Blendtec "Will It Blend?" viral video series (Tom Dickson).** Anecdotal case: videos of a blender destroying objects (lighters, hockey pucks, an iPhone) framed as creating a durable, shock-based somatic marker linking the brand to power.
- **Lindstrom's own GaJol lozenge case (Denmark, ~1990s).** Ad agency work: when Pavarotti canceled a Danish concert due to a sore throat, the author's team placed same-day ads for GaJol lozenges ("If only Pavarotti had known about GaJol"); the book claims the association persisted "even fifteen years later," with no measurement given.
- **Lindstrom's own "pink bank" anecdote (unnamed Eastern European bank).** Advice given at a dinner (author states he had "just polished off a large meal and a number of glasses of wine" when giving it): repaint the entire bank pink. Reported result, communicated by the client via email: initial customer dislike, then after ~90 days, "highest brand awareness of any bank in the country" and marketing costs "cut in half" — no independent figures, bank name, or country given.
- **Lamisil "gremlin" ad** — cited as a humor-based somatic marker example (anthropomorphized germs), no data given.
- **Fear-based somatic-marker examples (no data given):** Johnson's No More Tears Baby Shampoo (fear of stinging eyes); a Colgate ad citing gum-disease/heart-disease/diabetes/stroke associations; ADD/Ritalin-type marketing, which the author frames critically as fear-driven and culturally amplified rather than purely medically driven — his own opinion, not a study.

### 7.8 Senses: smell, touch, sight, sound (Ch. 8)

- **Neuroco/20th Century Fox in-game ad-attention study.** Virtual walk through Paris in a video game; EEG-type brain activity plus eye-tracking measured response to billboard/bus-stop/bus-side ads. Result: no ad noticeably captured attention; heavy visual saturation produced "glazed eyes," not higher engagement. No numeric results given.
- **"Pete's" fragrance/image congruence fMRI study.** 20 subjects; images and fragrances of J&J No More Tears Baby Shampoo, Dove soap, iced Coca-Cola, and an anonymized fast-food chain ("Pete's") shown/smelled separately, then together, rated on a 9-point pleasantness scale. Result: elements alone were rated similarly pleasant whether seen or smelled; congruent image+fragrance pairs (e.g., a fish sandwich + a lemon scent) rated **more** pleasant than either alone; incongruent pairs (e.g., a soap image + a fried-food scent) rated **less** pleasant than either alone. Brain correlates given: right medial orbitofrontal cortex (pleasant/likable perception) active for congruent pairs; left lateral orbitofrontal cortex (aversion) active for incongruent pairs; right piriform cortex (olfactory) and amygdala (emotional relevance) both active for congruent, better-remembered pairs.
- **Vanilla-scent apparel study (Pacific Northwest clothing store).** "Feminine scents" (vanilla) sprayed in the women's section reportedly **doubled** apparel sales; no sample size, duration, or independent verification given.
- **2005 lemon-scent/cleaning-intent study.** Two researchers; a barely perceptible lemon-cleaner scent piped into a room. Result: 36% of subjects in the scented room listed a cleaning-related activity as their plan for the day, vs. 11% in the unscented room. Follow-up: 22 college students given a messy cookie after sitting in a scented or unscented room; hidden-camera observation found scented-room subjects made less mess; subjects were unaware of the scent's influence when asked afterward.
- **Dr. Alan Hirsch Nike-shoe scent study.** Two identical rooms, identical Nike running shoes, one room lightly floral-scented. Result: 84% of subjects preferred the shoes viewed in the scented room, and valued them at roughly $10 more than the unscented-room shoes.
- **German home-improvement-store grass-scent study.** Freshly cut grass scent piped into the store; 49% of surveyed customers (before/after) rated staff as more knowledgeable about products after the scent was introduced.
- **Americhip multi-sensory Diet Pepsi print ad (in People magazine).** A magazine ad combining sound, taste and pop-up elements; reported 100% reader awareness — "for the first time in the magazine's history," per the book. No comparison-ad baseline given.
- **San Francisco "Got Milk?" scented bus-shelter campaign (2006).** Cookie-scent-infused ad strips pulled after 36 hours following complaints that the smell triggered allergic reactions — an example of scent marketing backfiring.
- **Bang & Olufsen remote-control weight test (Lindstrom's own).** 100 consumers given two otherwise-identical remote controls, one internally weighted with aluminum, one without. Result: consumers rated the lighter remote as "broken" and, even after learning it functioned correctly, still judged it lower quality.
- **Duracell bullet-shaped-battery concept (never released).** Men who loaded flashlights with heavier, bullet-shaped battery prototypes uniformly judged them "more powerful" than standard batteries despite the design actually reducing power output. No sample size given.
- **Saudi Arabian egg-branding case (Lindstrom's own consulting job).** Visited one of the world's largest egg farms near Jeddah; advised on shifting from white to brown eggshells (consumer preference had shifted, cause unstated) and on using a vitamin-based feed additive (not artificial coloring, for stated ethical reasons) to control yolk color/yellowness, on the premise that a more yellow yolk signals freshness/quality to consumers.
- **Tiffany-blue-box heart-rate test (Lindstrom's own).** 600 women given an empty blue Tiffany's box (no logo visible); heart rate and blood pressure measured. Result: heart rate rose ~20%, attributed to color association alone (engagement/marriage/fertility).
- **Heinz EZ Squirt "Blastin' Green" ketchup (2001).** Color-only reformulation; >10 million bottles sold in the first seven months, described as the brand's highest sales spike in its history.
- **Apple iMac color launch ("It doesn't have to be beige").** Preorders reportedly surged ahead of launch; Steve Jobs quoted as wanting people to "lick them," reflecting candy-inspired coloring. No sales figures given.
- **Phone-directory colored-vs-B&W ad study.** Colored ads held attention 2+ seconds vs. <1 second for black-and-white; a separate claim states the average product/ad has "one-twentieth of a second" to capture attention. No study name/sample given for either figure.
- **Seoul International Color Expo study.** Color found to increase brand recognition by up to 80%; 84.7% of respondents said color made up more than half their brand-choice criteria.
- **M&M's "vote a new color" campaign (Lindstrom's own BBDO work, Europe).** Web-based public vote to choose a new M&M's color (blue won); sales reportedly rose after the launch, no figure given.
- **French diet-mayonnaise bottle-shape study.** Two visually different bottle shapes (same product/label) shown to an all-female, diet-conscious panel; every subject preferred the narrower-middle shape over the "genie bottle" taper, without tasting either — interpreted as an unconscious body-shape association. No sample size given.
- **Sound-branding cases (mostly anecdotal, no data):** GE's 3-chime sound (1950s-era origin); Kellogg's Danish-lab-engineered cornflake "crunch"; Bahlsen's 16-person team engineering biscuit/chip crunch sounds; Ford Taurus's engineered door-latch "vault" sound; iPod's click-wheel and chime sounds; McDonald's fry-timer beep and straw-piercing-cup sound as brand sonic cues; classic jingles (Dr Pepper, Alka-Seltzer, Meow Mix) cited as memory anchors with no new data.
- **Nokia ring-tone fMRI/SST study.** Four product categories tested (phones, software, airlines, London imagery), each with an associated signature sound (Nokia's default ring tone, based on Francisco Tárrega's 19th-century "Gran Vals"; British Airways' "Flower Duet" from Delibes' Lakmé; Microsoft's startup/shutdown chime; William Blake's "Jerusalem" hymn for London), against generic benchmark sounds (random ring tones, a Bach excerpt). Ten images per brand tested alone, sound alone, and combined, across five repeated runs, rated 1-9. Result: 3 of 4 brand pairings (British Airways, Microsoft [slightly], London/Jerusalem) showed increased liking and long-term memory encoding when sound and image were combined vs. either alone. Nokia was the exception: its ring tone produced an across-the-board **negative** emotional response that **suppressed** the otherwise-positive brain response to Nokia phone imagery, and post-sound subject ratings favored unrelated benchmark images over Nokia's own phone images. The negative response localized (per the book) to the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, interpreted as the ring tone having become a negative somatic marker through overexposure/annoyance rather than the phones themselves being disliked.

### 7.9 Predicting success/failure (Ch. 9)

- **Quizmania SST predictive study (FremantleMedia, Dec. 2006).** 200 volunteers (four groups of 50), SST caps; watched a pilot of the UK game show Quizmania plus two benchmark shows: "The Swan" (a known ratings failure) and "How Clean Is Your House?" (a known ratings hit). Volunteers watched DVDs 24 hours beforehand to reduce novelty effects, then completed a written questionnaire and underwent SST scanning. Result: questionnaire ratings did **not** distinguish The Swan from How Clean Is Your House? (both rated similarly, despite very different real-world performance) and rated Quizmania as the least-likely-to-watch of the three. ### 7.10 Sex, beauty and controversy in advertising (Ch. 10)

- **Historical sexual-ad cases (anecdotal):** 1966 Ford Mustang "Six and the Single Girl" ad; 1971 National Airlines "I'm Cheryl, Fly Me" campaign, reportedly followed by a 23% passenger increase; 1977 Noxzema shaving-cream ad.
- **2007 UCL recall study (Ellie Parker and Adrian Furnham).** 60 young adults split into 4 groups; 2 groups watched a Sex and the City episode, 2 watched Malcolm in the Middle; within each pairing, one subgroup saw sexually suggestive product ads (shampoo, beer, perfume), the other saw non-sexual versions of similar ads. Result: sexual-ad viewers were **no better** at recalling brands/products than non-sexual-ad viewers; the Sex and the City group had **worse** ad recall than the Malcolm in the Middle group. Researchers' quoted conclusion: "sex does not sell anything other than itself."
- **MediaAnalyzer Software & Research eye-tracking study.** 400 subjects; print ads ranging from racy cigarette ads to bland credit-card ads; mouse-cursor gaze-tracking used as an attention proxy. Men's/women's cursors lingered on sexualized imagery and bypassed brand name/logo/text. Recall results: men 9.8% (sexual ads) vs. ~20% (non-sexual); women 10.85% (sexual) vs. 22.3% (non-sexual). Researchers coined the term "Vampire Effect" for this attention-drain pattern.
- **Calvin Klein history (anecdotal, extensively detailed).** 1980: 15-year-old Brooke Shields' "Nothing comes between me and my Calvins" campaign — jeans sales rose to ~2M pairs/month; by 1984 the CK brand was valued at ~$1B/year and controlled ~70% of the jeans market at retailers like Bloomingdale's (Klein quoted: "Did we sell more jeans? Yes, of course! It was great."). 1995: porn-styled ad campaign drew a US Department of Justice child-pornography investigation; Klein was never charged. 1999: an ad campaign featuring young boys in underwear on a couch was pulled by Klein himself after public outrage, before a planned Times Square billboard debuted. 2002: Klein sold his business to Phillips Van Heusen amid competitive pressure (e.g., from Gap).
- **Other controversy-based cases (anecdotal):** Abercrombie & Fitch's 2003 racy holiday catalog (boycotted by the National Coalition for the Protection of Children and Families, negative 60 Minutes coverage); a Dolce & Gabbana print ad resembling a gang-rape scene, pulled after protests in Spain, Italy and the US, described as having "suffered" the brand (no further detail); American Apparel, controversial since 2005 for its advertising, reported at 151 stores in 11 countries and ~$300M sales in 2006.
- **University of Florida beauty-perception study.** ~250 young women; viewed fashion-magazine photos (including celebrities like Uma Thurman and Lindsay Lohan); asked to sort images into 6 beauty categories, which collapsed into 2 broad categories ("sexy" and "wholesome"). Result: more provocative/sexual images correlated with more bored/disinterested reactions; more wholesome/natural images correlated with more positive reactions, among these female subjects specifically.
- **2001 Market Facts survey.** Cited finding: people were nearly twice as likely to say they'd buy a product shown with "love" imagery (53%) than with sexual imagery (26%).
- **"Real people" advertising cases (anecdotal):** Axe's "World's Dirtiest Film" UGC contest; Heinz's "Top This! TV Challenge" UGC ketchup-ad contest; a KFC ad built from fan-submitted homemade videos; Dove's "Real Beauty" campaign; French brand Comptoir des Cotonniers using real mother-daughter pairs as models; Mikhail Gorbachev appearing in a Louis Vuitton ad and a Russian Pizza Hut ad.
- **Nolita anti-anorexia campaign backfire (Milan, ~2007).** Billboard featuring Isabelle Caro, a 27-year-old French model weighing 68 pounds, tagline "No. Anorexia" (photographer Oliviero Toscani). Per a quote from the (unnamed) president of Italy's Association for the Study of Anorexia, the campaign reportedly triggered aspirational/envious reactions in some anorexic viewers rather than deterrence — the book frames this as a mirror-neuron-driven backfire, structurally parallel to the Ch.1 cigarette-warning finding.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Trusting stated preference (surveys, focus groups, "what people say they'll do") over revealed behavior when the two conflict — the book's recurring theme, illustrated by the cigarette-warning study, the Pepsi/Coke case, and the Quizmania study. `[b6e70f8909c7]` `[47ec35b97f3d]`
- Assuming a graphic warning, disclaimer, or scare tactic will deter the behavior it targets — the cigarette-warning and Nolita anti-anorexia cases both show the opposite effect (activating craving/aspiration instead of deterrence). `[f3b801517b67]` `[a882275a519a]`
- Buying broad media presence (a sponsorship, a placement, high ad spend) without narrative/emotional integration — Ford's $26M American Idol sponsorship is the book's central cautionary case; recall dropped below the pre-campaign baseline. `[0fdfce48d0e0]`
- Over-relying on the logo as the primary brand-recognition asset, in categories where regulation, clutter, or overexposure has weakened its power — illustrated by the tobacco/Silk Cut findings, though the author generalizes this into a stronger universal claim than the underlying evidence supports. `[d80a660bafff]`
- Deploying sexual or shock content as the primary creative hook without checking brand/product recall specifically — both cited studies (UCL, MediaAnalyzer) found recall dropped, not rose, versus non-sexual controls. `[15242b826b8b]` `[43f966448348]`
- Casting extreme beauty/celebrity when the audience needs to see themselves in the ad — the University of Florida study and the Gap's Kravitz/Stone campaign are both cited as backfires or failures. `[47ec35b97f3d]`
- Adding a signature sound (or any new sensory asset) to an established visual brand without testing whether it reinforces or undermines the visual brand — the Nokia ring-tone case is the book's clearest warning here, and the author frames it as a hard, uncomfortable finding for the client to hear. `[e519920226cf]` `[774df5ca9ac9]`
- Pairing incongruent sensory cues (a mismatched scent and image, a mismatched sound and image) — the "Pete's" study found incongruent pairings scored **worse** than either element alone, not merely neutral. `[598a56fddd0e]`
- Transplanting a ritual, number, or color association across cultures without adaptation (the number 4 vs. 8 in Chinese-influenced markets; the number 13 in Western markets). `[b43f8160f55f]`
- Using overt, single-faith religious symbolism in general-market campaigns — Lego's advent-calendar backlash is the book's example. `[f951cfe57e4e]`
- Trusting a company's own market research to greenlight a launch without any behavioral/predictive check — New Coke, Coca-Cola BlaK, the Segway, the Sinclair C5, RJR's Premier, and Ford's survey-built "ideal American car" are all cited as research-approved products that failed anyway. `[cce38d2b0f78]`

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Source-level caveats (about the book itself):**

- The book's central research program has no cited peer-reviewed publication; every number and brain-region claim in this file comes from the author's own account of research he personally commissioned and that his own company later monetized (BUYOLOGY INC.). This is a direct conflict of interest the book does not flag.
- Sample sizes for the flagship studies are small by modern standards for claims this broad (e.g., 20 subjects for the multi-sensory fragrance study; 12 men for a car-preference study; 10 subjects for the Super Bowl-ad study; 65 men for the brand-vs-religion study). The book presents these as decisive ("99 percent scientific certainty," "almost identical" brain patterns) without describing statistical methods, effect sizes, or confidence intervals.
- Many secondary claims are sourced only to news articles, blogs, or unnamed "researchers"/"a recent study," with no author names, journal, or year given in-text (endnotes point to URLs, several of which are now dead links or non-academic sites). Treat every unattributed number in Sections 4 and 7 as unverified until independently checked.
- The book's specific predictions have already failed to materialize as stated (e.g., its 2008 forecast that neuroscience would dominate election prediction "by 2012"), which is a direct, checkable failure of the author's own forecasting track record and a reason for extra caution on his other predictions (e.g., "sex in advertising will go underground," "branding . . . is just beginning"). `[web check]`
- The book never distinguishes ecommerce, online retail, or digital PDPs from physical retail, broadcast media, or in-person branding — every example is pre-ecommerce-native (TV, print, physical stores, cigarette packs, movie placements). Anything mapped to ecommerce below is this playbook's own extrapolation, clearly marked as such, not the book's claim.
- The author frequently generalizes from a single study or a single client anecdote to a sweeping universal claim ("the logo is dead," "90 percent of buying is unconscious," "branding as we know it is just beginning"). Where this file states such a claim as a "principle," it also states the narrow evidence it rests on — do not let the confident tone of the source imply broader proof than the underlying study shows.
- The Seki saba chronology (Section 7.11) contains an internal date inconsistency in the book's own text; flagged there.

**Where the source hedges or contradicts itself:**

- The author states plainly that "neuromarketing isn't the answer to everything" and that it is "limited by our still-incomplete understanding of the human brain," and invites his own findings to be questioned — but the book's marketing and rhetorical style (chapter titles, "99 percent certainty," "chillingly well") often overstates the confidence the hedges call for.
- The book argues both that logos are comparatively weak (Ch. 4's tobacco findings) and that certain logos/symbols (Apple's icon set, Nike's swoosh, McDonald's arches) remain extremely powerful (Ch. 6) — it does not fully reconcile these, beyond implying that established, simple, story-rich symbols retain power while generic corporate logos in cluttered/regulated categories do not.

### Ecommerce mapping (this playbook's own adaptation — not the book's claim)

The following maps each major mechanism to PDP, packaging, ads, or unboxing decisions an ecommerce brand-building agent might face. None of this is validated by the book's own research (which never tested an online store); treat it as a hypothesis set to test with a brand's own data, not a rule to apply blindly.

- **Self-report vs. revealed behavior (2.1):** Weight PDP/packaging/creative decisions toward add-to-cart, repeat-purchase, and search-term data over survey/focus-group sentiment when the two disagree. `[needs brand data: order history, repeat-purchase rate, on-site search terms, abandoned-cart patterns]`
- **Narrative integration vs. passive placement (2.2):** An influencer/UGC placement or a brand's own PDP hero content likely needs the product to be functionally load-bearing in the scene (used to solve a visible problem) rather than merely visible, to get a placement-style recall lift. `[needs brand data: creative-level recall or engagement tracking by placement type]`
- **Mirror neurons (2.3):** Product demo videos, unboxing content, and "customer using it" imagery on PDPs may work by imitation rather than information; prioritize first-person or POV demo content over static feature shots, and treat UGC unboxing videos as a plausible high-leverage asset type. `[needs brand data: PDP video vs. static-image conversion comparison]`
- **Somatic markers (2.5):** A distinctive, even odd, brand mascot, packaging quirk, or signature unboxing moment may build faster recognition than a features list, especially for a commodity-like product; but building it takes repetition — do not expect a one-off stunt to create a lasting marker. `[needs brand data: aided/unaided brand recall pre/post a signature asset launch]`
- **Rituals (2.6):** A designed "how to use it" ritual (a specific first-use sequence, an unboxing sequence, a replenishment cue) can be built deliberately into packaging and post-purchase email/SMS flows, following the Guinness/Oreo pattern of engineering rather than discovering a ritual. `[needs brand data: repeat-purchase rate for customers exposed to a ritual-style onboarding vs. not]`
- **Religion-like brand traits (2.7):** The 10-trait checklist (belonging, vision, us-vs-them, sensory appeal, storytelling, grandeur, evangelism, symbols, mystery, rituals) can be used as an audit of brand pages, About pages, and community/loyalty program design for an established or aspiring premium brand — it is not a checklist a new, low-trust brand should force before it has earned any of these traits organically.
- **Sensory branding (2.8):** For ecommerce, "smell" and "touch" mostly transfer to the unboxing moment (packaging materials, scent inserts, tactile packaging choices) rather than in-store scent marketing; "sound" transfers to app/notification sounds and video-ad sound design; "sight/color" transfers directly to PDP imagery, packaging color, and site theming, and is the most directly testable via standard A/B methods already used elsewhere in this codebase (see 09-CRO). Test congruent sensory pairings (packaging color/texture matching the product's positioning) against mismatched ones before scaling. `[needs brand data: unboxing survey or return-rate data if packaging sensory design changes]`
- **Sex/beauty/controversy in ads (2.9):** If a brand is considering sexual, extreme-beauty, or provocative creative, test product/brand recall specifically (not just click-through or view time) against a non-sexual control, since the book's own studies found recall drop rather than rise; a controversy-driven approach carries real reputational risk and should be reserved for brands that can tolerate volatility. `[needs brand data: brand-name recall survey by creative variant, if this route is taken]`
- **Product placement suppression effect (2.2, rule 7):** If running a co-branded promotion, giveaway, or shared media placement with another brand's logo, be aware the stronger brand's presence may suppress recall of the weaker one — plan hero placement/exclusivity accordingly rather than assuming shared exposure benefits both equally.
