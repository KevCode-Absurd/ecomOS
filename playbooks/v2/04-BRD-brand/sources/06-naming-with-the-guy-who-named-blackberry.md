## 1. What this source is for

Gives an operator a repeatable process for naming a brand, product line or SKU: how to audit the category, mine for candidate names in volume, judge which candidates are "right" versus merely "good," test them before showing decision-makers, and decide whether and how to rename an existing brand. It also gives a short management method for keeping a creative team producing distinctive (not just safe) ideas, and the guest's live, informal scoring of several real company names as a demonstration of his judgment criteria.

## 2. Principles

1. The name is the highest-frequency, longest-lived brand asset. It appears on every ad, pack, PDP title, email subject line and customer mention, more often and for longer than any other brand element, so a merely "okay" name compounds a small disadvantage for years while the "right" name compounds an advantage. Condition: applies to any name meant to last; a placeholder name chosen under deadline pressure (the guest's example: "our attorney called, we need a name, we spent a few hours") is the common failure path. Evidence: the guest's own portfolio and repeat client pattern, not a controlled study. [24a1ee27]

2. A name has to do three jobs: get attention, hold attention, and be surprising. "Holding attention" is defined narrowly as processing fluency — pronounceable, and containing something the listener can understand. "Surprising" explicitly excludes comfortable or popular; the guest states this third job is where most companies get it wrong. Condition: applies at first exposure to a new name, not to an established one. [24a1ee27]

3. Comfort Trap: judge candidates by whether they sit in the "tension zone" (roughly half the audience dislikes it, energy is high, it is polarizing) or the "invisible zone" (broad consensus, safe, unremarkable). The guest's claim, attributed to Andy Grove reviewing Intel's Pentium name, is that a name so polarizing it makes people uncomfortable is a name with energy inside it worth examining, even if you ultimately reject it. Condition: this is a decision heuristic for shortlisting, not a launch-readiness test — a polarizing name still needs to be believable, legally clear and commercially sound. Do not use consensus as your tiebreaker; a shortlist where everything reads as agreeable is a sign the process filtered out the distinctive options. [9d81ed21, 0db63ae1]

4. Originality means "original in the category and context," not necessarily a coined or invented word. Real words (like Feather or Azure) can be original if nothing comparable exists in that category's naming conventions. Condition: unexpectedness is category-relative — the guest rated "Azure" highly for Microsoft (a portfolio of otherwise descriptive names) but says the identical word would have drawn less attention from Google. [24a1ee27, 99647fec]

5. Comfortable, popular and consensus-friendly names test well in research precisely because they only confirm what the category already means — they carry no differentiation. The guest's example: Clorox's "Ready Mop" tested as "a mop," which people already understood; that is not evidence of quality, only of familiarity. Condition: watch this specifically when interpreting consumer name-testing scores — a high comprehension score is not the same as a high differentiation score. [0db63ae1]

6. Suspend evaluation during ideation; evaluate later, on a schedule. Killing ideas the moment they surface ("that's too expensive," "that'll never pass legal") shuts down the problem-solving instinct the source says humans are wired to use. Condition: this is an ideation-phase rule only. It does not remove the need for a real feasibility, margin, cost and compliance pass later — someone still has to run that pass, and deferring judgment indefinitely just stalls the project. [ae16dc1d, 69fd0d78]

7. "Approximate thinking": ask contributors to stop in the middle of a spectrum that runs from bizarre/absurd/illegal at one end to safe/workable at the other, and to present unfinished, half-baked ideas rather than polishing (or discarding) before sharing. The guest states this explicitly makes team creativity "spike." Condition: works for ideation sessions (naming, bundles, subscription mechanics, landing-page angles, product extensions); the source gives no data, only his own coaching experience, and warns that if a team only ever produces safe ideas, the framing needs to be reset. [7fec0334, 15f3c7de]

8. Quantity leads to quality. The guest states clients typically get stuck after generating 50-100 names; his own agency works toward roughly 2,000 candidate words on a project, almost all of which are unusable raw material he calls "trash." Condition: this is the paid agency's working number for a client engagement, not a required target for every task — a solo operator should scale the number down but keep the ratio of large generation before selection. [3b6889a8]

9. Small teams beat group brainstorming. An internal, informally run 18-month study led the agency to stop group brainstorming and stop hiring freelancers, because peer pressure and a slow "evaluation cascade" degrade output once a group reaches four to ten people, and freelancer output lacked the internal "esprit de corps." Best output came from individuals or two-person teams. Condition: the guest calls his own study "a little sloppy and crude" by his own account — treat the conclusion as directional, not proven. Two-person teams assume staff availability; a solo operator should run sequential briefs instead of skipping the diversity of framing. [8d9104bf]

10. Vary the information diet across teams to widen the candidate space. In the guest's fiber example, Team 1 knows the full product story, Team 2 is told the product also has an added ingredient (changing their frame), and Team 3 is not told it is a fiber product at all and works from an unrelated angle (athletic performance). The third, least-informed brief produces names the other two structurally cannot reach. Condition: a small store can simulate this by running the same brief through 3 lenses (product specs, buyer outcome, an unrelated domain) rather than 3 separate teams. [8d9104bf]

11. Name the ultimate benefit, not the category. Most category labels (e.g., every fiber package says "fiber") assume the buyer already knows why that category matters and stop there. Climb the benefit ladder with the customer's own language (gut health, regularity, metabolism, "feeling lighter") and build the naming territory from the endpoint, not the ingredient. Condition: keep the category word visible in metadata, filters, tags and secondary copy so a benefit-led brand name does not hurt search discoverability in a search-driven commodity category. [6286b8c5]

12. Run a category audit before generating any names: map every existing name in the category, note the pattern (the guest's fiber example: "Fiber One," "Fiber Plus," "Ready Mop" are all descriptive), and form a first working hypothesis of "we don't know where we're going, but we know we're not going there." Then audit your own product (what is actually different), then audit the buyer (why they buy, what they expect, the ultimate benefit). This category-plus-product-plus-buyer audit doubles as a PDP positioning brief. Condition: you may return to a direction the audit initially rejected once the product truth is clearer; the first hypothesis is a filter, not a verdict. [0c6615eb]

13. Ask four sequencing questions before generating names, in this order, and expect different stakeholders to answer the first one differently: (1) How do you define winning here? (2) What do you have to win (the real product facts)? (3) What do you need to win (the breakthrough claim)? (4) What do we need to say? The guest states these "pay off greatly" with "almost no exception." [0c6615eb, 14751ff1]

14. Mine reference databases systematically instead of waiting for inspiration: Latin and Greek roots, Roman and Greek mythology, and an internally enriched periodic table (loaded with extra articles and vocabulary beyond the bare element list) are named sources. Intel Pentium came out of periodic-table work. Condition: raw database output needs heavy filtering; it is a starting point, not a source of finished names. [4e009c4e]

15. Build a cross-category, theme-based archive rather than a per-product one. The guest's example: a small car name project for Nissan and a small Intel processor name project both feed into one internal database called "small things," so unrelated client assignments cross-pollinate each other over time. [4e009c4e]

16. Synchronicity — connecting seemingly irrelevant things — is described as "the fundamental layer of creativity." The guest's own practice: spend about 30 minutes in genuinely unfamiliar material (a book or magazines never read before, on a subject unrelated to the product) looking only for transferable connections, suspending logic and evaluation during that window. He estimates this yields a new perspective, spin or insight roughly 30% of the time, "from his own practice" — not a measured rate. Impossible Burger is his repeated example: deliberately not a natural-foods-style name that would blend into a Whole Foods shelf. [4e009c4e, 3cd4209d]

17. A repeatable 20-minute solo naming routine: start with pen and paper (not a screen) and free-associate about the direction, sometimes drawing a treasure-map grid. Use AI tools to ask questions about the subject matter (for a fiber product: about fiber, digestion, lightness), not to ask for names directly — treat AI answers as territory-seeding, not as candidates. Then pick a promising territory and start crafting actual names. Condition: this reflects one practitioner's method; verify any AI tool's current interface and capability before relying on it, since these change. [d9c1184d, `[verify UI]`]

18. Judging names, not generating them, is now the scarce skill. Anyone can generate 200-300 names with AI assistants such as ChatGPT or Claude, so raw generation is no longer a competitive edge; the durable advantage is a defined judgment framework (criteria for "right" set before you look at candidates) plus proprietary data to score against. Condition: a small brand cannot replicate a naming agency's proprietary dataset, so weight your own category and customer knowledge more heavily than a generic AI naming exercise. [0b1a266e, bcf9030]

19. Believability is the internal gate before anything goes to a client or gets published: in under a second, does the viewer lean toward "I believe this could exist"? The example given is Microsoft Azure — believable as a cloud brand even though Microsoft's other names are typically descriptive. Present finalists in realistic context (a mocked article, an endorsement-style ad, a PDP header, packaging) rather than as a plain list, because most decision-makers have little practice making creative judgments and context supplies the comparison a list cannot. Condition: mock-ups must not imply real coverage, real endorsements or real claims that do not exist. [b7494c09, 0db63ae1]

20. Names compound over time in the market through accumulated familiarity, which is the source's argument for investing properly at the very start rather than settling for a rushed placeholder. Bad names create ongoing friction (search, spelling, recall). Condition: this is the same compounding argument as principle 1, applied specifically to the decision of whether to rename later. [fa435f93]

21. Creativity is described by the guest as an acquired, teachable skill built on process and persistence (citing Leonardo da Vinci as an example of tenacity, not a single burst of talent), not raw, unteachable talent. Managing creative contributors well means removing fear of instant evaluation, not demanding brilliance. Root the practice in "encouragement" (root word: courage) — give people room to flow rather than leading every move. Separate the dreaming, planning and judging stages in time (the source cites Disney's reported three-room practice) so judgment happens on a schedule, not in the room during ideation. [a68351f8]

## 3. Procedures

### A. Full naming project process (agency-scale)
1. Category audit: list every existing name/label in the category; note the dominant pattern; form an initial hypothesis of what direction to avoid. [0c6615eb, 14751ff1]
2. Product audit: build the factual list of what is actually different about your product (ingredients, process, format, materials, usage). [0c6615eb]
3. Consumer audit: establish why buyers buy, what they expect, and the ultimate benefit up the ladder from the functional one. [0c6615eb]
4. Ask the 4 strategy questions in order: how do you define winning; what do you have to win; what do you need to win; what do we need to say. [0c6615eb, 14751ff1]
5. Generate in small teams (or, for a solo operator, in sequential passes) using deliberately different information diets — one team fully briefed, one given an altered/added detail, one working an unrelated angle. Target large volume (the agency's benchmark is roughly 2,000 raw candidates for a paid project); give explicit permission to produce bad ideas. Run reference-database hunts (roots, mythology, periodic table, cross-category archive) and short bounded side-hunts (about 15 minutes) into unrelated domains as part of this step. [8d9104bf, 4e009c4e, 3b6889a8]
6. Run this generation over roughly 2 cycles across about 2 to 2.5 weeks (team members working other projects in parallel), then stop and check against the creative framework and original objectives — did each brief produce anything usable. [3b6889a8, 8d9104bf]
7. Route the surviving shortlist through trademark and legal review (paralegals plus a trademark attorney); this step alone prunes many candidates automatically. Narrow to roughly 10-15 candidate names. [4e009c4e, b7494c09]
8. Score remaining candidates for processing fluency (pronounceable, partly understandable), sound symbolism (see section 4), and category-relative unexpectedness, using the agency's proprietary scoring software (name mentioned: "Predict," AI-integrated `[verify UI]`). [99647fec, bcf9030]
9. Apply the internal believability gate: does the name look believable in under a second, presented in context? Build proof-of-concept mock-ups (article layout, endorsement-style ad, packaging or PDP visual) for 2-3 finalists before external presentation. [b7494c09, 0db63ae1]
10. Present the finalist set (roughly 10, sometimes 15 names) to the client/decision-maker inside those context mock-ups, not as a spreadsheet list. [b7494c09]
11. On sign-off, extend the deliverable beyond the bare name: nomenclature (how the product is described), a positioning line, and a short brand story. For multi-market launches, route through in-house linguists for each target market/language. [a714872, 14751ff1]
12. After launch, run a small quantitative tracking study at roughly 90-120 days to check how the name is landing. [d08af5a4, a714872]

### B. Ecommerce-adapted compressed version (small operator)
1. Do steps 1-4 above using your own product specs, reviews, support tickets and search-query logs in place of agency interviews — the sequence is the same, only the inputs differ. [14751ff1]
2. Generate through 3 lenses instead of 3 teams: product specs, buyer outcome, and one unrelated domain (aviation, geology, hometown streets, sports). Scale down the volume target but keep a large-generation-before-selection ratio. [8d9104bf, 3b6889a8]
3. Run a 20-minute solo session per the routine in principle 17 to seed additional directions. [d9c1184d]
4. Before finalizing, spend about 30 minutes in one genuinely unrelated publication or community looking only for a transferable metaphor or angle, and check it against your real product facts before using it in a claim. [3cd4209d]
5. Score the shortlist with the "no sevens" rule (force a real call, high or low, not a lukewarm middle) against: category-relative unexpectedness, processing fluency, sound symbolism, and multi-market legibility if relevant. [99647fec]
6. Build a lightweight believability mock-up (PDP header, ad in its real placement, packaging visual, search-results listing) for your top 2-3 candidates before locking in. [b7494c09]
7. Check trademark and domain availability before presenting internally as finalists (the source does this via legal counsel; a small operator should at minimum run a trademark and domain search `[web check: trademark register and domain availability for each finalist]`). [4e009c4e]

### C. Rename decision and launch
1. Check for a simple trigger first: is the current name causing a legal problem (e.g., being sued)? If so, the decision is already made. [fa435f93]
2. If not, weigh the pluses and minuses explicitly. Decline to work on renaming a client/brand that is determined to keep the old name regardless. [fa435f93]
3. Identify friction as the real trigger to act on: hard to spell, hard to find in search, hard to recall. The source's cited example, Kodium to Windsurf, took about 6 weeks to execute and he attributes the brand's subsequent growth partly to the rename — stated as his account, not an isolated, controlled result. [fa435f93]
4. Weigh company stage: the source calls a rename "not traumatic" for a Series A/B company, but says a company at Coca-Cola's scale needs a compelling reason given the accumulated brand equity. [fa435f93]
5. Launch with enthusiasm and an explicit story: "we were here, now we're going this way, and here's the benefit to you." Reassure customers explicitly that the underlying product/technology has not changed, only the name. [fa435f93]
6. Budget roughly 6-9 months of extra resources to execute the transition (redirects, listings, assets, announcement) as the cost of removing the friction. [fa435f93]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- Check: is your naming shortlist all consensus, agreeable options? Good: at least one candidate splits the room. Bad: everyone mildly likes everything. Fix: go back and generate more polarizing candidates; do not use "everyone agreed" as a selection criterion. [9d81ed21, 0db63ae1]
- Check: are you scoring candidates with a lot of "7 out of 10"s? Good: forced high/low calls. Bad: a cluster of middling scores. Fix: apply a "no sevens" rule to force reviewers to commit, since middling scores hide the specific quality (or flaw) that matters. [99647fec]
- Check: does the name pass the sub-second believability test in context (mock article, ad, PDP)? Good: viewer's first instinct is "this could exist." Bad: it reads as invented, ridiculous or as belonging to a different kind of company. Fix: rework or discard; do not present to a client/decision-maker until it clears this gate. [b7494c09, 0db63ae1]
- Check: is the category word still visible somewhere (title, tags, metadata) if you chose a benefit-led, non-descriptive brand name? Good: shoppers can still find and understand the product via search. Bad: an abstract name with no category anchor in a search-driven commodity category. Fix: add the category term back into secondary copy, filters or subtitle. [6286b8c5]
- Check: for each finalist, has it cleared trademark and domain screening? Good: clear in your target market(s). Bad: conflicts found late, after creative investment. Fix: route every shortlist through legal/trademark review before final presentation, and check domains directly `[web check: trademark register and domain registrars per market]`. [4e009c4e]
- Check: is your team's creative output landing consistently in the "invisible zone" (safe, on-brief, forgettable)? Good: some ideas provoke real internal disagreement. Bad: uniform mild approval every round. Fix: reintroduce the approximate-thinking spectrum and the "no immediate rejection" language rule; check whether contributors are being smacked down on early submissions. [15f3c7de, a68351f8]
- Check: post-rename, is name recall, category association, and correct spelling/search improving between launch and the ~90-120 day tracking wave? Good: measurable improvement in a second wave. Bad: no material change. Fix: weigh a repositioning line versus a further rename; do not change the name reflexively on noisy small-sample data. [d08af5a4]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Letting a placeholder, deadline-driven name become permanent (the "our attorney called, we need a name" origin story) instead of investing properly at the outset. [24a1ee27, fa435f93]
- Choosing a safe, comfortable, consensus-approved name because it tests well, without recognizing that comprehension and differentiation are different things. [0db63ae1]
- Running large-group brainstorming sessions: peer pressure and a slow "evaluation cascade" degrade output once a group passes about 4-10 people. [8d9104bf]
- Killing ideas in the room with instant rejection language ("that's too expensive," "that'll never pass legal") instead of reframing them as solvable problems. [ae16dc1d, 69fd0d78]
- Stopping name generation too early (at 50-100 names) because that is where most clients "got stuck." [3b6889a8]
- Scoring candidates with lukewarm, noncommittal ratings that hide the actual signal (the "no sevens" rule exists to prevent this). [99647fec]
- Assuming AI-generated name volume is itself the deliverable, instead of building a judgment framework to filter it. [0b1a266e, bcf9030]
- Skipping trademark/domain checks until late, after creative and stakeholder investment has already been made. [4e009c4e]
- Avoiding a needed rename purely out of fear of losing brand equity/momentum — the guest states he has never seen that loss materialize in his own experience, provided the relaunch is done with enthusiasm and a clear story (his claim, not independently verified). [fa435f93]
- Managing creative contributors by evaluating instead of encouraging; smacking down a first bad idea trains people to stop offering distinctive ones. [a68351f8]
- Forcing an abstract, benefit-led name onto a highly search-driven commodity category without keeping the category term visible somewhere in the listing. [6286b8c5]
