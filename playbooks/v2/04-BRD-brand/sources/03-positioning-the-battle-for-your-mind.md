## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a single lens for every brand decision: positioning happens in the prospect's mind, not in the product, and the mind already holds the category as a short mental "ladder" of brands. The book's job is to teach you to (a) read that ladder before you write a word of copy, (b) find or force open a slot on it (creneau), (c) pick a name and a claim that can hold that slot, and (d) hold the position for years without drifting into the traps that erode it: initials, free rides, line extension, "everybody" appeal, and head-on attacks on an entrenched leader. It is a strategy book about what to say and where to stand, not a tactics or media-buying manual; it gives almost no budgets, no test sizes, no modern channel guidance.

## 2. Principles

Grouped by the book's own chapter sequence. Each principle states the claim, its condition, and the evidence the authors give.

### 2.1 Positioning happens in the prospect's mind, not on the product [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
Positioning is not something you do to a product; it is what you do to the mind of the prospect. Changes to name, price, or package are cosmetic moves made to secure a slot in the mind, not real product changes. Condition: applies whenever a brand is trying to be remembered inside a category the prospect already has an opinion about. Evidence: Kotler's foreword examples (Volvo built safety into the product to own a safety position and was later bought by Ford at a premium; Häagen-Dazs set a premium price to own "premium ice cream"; Hanes' L'eggs used supermarket distribution to become the leading pantyhose brand; Little Caesars turned a two-for-one promotion into a position, then lost sales when it dropped that promotion, showing both the power of a position and how hard it is to change once set). ### 2.2 The overcommunicated society forces oversimplification [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound numbers]
Modern markets carry far more messages than minds can process, so the average mind protects itself by accepting only what matches prior belief and rejecting the rest. Numbers the authors give (era-specific, 1980-ish): per-capita US ad spend $376.62/year vs $16.87 in the rest of the world; a $1M/year advertiser reaches the average consumer with under half a cent per day; US supermarkets stock ~12,000 products; the average mind holds no more than about 7 units at a time (citing Harvard psychologist George A. Miller), which is why people rarely name more than 7 brands in a high-interest category and often only 1-2 in a low-interest one. Consequence: communication is a "selection project," not a transmission project — pick one claim, sharpen it, discard everything else. Condition: applies to any paid or owned message competing for attention; the specific dollar and count figures are 1980 US data and should not be quoted as current. [079c0cc5fa06950033c13a1c] [d6bb004cc9ea61609a6119d3]

### 2.3 Expectation filters perception; the mind sees, tastes, and reads what it expects [TIMELESS mechanism]
People do not evaluate products neutrally; they confirm what they already believe. Evidence: a Democrat and Republican each pull confirming facts from the same article; blind champagne tastings rank cheap California brands above French ones, but not with labels visible; pouring cheap wine into an old fine-wine bottle changes the taster's verdict; Gablinger's diet-beer advertising primed people to expect a bad taste and they reported one. Application: advertising's job includes deliberately setting an accurate, positive expectation, and screening copy, naming, and imagery for accidental negative priming (e.g., "diet," "light," or overly clinical ingredient language). Condition: works both for and against you — priming can create a self-fulfilling positive reception or a self-fulfilling negative one. [482e1229432994c711ca255e]

### 2.4 Being first in the mind beats being best in the product [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
The easy way into a category's mental ladder is to be first; the hard way is to be second, which the authors call "nowhere." Examples: Lindbergh (first solo transatlantic flight) is remembered, the second flyer is not; Kodak, Kleenex, Xerox, Hertz, Coca-Cola, and General Electric all became generic-strength names by being first into their category's ladder. "First" means first in the mind, not first to invent: IBM did not invent the computer (Sperry-Rand did) but built the mental position; Amerigo Vespucci arrived 5 years after Columbus but got America named after him because he named the New World as a separate continent and published five letters about it (one translated into 40 languages over 25 years) — Columbus stayed quiet and died in jail. Condition: this is a strong, repeated claim by the authors ("second is nowhere") that is contradicted in practice by many durable No. 2 and No. 3 brands; treat as their doctrine, not a law. [1a89c8f731f56e25ef60dcbe]

### 2.5 The product ladder: every category is a short, ranked list in the mind [TIMELESS mechanism]
People rank brands in a category on a mental "ladder," rarely more than about 7 rungs, often fewer. To gain share, a challenger must either dislodge the brand above it (called "usually impossible") or relate its own brand to the leader's position; advertising that ignores the competitor's ladder position "fails to get through." A genuinely new category needs a new ladder, which the mind resists unless it is explained against an old one — hence "horseless carriage," "off-track betting," "lead-free gasoline," "sugar-free soda." Procedure implication: map your category's ladder (ask real prospects to name brands unprompted, in order) before writing any positioning claim. [d6bb004cc9ea61609a6119d3] [92e5149e81d9fc79c1f46fc6]

### 2.6 The "against" position: relate to the leader rather than compete with them [CASE-BOUND example, TIMELESS mechanism]
If you are not first, state your position relative to the leader rather than claiming superiority. Case: Avis, after 13 straight years of losses, ran "Avis is only No. 2 in rent-a-cars, so why go with us? We try harder," and reported profits of $1.2M, $2.6M, then $5M in the next three years before being sold to ITT. The authors argue Avis succeeded because it related itself to Hertz's position (ladder: Hertz #1, Avis #2, National #3), not because it literally "tried harder" ("If trying harder were the secret of success, Harold Stassen would have been President many times over"). Anecdote: Time magazine initially refused the line as too competitive with Hertz; the ad was briefly softened to "we try damned hard," then reverted once Time relented. Parallel case: 7-Up's "the uncola" wormed onto the cola ladder (roughly 2 of every 3 soft drinks sold were colas at the time) by defining itself as what it was not; McCormick Communications used the same device to take a Providence, RI radio station from also-ran to No. 1 as "the unrock station." Condition: requires an actual, recognized leader in the prospect's mind to relate against; if no leader exists, there is nothing to be "against." [4f50a6f783d79bdabf187a08] [078eeee4243ee14c063212a2] [0d6ad2f3cdfc0731f686523f] [ff2d9fce9f885510166869db]

### 2.7 The F.W.M.T.S. trap: don't abandon a working position [CASE-BOUND example]
"Forgot What Made Them Successful": after Avis was bought by ITT, it dropped the No.2 line for "Avis is going to be No. 1" — the authors call this "advertising your aspirations," wrong psychologically (it broke underdog sympathy) and strategically (Avis had no real path to unseat Hertz). Despite many later campaigns ("The wizard of Avis," "You don't have to run through airports"), the authors say only the original No.2 line is remembered, and warn Avis will regret abandoning it if National ever overtakes it. Condition: applies once a position is actually established and working; consistency, not novelty, is what a working position needs. [a3b068db34cdc3d4b13ae8ee]

### 2.8 "You can't get there from here": some positions are structurally hopeless [CASE-BOUND example]
A company can have a great product, sales force, and ad budget and still fail if it is trying to occupy a position the mind will not give it. Case: RCA declared in 1970 it would take a "firm No. 2 position" in computers against IBM (which the authors say held ~70% of the mainframe market); by 1971 Business Week reported "the $250 million disaster that hit RCA," and GE sold its computer business to Honeywell the same period. The authors' 1969 prediction (in Industrial Marketing) was that no company could progress head-on against IBM's position; their prescription was to relate to a position you already own instead — RCA should have built computers around its existing "communications" position; GE around its (retained, profitable) time-sharing niche; NCR succeeded by concentrating on retail data-entry (computerized cash registers) rather than general-purpose computers. Condition: the authors argue outright failure is often preferable to mediocre, expensive failure — this is a resource-allocation opinion, not a measured finding. [d68f5389fbe9fd8aa4f252f6]

### 2.9 Leadership: reinforce, don't repeat "we're No. 1" [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
Once a brand owns the top rung, running "we're No.1" ads is self-defeating: if the prospect already knows, they wonder why you're insecure; if they don't know, you've defined leadership on your own terms, which does not transfer. Better: reinforce the original claim that got you there (Coca-Cola's "the real thing" positions competitors as imitations); sell the category, not just yourself (the source says IBM's ads sold the value of computers generally); claim "we invented it" when true (Xerox, Polaroid, Zippo); broaden use occasions to grow the category (Arm & Hammer in the refrigerator, orange juice "isn't just for breakfast anymore"). Leaders should "cover" competitive threats early: General Motors spent $50M to license the Wankel engine as "cheap insurance," while Kodak and 3M's refusal to buy early xerography rights let Haloid become Xerox, a $9B company Fortune called the maker of "probably the single most profitable product ever manufactured in the United States" (era-specific claim). Multibrand is really single-position strategy: Procter & Gamble never put "Ivory Detergent" on the market (it introduced Tide instead) and gave each brand — Joy, Crest, Head & Shoulders, Tide, Cascade — its own separate position; the authors say P&G did roughly twice the business and five times the profit of Colgate-Palmolive (era-specific claim), which does put house names on many products. [e8ed01410f31b8048c62bb2d] [be7172025ae3ad1ce9bab497]

### 2.10 Followers: cherchez le creneau ("look for the hole") [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
A follower that tries to be "better" instead of first usually fails because it spends time perfecting the product, launches with a smaller budget than the leader, and slaps the house name on it — "all deadly traps." Instead, find an unoccupied creneau (niche) in the mind, not the factory, and claim it plainly. Named creneau types and their era examples:
- **Size**: Volkswagen's "Think small" claimed the small-car position even though other small cars existed; no one had claimed the position itself.
- **High price**: Michelob was not the first premium domestic beer, but the first to build that position ("First class is Michelob"); other era examples: BMW 635-CS, Mercedes 500-SEL, S.T. Dupont lighters, Piaget watches, Mobil 1 synthetic oil. Rule stated explicitly: the secret is being first to establish the high-price position with a valid product story in a category open to it — "charging high prices is not the way to get rich."
- **Low price**: works well for new, unproven products (fax machines, videotape players at the time) where the buyer is "taking a chance"; less available where retailers have already trained shoppers to expect discounts (generic/no-name foods cited as an example).
- **Sex**: Marlboro built a masculine position (climbing from 5th to 1st in cigarette sales over a decade); Virginia Slims did the reverse with femininity; late me-toos failed (Lorillard's "Luke," ~20 years after Marlboro; Eve, a feminine me-too after Virginia Slims). Counter-example: Revlon's Charlie became the best-selling perfume in the world by breaking the feminine-name convention.
- **Age**: Geritol (older adults), Aim toothpaste (children, reaching ~10% share against Crest and Colgate).
- **Time of day**: Nyquil, "the first night-time cold remedy," deliberately sacrificed the daytime market to own the night slot.
- **Distribution**: L'eggs was first to sell hosiery through supermarkets and mass merchandisers, becoming the category leader.
- **Heavy-user framing**: Schaefer positioned as "the one beer to have when you're having more than one."
Condition: the authors warn timing is everything — being first on a dimension works; copying a dimension someone else already owns (Luke, Eve) does not. [7cbf151d9951531fb508dfaa] [7f129dfe890c89b5bc79bd38] [6eae715aafa428f4e0ebbdc4]

### 2.11 The factory creneau vs. the mind creneau [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
A gap in your product line (a "factory creneau") is not the same as a gap in the prospect's perception (a "mind creneau"); only the latter is a real position. Case: Ford's Edsel filled a genuine gap between Ford/Mercury and Lincoln inside the company's lineup, but there was no open slot for another heavily chromed, medium-priced car in the buyer's mind — the authors argue a "high-performance," two-door, bucket-seat version with a matching name might have succeeded instead. Case: the National Observer (a weekly newspaper) filled a printing-press gap at Dow Jones but no perceptual gap, since readers already had Time, Newsweek, and U.S. News for weekly news. Case: Frost 8/80, "the first dry, white whisky" (Brown-Forman, 1971), was a genuine technical first with no mind creneau — the position was already occupied by gin, vodka, rum, and tequila — and died within 2 years after projecting only 1/3 of forecast volume. [47ce0c7f290e44b91b9b3995]

### 2.12 The everybody trap: appealing to all excludes the position [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound example]
Trying to be all things to all people worked when there were fewer brands and less noise; today it keeps an already-dominant brand afloat but is "deadly" for a brand trying to build a position from nothing. Case: Rheingold beer ran ads showing Italians, Black people, Irish people, and Jewish people all drinking Rheingold to court New York's working class broadly — the campaign alienated every group instead of impressing any of them. Condition: a strong position requires deliberately not appealing to everyone; the authors call this a "sacrifice," not a limitation to be minimized. [32142fdf58930bd258189588] [8bc6ea16e3c6ef20e8906f7f]

### 2.13 Repositioning the competition beats comparative "we're better" advertising [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
When no open creneau exists, move an existing competitor out of its slot by making the prospect reconsider the competitor's product, not by praising your own. Case: Tylenol's ad ran about 60 words on aspirin's risks (stomach irritation, allergic reactions, hidden GI bleeding) before mentioning Tylenol at all; Tylenol became the top analgesic brand, ahead of Anacin, Bayer, Bufferin, and Excedrin. Case: Royal Doulton repositioned Lenox (widely assumed to be imported, "sounds English") by stating "the china of Stoke-on-Trent, England vs. Lenox, the china of Pomona, New Jersey," reportedly gaining 6 points of share. Case: Stolichnaya's ads pointed out that rival "Russian" vodkas were made in Schenectady PA, Hartford CT, and Lawrenceburg IN, while Stolichnaya's own label said Leningrad — sales soared (with a temporary dip tied to the 1979-80 Soviet invasion of Afghanistan). Case: Borden's Wise struck back at Procter & Gamble's Pringle's by reading both ingredient labels on TV ("potatoes, vegetable oil, salt" vs. "dehydrated potatoes, mono- and diglycerides, ascorbic acid, butylated hydroxy-anisole"); Pringle's share fell from 18% to 10%, with "tastes like cardboard" the most common complaint — the authors' point is that taste is partly in the mind, shaped by the words used ("dihydrogen oxide" vs. "water"). Case: P&G's Scope used the two words "medicine breath" to undercut Listerine's "taste you hate, twice a day," but the authors say Scope never fully cashed in because the name itself sounds like a board game, not a good-tasting mouthwash. Contrast with **comparative** advertising, which the authors say usually fails: "Ban is more effective than Right Guard, Secret, Sure..." invites the reasonable question "if you're so good, why aren't you the leader?" Real repositioning changes what the prospect believes about the competitor's product; comparative advertising just uses the competitor as your own benchmark. Legal/ethical note (era-specific): the authors trace US TV networks' acceptance of comparative ads to FTC pressure on ABC and CBS in 1972 (NBC had already dropped its ban in 1964) and the 1974 American Association of Advertising Agencies guideline reversal; they argue disparagement is no more unethical than negative news coverage, "society sets the rules." ### 2.14 The name is the single most important marketing decision [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
The name is the hook that hangs the brand on the ladder; a name should begin the positioning process by signaling the benefit (Head & Shoulders, DieHard, Intensive Care, Shake 'n Bake, Edge) rather than being a lazy, internal, or founder-derived label. "Shakespeare was wrong" — people smell/taste/see what the name tells them to; renaming Hog Island to "Paradise Island" changed its fortunes; a hypothetical perfume named "Alfred" would not have sold like "Charlie" did. Descriptive names (Spray 'n Wash) beat coined ones for most launches because they carry meaning at zero teaching cost and block me-too competitors from the same territory; a coined, meaningless name (Kodak, Kleenex, Keds) is only justified when you are first in the mind with an absolutely new product ("the Charles Lindbergh syndrome" — get there first and any name works). Guard against a name sliding all the way to generic: "Lite beer from Miller" was shortened by the public and press to "Miller Lite," and Miller lost exclusive trademark rights to "light" for beer, spawning Schlitz Light, Coors Light, and Bud Light. Sound matters as much as spelling, because the mind processes language aurally before visually: RCA (3 syllables) beats "Radio Corporation of America" (12); GE (2) beats "General Electric" (6); but Ford stays "Ford" because it is already 1 syllable, and Trans World Airlines' "TWA" is phonetically no shorter than "Trans World." Common initial letters in English are S, C, P, A, T; the rarest are X, Z, Y, Q, K — the authors use this as a rough memorability guide. A hidden or evasive name invites suspicion: the authors argue "margarine" should have been "soy butter" to give "pride of origin" the way "corn sugars" (replacing "high-fructose corn syrup") put corn sweeteners on equal footing with cane and beet sugar; they draw an explicit parallel to "Negro" (a "margarine name") giving way to "Black" as a polarizing, pride-building choice. Names decay: Eastern Airlines' regional-sounding name put it in the same mental bracket as Piedmont and Ozark rather than with United and American, despite serving national routes and spending ~$70M/year on advertising (era-specific figure); it consistently ranked 4th of 4 major carriers in passenger surveys. Confusable names cost share: B.F. Goodrich was first to market steel-belted radials in the US, yet in a later survey 56% of buyers credited Goodyear (who didn't make them domestically) versus 47% for Goodrich. ### 2.15 The no-name trap: initials and acronyms only work after you're already famous [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound example]
Initials are a "cause and effect" confusion: IBM got rich and famous first (cause), which is why its initials are widely understood (effect); adopting initials early does not reverse the sequence. The authors cite their own Business Week subscriber survey: average awareness of "initial" companies was 49% vs. 68% for a matched set of "name" companies, a 19-point gap (single, era-specific study — treat as illustrative, not a benchmark). Practical tests: does the mind still hold the full name behind the initials (FBI, IRS: yes; HUD, GAF, CPC International: mostly no)? Is there real phonetic shortening (RCA at 3 syllables beats 12; GE beats 6) or none (TWA is not shorter than "Trans World")? Does the initialed name still sort sensibly in a directory or search list (MCI sorted 48 pages away from "Mc-" names in the Manhattan phone book; USM sorted with unrelated "US-" listings)? Deliberate, meaningful acronyms can work (Fiat, Sabena, CARE, MADD) because they were built to. Condition: applies to brands not yet household-famous; the rule is "do the initials add phonetic or mnemonic value," not "never abbreviate." [868f7e43ad95961d44c7f71a] [dca1b04ea88b10fa605de960]

### 2.16 The free-ride trap: a famous name does not automatically transfer [CASE-BOUND example]
It is tempting to attach a new product to an already-famous name to save launch spend, but the authors argue the "free ride" often does not happen. Case: Alka-Seltzer Plus, meant to compete with Dristan and Contac, instead cannibalized Alka-Seltzer itself; over successive label redesigns "Alka-Seltzer" shrank and "Plus" grew. Case: Life Savers gum, despite Life Savers candy's brand equity ("excellence in flavor, outstanding value, dependable quality" per the company's own research), never got more than a small market share and was quietly killed — while the same company's Bubble Yum (a new name, first-to-market soft bubble gum) became a runaway success that outsold Life Savers candy. Condition: the book explicitly allows exceptions (it cites GE as a case where extension worked), so treat this as a warning about assumed transfer, not an absolute prohibition. [46df3117bf9a3ac4930e3678] [e2d2e2bdb4c664cb4ca4d2fb]

### 2.17 The line-extension trap and the teeter-totter principle [TIMELESS mechanism, heavily case-bound examples]
Line extension — putting an established name on a genuinely different product — is called "the marketing sickness of the past decade" and "seldom works," even though the internal logic ("our customers trust us, they'll accept the new item, and it saves advertising dollars") is "overwhelming." The **teeter-totter principle**: one name cannot stand for two distinctly different products without one meaning weakening as the other strengthens. Documented era cases: Bayer nonaspirin never exceeded a tiny share of the acetaminophen market, and Bayer's total analgesic share kept falling as it added timed-release aspirin, decongestant tablets, and nonaspirin products; Dial soap has a large soap share and a very small deodorant share after "Dial deodorant"; Protein 21 shampoo/conditioner fell from a 13% shampoo-market share to 2% after Mennen added Protein 21 hairspray (regular/extra-hold, scented/unscented), conditioner (two formulas), concentrate, and "Protein 29" for men; Scott lost toilet-tissue leadership to Charmin partly because ScotTissue, Scotties, Scotkins, and BabyScott diluted what "Scott" meant, while the company's later Viva (towels) and Cottonelle (tissue), each with its own name, succeeded; Heinz became No.1 in ketchup by making "Heinz" mean ketchup — and lost pickle leadership to Vlasic in the process (the teeter-totter's other side); Xerox spent almost $1B buying Scientific Data Systems, renamed it Xerox Data Systems, ran an ad literally headlined "This Xerox machine can't make a copy," and wrote off an additional $84.4M when it folded; Pall Mall was the No.1 US cigarette brand in 1964 (14.4% share) but fell to 3.8% share after line-extending into Pall Mall Menthol, Extra Mild, and Light 100s starting 1965, opening the door for Benson & Hedges to claim the 100mm-cigarette position instead; Volkswagen fell from 1st to 4th among imported cars (behind Toyota, Datsun, Honda) after extending its reliability position to buses and jeep-type vehicles; Levi's line-extended into casual clothes and then found its core jeans position undercut by "designer label" jeans; Saturday Review's 4-edition split cost an $17M loss; Sara Lee's frozen-entree line (Chicken & Noodles Au Gratin, Beef & Pepper Stew) cost ~$8M before being dropped; Levi's-and-Brown-Shoe's "Levi's for Feet" failed. Diagnostic tools the authors name: the **shopping-list test** (write "Kleenex, Bayer, Dial" on a list — most people know exactly what to buy; write "Heinz, Scott, Kraft" and the reader cannot tell which product is meant) and the **bartender test** ("Chivas on the rocks" reliably gets the premium brand; "Cutty on the rocks" leaves ambiguous whether you meant Cutty Sark or the pricier Cutty 12; step-up names like Cutty 12 and Johnnie Walker Black Label under-sell versus a cleanly separate premium name like Chivas Regal). Step-down extensions show the opposite failure curve — a fast initial hit followed by a slow-motion collapse of the parent's prestige (Packard's inexpensive "Clipper" was its best-selling model and killed the company's luxury position within roughly two decades). The one clean exception the authors give for why an extension can survive: Vaseline Intensive Care works because customers simply say "Intensive Care," never "Vaseline," so the extension effectively earned its own name in speech even while sharing a package. Short-term illusion: a line-extension launch produces an "instant flash" of recognition and of trade-stocking sales (the authors' arithmetic: booking $1M in first orders needs only ~$35 of stock per US supermarket) that looks like a hit for 6 months before reorders fail to arrive. ### 2.18 When line extension can work: reverse extension and the 5 rules of the road [TIMELESS mechanism]
The authors do allow two escape hatches. First, **reverse line extension** ("broadening the base"): keep the identical product, package, and name and simply promote a new use occasion — Johnson's baby shampoo became a leading adult shampoo purely by promoting its mildness to grown-ups (the authors argue a separate "Johnson's Adult Shampoo" would have flopped); Blue Nun white wine grew by being promoted as good with meat, not just fish; Arm & Hammer baking soda succeeded as a fridge/drain deodorizer but a literal "Arm & Hammer deodorant" line extension went nowhere. Second, the **5 rules of the road** for deciding house name vs. new name on a genuine new product: (1) Expected volume — potential winners should get a new name; small-volume items can ride the house name; (2) Competition — a crowded field favors a new name, a vacuum favors the house name; (3) Advertising support — a big ad budget favors a new name, a small one favors the house name; (4) Significance — a breakthrough favors a new name, a commodity (e.g., industrial chemicals) favors the house name; (5) Distribution — off-the-shelf/self-serve items favor a new name, items sold through reps can carry the house name. The authors present these as a combined judgment call, not a scored formula, and note companies with thousands of small-volume SKUs (3M is their example) cannot give every item its own name. ### 2.19 Positioning a company: name obsolescence and weak corporate platforms [TIMELESS mechanism, case-bound examples]
Corporate names lock in past reputations even after the business changes: Pullman is barely in the railroad-car business, Greyhound gets most of its revenue outside buses, yet both remain perceptually stuck to their old identity, and the authors argue no amount of "we're more than a bus company" corporate advertising fixes that — only a name change does. A corporate position needs one attribute that spans the whole product range: Ford settled on "innovation" ("Ford has a better idea") because it makes every size and type of vehicle; the authors call "our people are our greatest resource" a weak, common platform, because prospects already assume bigger, more successful companies have better people, so a smaller company claiming this creates a credibility gap it cannot resolve. "Diversification" is called explicitly ineffective as a corporate theme: GE is known as the world's largest electrical manufacturer, not a "diversified maker of industrial, transportation, chemical, and appliance products," and even the stock market, per the authors, undervalues conglomerates like ITT and Gulf & Western relative to their parts. A position can also become so broad it is meaningless: Bell & Howell once called itself "a developer and supplier of information systems for work, education, and entertainment." [dc9410d03a81c7a42d5ecb80]

### 2.20 Third-leg strategy: extend from the position you already own [CASE-BOUND, and this specific strategy was never proven — the authors' proposal was not adopted]
Rather than fighting a leader on its own turf, grow outward from your own owned position. Framework: the 1980s office had three "legs" — telephone (AT&T), typewriter/computer (IBM), copier (Xerox); the authors observe Xerox failed at computers/workstations/networks (IBM's leg) and IBM failed at copiers (Xerox's leg), so the "gap" between legs, not a head-on clash, is the opportunity. Their proposal for Xerox: reframe the copier leg as the emerging "output leg" (printers, scanners, storage) and coin "lasography" to connect Xerox's copier heritage (xerography) to the leading-edge laser technology already credible in telecom, medicine, retail checkout, and consumer electronics. Caveat stated plainly by the source: this was a proposed strategy, not a documented Xerox success — the book does not report Xerox adopting or benefiting from it. [97bafe17846bfa7fbe771de2]

### 2.21 Positioning a place before positioning the vehicle that serves it [CASE-BOUND examples]
When demand for the underlying destination or category does not exist, positioning your specific brand inside it is wasted spend. Case: Sabena Belgian World Airlines sold "food and service" while only about 1 in 50 North Atlantic travelers flew to Belgium at all — the fix was to position Belgium itself, not Sabena, borrowing an already-trusted anchor perception (Amsterdam) and an outside authority (the Michelin Guide, which rated 5 Belgian cities "three-star, worth a special journey" vs. 1 for Holland) for the ad "In beautiful Belgium, there are five Amsterdams." The program generated a large inquiry volume (including a call from Holland's tourism minister demanding it be killed) but was abandoned when new, uncommitted management changed strategy — the authors treat this as proof that positioning requires sustained management commitment, independent of the concept's quality. Parallel case: Jamaica's positioning search tried (1) hunting for one perfect existing postcard image, (2) shooting new photography, and only succeeded on (3) looking into the prospect's mind, finding that the existing verbal image of Jamaica already matched Hawaii's ("big green volcanic mountains meeting blue sea"), and building "the Hawaii of the Caribbean" around verifiable facts (Blue Mountain at 7,402 ft, higher than any point east of the US Mississippi; Jamaica's land area larger than the Bahamas, Puerto Rico, US Virgin Islands, or Bermuda). The authors warn generic Caribbean beach-and-palm imagery gets filed by the mind into one indistinguishable "Islands" slot and tunes out, unlike Bermuda's distinctive motorbikes-and-pink-sand imagery. Note: the book's own account leaves Jamaica's campaign unresolved, tied up in politics. [6b46650fd04c67f1d8028d1c] [2362e3449185aa8afe1f00d9] [7a5f6e23a28dcc13fb520626] [ea573c35f69c92249e71dff9] [f0d81e00c9a55aef8eef36b1]

### 2.22 Positioning a product by repositioning the category, not the product [CASE-BOUND example]
When a small-budget brand cannot outspend a category's dominant advertising, the fix can be to redefine the category itself around the leaders' hidden weakness. Case: Milk Duds (Switzer Clark) could not out-advertise Hershey's, Nestlé's, Mounds, Almond Joy, Reese's, Snickers, or Milky Way, so instead of trying to build a separate identity it positioned itself as "America's long-lasting alternative to the candy bar," exploiting the real and voiced consumer frustration that shrinking candy bars "don't last very long." The authors say this obvious angle had never appeared in 15 years of Milk Duds' prior TV advertising, and that using it reversed a sales decline and produced record volume. Research finding worth noting: the actual Milk Duds buyer was profiled as a "sophisticated" 10-year-old, an experienced, cautious, value-seeking purchasing agent, not "some little kid who doesn't know the score" — a reminder to define the real prospect before writing to them. [babb616ba8a918fd717183cf]

### 2.23 Positioning a service: relate to the nearest existing mental anchor, then test the frame [CASE-BOUND example with real test data]
For Western Union's Mailgram (launched 1970 with the US Postal Service, delivered next business day, sent by phone/telex/TWX/computer/fax), the authors rejected the technically accurate framing ("new automated computerized electronic communication service") because it relates to nothing already in the mind. Two candidate frames were tested head-to-head in 6 large US markets (Boston, Chicago, Houston, LA, Philadelphia, San Francisco) over a 13-week program: "Mailgram: impact of a Telegram at a fraction of the cost" (anchored to the already-famous Western Union Telegram, priced at roughly 1/3 the cost) vs. "Mailgram: a new high-speed service for important messages" (anchored to first-class mail, at the time 68 billion letters/year, ~815 per US household/year). Result: pre-campaign awareness was statistically even (~23-27%) across both test groups; after 13 weeks, the low-cost-Telegram cities' awareness rose from 23% to 47%, while the high-speed-letter cities' awareness actually fell slightly (27% to 25%); Telegram volume itself held stable (no cannibalization observed) even though Mailgram was explicitly positioned against it. Years later Western Union switched Mailgram's strategy to "next-day delivery" and hired a new agency; the authors report volume declined immediately afterward, and call this an unusually clean example because "most examples are clouded by other factors." Note the internal objection the authors record and reject: "why position Mailgram against the Telegram, also a Western Union service? Why take business from ourselves?" — logic they say is sound but not the best guide to human minds. [9794ccdb926ce12b587b0b87] [c4d605d4c32792d4464c12ca]

### 2.24 Positioning local businesses against a giant's real weakness — and matching operations to the promise [CASE-BOUND, with real research data]
Two banking cases. **Long Island Trust** (the largest local bank on Long Island, under new competitive pressure once NY State allowed unrestricted branch banking, letting Citibank/Chase/Chemical move in): the authors ran semantic-differential research asking prospects to score all 6 competing banks 1-10 on 6 attributes — 4 traditional (many branches, full range of services, quality of service, large capital) and 2 local (helps Long Island residents, helps the Long Island economy). Long Island Trust rated **last** on all 4 traditional attributes but **first** on both local attributes. Rather than "fix weaknesses" (conventional wisdom), the strategy accepted the one thing the prospect was already willing to give the bank — the "Long Island" position — and ran ads naming the enemy explicitly (contrasting the bank's 50+ years and Long-Island-only presence against city banks' branches in the Bahamas and Cayman Islands, worth ~$75B in loans booked there per the source). Result 15 months later, same research repeated: Long Island Trust moved from last to first on "many branches" (despite Chemical Bank literally having more than double its branch count) and from last to first on "large capital," and improved from 6th to 4th on both "full range of services" and "quality of service." **United Jersey** (3rd-largest local bank, sandwiched between NYC banks to the north and Philadelphia banks to the south, offering essentially the same regulated services as competitors): studied competitors, not itself, per the "Tylenol approach," and named the leaders' real weakness — large-bank slowness — building the "fast-moving bank" position. Crucially, operations were changed *before* the advertising ran: (1) decentralized lending decisions (one team could approve a $10M loan, meeting daily); (2) cross-trained staff so no question got referred elsewhere; (3) the state's largest private ATM network; (4) lock-box pickup 6x/weekday, 4x/weekend with its own ZIP code; (5) FACT terminals for fast merchant transaction approval; (6) an explicit responsiveness commitment; (7) a new, geographically central Princeton HQ. TV ads then contrasted United Jersey against a fictional "Lethargic National Bank"; unaided awareness nearly tripled within about a year, and earnings rose a reported $30M (26%) that year. Condition stated by the authors: "there is no one positioning approach that will work everywhere" — the two banks needed different strategies because their starting positions differed. [fcfff9855adc5037bab95851] [6355cdae1a6b79393b2e98b4] [f67d4b2a278eafdd5338aad8]

### 2.25 Borrowed authority can justify an inconvenience [CASE-BOUND example]
Stowe, Vermont needed a reason for skiers to drive 1-2 hours past closer Vermont resorts (Stratton, Sugarbush, Big Bromley, Mt. Snow). The authors used ski/travel writer Abby Rand's "top 10 ski resorts in the world" list in Harper's Bazaar (Stowe was the only Eastern US resort on it, alongside Aspen, Courchevel, Jackson Hole, Kitzbühel, Portillo, St. Christoph, St. Moritz, Sun Valley, Vail) to run "Of the world's top 10 ski resorts, only one is in the East," generating a reported surge in brochure requests and attendance records. The mechanism named: humans defer readily to authority, illustrated by an unrelated anecdote (Allen Funt's Candid Camera "Delaware Closed Today" sign — motorists asked "Is Jersey open?" without questioning the premise). Condition: this only works with a genuinely credible, independent authority; a self-serving or fabricated "top 10" list would not carry the same weight and risks trust. [2e309f07feea6f476a03e009]

### 2.26 Repositioning an institution: retrace the original mission, state it simply [CASE-BOUND example, execution never happened]
Applying positioning to the Catholic Church post-Vatican II: symptoms of a lost position included regular Mass attendance falling below 50% of Catholics (a reported 20% drop while Protestant attendance stayed stable) and a reported 60% drop in vocations, alongside the Church ranking last among major institutions in a U.S. News & World Report executive survey despite being called (by Protestant theologian Peter Berger) the "largest community of moral authority in American society." Method: retrace the organization's original mission the way a corporate identity project pores over old annual reports — here, retracing Scripture (Matt. 17:23, Matt. 28:19) to the simple position "teacher of the word" (vs. the pre-Vatican-II "teacher of the law"). The authors' own caveat: "what happened? Nothing" — bishops resisted lay people prescribing strategy and, per the authors, favored a theologian's more complex "six roles" answer because "the human mind admires the complicated and dismisses the obvious." Treat this as a method illustration with a documented non-adoption, not a proof of results. [f60fcd1475d34ddf220668ef]

### 2.27 Positioning yourself and your career [TIMELESS mechanism, illustrative anecdotes]
The same rules apply to a person: define a single concept you stand for ("I'm the best lawyer in Dallas") rather than vacillating between several; avoid initials on the way up (only useful once you are already known); avoid line-extension names for people too (Mary 1/Mary 2/Mary 3; Frank Sinatra Jr. "starts with two strikes" because the audience expects him to sing worse than his father, and — per the mind's confirmation bias — hears it that way). "Anything worth doing is worth doing lousy": the authors argue trying often and succeeding sometimes builds a better reputation than only attempting sure things, illustrating with Ty Cobb's 96-of-134 (70%) stolen-base rate being remembered over Max Carey's 51-of-53 (96%), and jockey Eddie Arcaro's 250 consecutive losses before his first win — anecdotes about persistence, not measured career outcomes. "Find a horse to ride": success depends more on external leverage than solo effort — pick a growing company (not a declining one, however loyal you feel), pick a competent boss to work under, cultivate business friendships actively (not only when job-hunting), be willing to champion an unpopular idea early (expect resistance as a sign the idea matters — quoting psychologist Charles Osgood), and have faith in others' ideas (Ray Kroc bought the McDonald brothers' concept and name for "relatively few dollars" and became one of the richest people in America through outsider vision, not the brothers themselves). [5b737610a5e97b90e2397c83]

### 2.28 Six questions to start any positioning program [TIMELESS mechanism]
Before writing any creative, answer, in this order: (1) What position do we already own? — get this from the marketplace via research, not the marketing department, and look at the big picture (Sabena's problem was Belgium, not the airline; 7-Up's problem was cola's share of mind, not lemon-lime preference). (2) What position do we want to own? — "own" is the operative word; do not aim for a slot someone else already holds (Edsel), and do not aim too broad to defend (a Rheingold-style everybody claim). (3) Whom must we outgun? — map competitors' claimed positions and find a rung nobody holds firmly; never plan a head-on approach against an entrenched leader. (4) Do we have enough money? — it takes money to build, establish, and hold a share of mind; if not enough for national scale, shrink the map. (5) Can we stick it out? — positioning is cumulative; expect to hold the same basic strategy for years, varying only tactics. (6) Does our execution match the position? — check whether creative, tone, and even the team's own presentation reinforce or undercut the intended claim. [e247ee27ad80dc32e7426a42] [4c9bc44b263f2a103dd159ba]

### 2.29 Words are empty containers; rename when the word cannot hold the position [TIMELESS mechanism]
Per general semantics (crediting Alfred Korzybski), words have no inherent meaning — the mind fills them. If a name cannot hold your intended concept, discard it rather than force the concept in: "Volkswagen" could not carry the concept of a medium-sized luxury car, so the same manufacturer used "Audi" instead, which could. A name only works if it is not "too far out of touch with reality" — the mind refuses to use a mismatched word (a toothpaste tube everyone calls "small" but the label calls "large"; "Red China" persisted as a name because outside observers didn't believe it was a "people's republic"). The authors extend this to say the "psychologically right" name changes perceived reality itself (a car named "Mustang" reads as sportier than the identical car named "Turtle" would). [e75b50392fca21aeffb8054d]

### 2.30 The discipline of holding a position: vision, courage, objectivity, simplicity, subtlety, sacrifice, patience, global outlook [TIMELESS mechanism]
Change is constant, but the authors argue the underlying position should almost never change — only the tactics dramatizing it (their example: finding new ways to keep "Ronald McDonald end up eating a hamburger"). Named requirements: **vision** (decide what the business will be doing in 5-10 years, since "changing the direction of a large company is like turning an aircraft carrier — it takes a mile"; era-cautionary examples of chasing booms instead of vision: Singer into home appliances, RCA into computers, General Foods into fast food, all called "debris," contrasted with Maytag, Disney, and Avon "sticking to their knitting"); **courage** (leadership positions are won by pouring in money and initiative while a category is still fluid — Hershey's position was so strong it felt no need to advertise, a luxury Mars couldn't afford, but when Hershey finally did advertise "it wasn't in time," and the authors note Hershey's milk-chocolate bar was, by the time of writing, not even in the top 5); **objectivity** (eliminate ego, and get a second person or outside "backboard" to bounce ideas off — "positioning is a game best played by two people," which the authors note is why the book itself has two authors); **simplicity** ("only an obvious idea will work today"; examples given: "Raisins from California. Nature's candy." and "Bubble Yum. Number yum in bubble gum."; the ideal ad is simple enough that it *is* the strategy statement, as with "Avis is only No. 2..."); **subtlety** (the winning spot is usually near the center of the spectrum, not the extreme edge — "Rolls-Royce thinking" is the trap of a unique position too narrow to sustain a business: Rolls-Royce sold roughly 1,000 cars/year in the US, about 0.01% of the market, versus Cadillac's 300,000+, both "luxury" but wildly different in reach); **sacrifice** (a real position requires giving something up — NyQuil deliberately gave up the daytime cold-remedy market to own nighttime); **patience**, expressed through 4 roll-out patterns for brands that cannot launch at full scale — geographic (build one market, then the next), demographic (Marlboro won college campuses before going nationwide), chronologic (Pepsi built "the Pepsi Generation" among the young, then aged with them), and distribution (Wella built through beauty salons before drugstores/supermarkets); and **global outlook** (a position owned in one country can be a wedge into another — IBM earned, per the source, roughly 60% of the German computer market and over half its total profit outside the US — but expanding internationally can expose a name problem, as when U.S. Rubber, selling many non-rubber products worldwide, became Uniroyal). The book's closing first rule of positioning, repeated as its central thesis: never compete head-to-head against an established position — go around, under, or over it, never straight at it, because the leader owns the high ground. ## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Map your category's product ladder
1. State the category the way a customer would (their word for it, not your internal taxonomy).
2. Ask target prospects, unprompted, to name every brand in that category, in the order they'd rank them. [d6bb004cc9ea61609a6119d3]
3. Record the resulting ladder: who is on top, second, third, and who is missing entirely.
4. Decide your message from your rung: if you are #2 or lower, do not advertise as if you were the leader; if you are unranked, you need either a genuinely new ladder or an "against" position. [d6bb004cc9ea61609a6119d3] [ff2d9fce9f885510166869db]
5. Re-run the same question periodically to see whether your positioning work is moving the ladder or only raising awareness.
The source gives no sample size or survey design; it treats this as directional research, not statistically governed. `[needs brand data: real prospect interviews or search-term data per category]`

### 3.2 The six-question positioning audit (Chapter 24)
1. What position do we already own? Answer from the marketplace (research, reviews, search terms), not from the marketing team. [e247ee27ad80dc32e7426a42]
2. Look at the big picture before answering — the real problem may sit one level up from your product (category share of mind, or a place/context problem, not the product itself).
3. What position do we want to own? Check that no competitor already holds it firmly and that it is not so broad it cannot be defended.
4. Whom must we outgun? Map competitors' owned positions and identify the one open rung.
5. Do we have enough money to establish and then hold the position? If not, shrink the geographic or channel scope rather than underspending everywhere. [67d6adbf55589fcc3535e00a]
6. Can we stick it out for years, varying only tactics, not the core claim?
7. Does our execution (creative, tone, packaging, even how the team presents itself) match the intended position? [4c9bc44b263f2a103dd159ba] [bac208e763af56184e1e4808]
The source gives no scoring method; answering the six questions honestly, in writing, is the entire procedure.

### 3.3 Semantic differential research to find your real position (Long Island Trust method)
1. Select attributes that plausibly matter to the buying decision in your category — do not default to generic ones if the category has little price perception (banking had almost none, so the authors added 2 locally relevant attributes to 4 traditional ones). [fcfff9855adc5037bab95851]
2. Have real prospects score every named competitor (not just you) on each attribute, 1-10.
3. Look for the pattern, not just your average score: you may rank last on the attributes everyone competes on and first on one nobody else can claim.
4. Build the position around the attribute you already win, not the ones you are trying to fix — "accept your strengths" beats "fix your weaknesses" here.
5. Name the competitor's real behavior specifically in the resulting ad (e.g., where their money is actually parked) rather than a vague claim.
6. Re-run the identical research 12-15 months later to confirm the position moved, and report movement per attribute, not just an overall favorability score.
`[needs brand data: attribute list, competitor set, survey instrument, and a research budget]`

### 3.4 Test-market two candidate positioning frames before committing (Mailgram method)
1. Identify at least two plausible mental anchors for a new product or service (Mailgram: the Telegram vs. first-class mail). [9794ccdb926ce12b587b0b87]
2. Write one distinct ad theme per anchor.
3. Select several large, comparable test markets (the source used 6 major US cities over 13 weeks).
4. Measure both volume and unaided/aided awareness before and after in each market — awareness change is the leading indicator, not just short-term volume.
5. Also track the "cannibalized" product's own volume during the test to check whether the feared cannibalization is real.
6. Roll out the winning frame nationally, and hold it. Do not switch frames once it is working, even years later, without expecting a real risk of decline. [c4d605d4c32792d4464c12ca]

### 3.5 Reposition a local/regional business against a national or larger competitor's weakness
1. Study the competitor, not your own product, to find the position (the "Tylenol approach"). [326a3dca9341c8c6cbd452f0] [6355cdae1a6b79393b2e98b4]
2. Target the competitor who is actually in the prospect's mind, even if smaller local rivals exist — positioning deals with what's in the mind, not the full competitive set.
3. Name the leader's structural weakness (in the source's case, large-bank slowness).
4. Before writing any ad, change internal operations so the promise is true; the source lists 7 concrete operational commitments made before advertising ran (decision speed, staff cross-training, technology commitment, service-speed commitments, a symbolic "time is money" reminder for staff).
5. Write creative that dramatizes the specific weakness (fictional "Lethargic National Bank" contrast ads).
6. Track unaided awareness and internal staff behavior change, not only revenue, as an intermediate signal that the position is taking hold.

### 3.6 Decide house name vs. new name for a line extension (5 rules of the road)
1. Score the candidate extension against the 5 criteria: expected volume, competitive intensity, planned advertising support, significance (breakthrough vs. commodity), distribution model. [e192263b85cc676c7aa70c03] [4e20426232bb64bb73fb16c8]
2. Weigh them together, not individually — the source gives no numeric scoring, only the direction of each factor.
3. Run the shopping-list test on the parent name today: if a customer given just the brand name could name exactly one product, the parent name still has a defensible position worth protecting. [c4a8fdae883ca1b301a45699]
4. Run the teeter-totter check: if the new product is genuinely different from what the parent means today, expect the parent's core meaning to weaken as the new one is promoted. [1aaf6ab34b2e66e32595f55a]
5. If proceeding under the house name anyway, plan for and monitor the trade-off explicitly rather than assuming a free ride.
6. If launching under a new name, budget real advertising support for it — a new name with no support behind it will underperform a known name for a low-involvement purchase.

### 3.7 Roll out a position when you cannot launch at full scale
1. Choose one rollout axis where you can own the position exclusively at the lowest cost: geographic (one city/region first), demographic (one dense, reachable segment), chronologic (one age cohort, ridden forward in time), or distribution (one channel before broader retail). [d1ef31a9421aa8530e50aac2]
2. Overspend in the first market/segment/channel rather than underspending broadly — the source's own comparison: dominating New York City as the top scotch brand (the country's largest scotch market at the time) justified a national rollout; a thin national spend would not have. [67d6adbf55589fcc3535e00a]
3. Confirm the position is actually established (via the ladder-mapping or awareness-tracking methods above) before rolling out to the next market or segment.
4. Repeat the identical positioning claim in each new market; do not re-invent the message per market.

### 3.8 Find and hold a "third-leg" extension from your existing position
1. Map your category into its structural "legs" or adjacent jobs-to-be-done (the source's office example: communication, input/processing, output).
2. Identify which leg you already own, and note that attacking a leg a bigger rival owns has historically failed for both sides (Xerox vs. IBM's computer leg; IBM vs. Xerox's copier leg). [97bafe17846bfa7fbe771de2]
3. Ask what your own leg is *becoming* as technology or customer behavior shifts, rather than assuming it stays static.
4. Borrow credibility from a technology or trend already proven elsewhere and attach it to your leg with a name that echoes your existing position (the source's own proposal, "lasography," was never adopted — treat the naming move as a template, not a guaranteed outcome).

## 7. Examples and case results

All era-specific (roughly 1960s-1980), reported by the authors themselves with no independent citation unless noted. Treat every number as an illustration of a mechanism, not a benchmark to replicate.

- **Avis vs. Hertz**, rent-a-cars, ~1962-63 campaign: "We're only No. 2, so we try harder." Turned 13 years of losses into $1.2M, $2.6M, $5M profit in 3 successive years, then sold to ITT. Later F.W.M.T.S. failure when Avis ran "Avis is going to be No.1." [4f50a6f783d79bdabf187a08] [a3b068db34cdc3d4b13ae8ee]
- **7-Up "the uncola,"** soft drinks, positioned against the cola category as a whole rather than as a lemon-lime variant. [078eeee4243ee14c063212a2]
- **RCA vs. IBM**, computers, 1969-71: predicted and then documented ~$250M failure after declaring intent to take a "firm No. 2" computer position; GE exited the same business to Honeywell in the same period. [d68f5389fbe9fd8aa4f252f6]
- **Miller Lite**, beer, late 1970s: "Lite beer from Miller" succeeded so well the name became generic, costing Miller exclusive trademark rights to "light" for beer. [5fffd494c3af50fc9a360416]
- **Tylenol vs. aspirin**, analgesics: ~60 words on aspirin's risks before naming Tylenol; became the #1 analgesic brand. [4e43c308641d205d16badcd1]
- **Volkswagen "Think small,"** small cars, then extension into buses/jeeps/bigger cars: fell from 1st to 4th among US imports (behind Toyota, Datsun, Honda). [7cbf151d9951531fb508dfaa] [c3e977f3ebfa33eb3cc17bef]
- **Xerox Data Systems**, computers, 1970s: ~$1B acquisition of Scientific Data Systems, renamed, folded with an additional $84.4M write-off. [3dc57a790b44752be812e919]
- **Heinz vs. Vlasic**: Heinz became #1 in ketchup by making "Heinz" mean ketchup, and lost pickle leadership to Vlasic in the same move (teeter-totter). [3dc57a790b44752be812e919]
- **Bayer / Protein 21 / Pall Mall / Scott**, packaged goods line-extension failures with reported share erosion, detailed in 2.17 above. [322fbe17b694f5b2a5763339] [8a909a51fd898428f27e5648] [6b536db78f7b5b53a528bb19]
- **Milk Duds**, candy: repositioned as "the long-lasting alternative to the candy bar" against Hershey's et al.; reversed a sales decline to record volume. [babb616ba8a918fd717183cf]
- **Mailgram**, Western Union, ~1970s: 6-city, 13-week test proved "low-cost Telegram" framing beat "high-speed letter" framing on awareness (23%→47% vs. 27%→25%); volume declined years later after switching to "next-day delivery." [9794ccdb926ce12b587b0b87] [c4d605d4c32792d4464c12ca]
- **Long Island Trust Company**, banking: semantic-differential research found it rated last on 4 traditional attributes, first on 2 local ones; the "Long Island position" campaign moved every measured attribute up over 15 months. [fcfff9855adc5037bab95851]
- **United Jersey**, banking: "fast-moving bank" position backed by 7 real operational changes; reported unaided awareness nearly tripled and earnings rose 26% in the following year. [6355cdae1a6b79393b2e98b4]
- **Sabena / Belgium**, tourism: repositioned the country (not the airline) using the Michelin Guide's 5 three-star Belgian cities vs. Amsterdam's 1; generated a large inquiry response before the program was abandoned by incoming management. [6b46650fd04c67f1d8028d1c] [f0d81e00c9a55aef8eef36b1]
- **Jamaica**, tourism: positioned as "the Hawaii of the Caribbean" using verifiable comparisons (highest peak, largest land area among competing islands); outcome left unresolved in the source, attributed to political inertia. [7a5f6e23a28dcc13fb520626]
- **Stowe, Vermont**, ski resort: borrowed Harper's Bazaar's "top 10 ski resorts in the world" list (Stowe the only Eastern entrant) to justify a longer drive than closer competitors; reported brochure requests and attendance records both rose. [2e309f07feea6f476a03e009]
- **Catholic Church**, post-Vatican II: diagnosed a lost position (Mass attendance below 50% of Catholics, a reported 20% drop; vocations down a reported 60%); proposed "teacher of the word" repositioning was never implemented by Church leadership. [f60fcd1475d34ddf220668ef]
- **Ray Kroc / McDonald's**: bought the McDonald brothers' idea and name for "relatively few dollars" and became one of America's richest people; used as an illustration of "faith in others' ideas" rather than a marketing case per se. [3faac4e5fe42a84f30b8e3aa]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Trying to change what's already in the prospect's mind instead of working with it ("mind-changing is the road to advertising disaster"). [079c0cc5fa06950033c13a1c]
2. Attacking a category leader head-on instead of going around, under, or over their position. [78e52b831776674a9084e900] [d68f5389fbe9fd8aa4f252f6]
3. Confusing a factory gap (a hole in your own product line) with a real gap in the prospect's mind. [47ce0c7f290e44b91b9b3995]
4. Trying to be all things to all people (the everybody trap), especially when building a new position from nothing. [32142fdf58930bd258189588] [8bc6ea16e3c6ef20e8906f7f]
5. Running "we're better than X, Y, Z" comparative ads instead of genuinely repositioning the competitor's meaning. [a57fe56ea376d37a21937b1e]
6. Adopting initials or acronyms before the underlying name is already famous. [868f7e43ad95961d44c7f71a] [dca1b04ea88b10fa605de960]
7. Assuming a famous name gives a new, different product a "free ride." [46df3117bf9a3ac4930e3678]
8. Line-extending a strong name onto a distinctly different product (the teeter-totter principle) and mistaking early trade-stocking sales for real demand. [ad5c24b3c95342018b0280f2] [3c603945dca9ef67099e4e14]
9. Hiding a product's true nature or origin behind a euphemistic name, inviting suspicion instead of building "pride of origin." [9eb8f61cd2ab8eb7b5806b9b]
10. Abandoning a working position for an aspirational or "novel" campaign (the F.W.M.T.S. trap). [a3b068db34cdc3d4b13ae8ee]
11. Building a corporate position on "diversification" or "our people" instead of one specific, ownable achievement. [dc9410d03a81c7a42d5ecb80]
12. Reading your own advertising too admiringly and assuming marketing skill, rather than the owned position, caused past success — then trying to transfer that "skill" to unrelated categories (Xerox in computers, IBM in copiers). [93ec091be57a59b3f3fe9d49]
13. Underspending across many markets instead of overspending in one and rolling out from strength. [67d6adbf55589fcc3535e00a]
14. Switching a working strategy for a fresher-sounding one without a compelling reason (Mailgram's late switch to "next-day delivery" preceded a volume decline). [c4d605d4c32792d4464c12ca]
15. Letting clever creative execution obscure or override the underlying strategy. [bac208e763af56184e1e4808]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- The book is the authors' own doctrine, sold through their own agency's case studies; none of the "before/after" numbers (Avis profits, United Jersey earnings, Long Island Trust research) are independently sourced or audited in the text. Treat them as the authors' account of their own work.
- Several of the book's strongest claims are explicitly presented as absolute rules ("second is nowhere," "line extension seldom works," "never compete head-on") that are contradicted by many real, durable No. 2/No. 3 brands and by successful modern brand extensions. The book itself concedes exceptions in places (GE for line extension, Vaseline Intensive Care, Del Monte's multi-vegetable line) — the exceptions are as instructive as the rule.
- The book gives almost no "how": no budgets, no sample sizes for research, no digital-era channel guidance, no timeline benchmarks beyond a few specific case durations (13 weeks for Mailgram, 15 months for Long Island Trust). It is a strategy lens, not an execution manual.
- All case studies are 40+ years old, from a three-network, pre-internet, pre-search, pre-social US media environment. Ad-spend figures, market-share numbers, and even legal/regulatory context (e.g., the 1970s FTC history behind comparative advertising) are historical background, not current fact.
- The book does not address digital-era mechanisms at all: search intent, SEO, marketplace algorithms, review platforms, influencer/UGC, or paid social auction dynamics. Anywhere this playbook maps a book concept onto "PDP," "ad copy," or "landing page," that mapping is this playbook's own inference, not the book's.
- Internal tension: the book insists a strong position requires narrowing (the everybody trap, sacrifice) while also insisting a position must not be "too narrow" to sustain a business (subtlety, Rolls-Royce thinking, section 2.30). No formula is given for finding the exact width; both principles are stated as true and left for the reader to balance case by case.
- The book gives no method for verifying that a "mind creneau" is real before spending against it, beyond "check with customer language" — modern search-term data, review mining, and social listening are natural substitutes the book could not have anticipated. `[needs brand data: any positioning claim below must be checked against the brand's own customer language, not assumed from the book's era examples]`

### Ecommerce adaptation (this is this playbook's own adaptation, not the book's claim)

The book never discusses ecommerce. The mappings below are inferences for this operating context, not statements from Ries and Trout.

- **Positioning statement**: the book's "own one clear rung on the category ladder" maps to a store or product's positioning statement: one sentence naming the category, the target customer, the single claim, and (where relevant) the leader you are positioned against or the mind creneau you occupy. Write it before PDP copy or ad hooks, not after. `[needs brand data: real, unprompted category ladder from the brand's own customers]`
- **Naming**: the book's naming rules (benefit-carrying, sayable, not-quite-generic, checked aloud) transfer directly to product titles, SKU names, and collection names in a storefront and in search results, where a shopper reads a name with no salesperson to explain it. The "shopping-list test" and "bartender test" translate to: does a customer searching your brand name land on one clear product, and does voice search or a friend's recommendation ("get me the [brand]") resolve unambiguously?
- **Line-extension risk in DTC**: the book's teeter-totter principle is a direct warning against a DTC brand's most common growth move — stretching a hero SKU's name across an unrelated new category (a supplement brand's skincare line, a coffee brand's apparel drop). The diagnostic to run before launch is the same shopping-list test: can a customer still say what your brand name means after the new SKU ships?
- **Category creation**: the book's "new product needs a new ladder, explained against the old" principle maps to how a genuinely novel product should be framed on a landing page or PDP — anchor the new format or model to the nearest thing the shopper already understands ("the [X] without the [downside of X]"), rather than assuming the shopper will build a new mental category from a features list.
- **Repositioning vs. comparison pages**: the book's distinction between comparative advertising ("we're better than X") and true repositioning (changing what the prospect believes about the competitor) maps onto "us vs. them" and ingredient/label comparison pages: a page that only claims superiority reads as the expected sales pitch; a page that states a specific, verifiable fact about the competitor's product (an ingredient, a policy, an origin) does the work the book describes — subject to legal review for any factual claim about a named competitor.
- **New Product, New Ladder, New Name for private label**: a private-label or in-house brand launching a category the parent store has no position in should default to a new brand name rather than the store's own house name, per the book's 5 rules of the road (expected volume, competition, ad support, significance, distribution) — a high-volume, differentiated, competitive-category product earns its own name; a low-volume, commodity, off-the-shelf item can ride the store's house name.
