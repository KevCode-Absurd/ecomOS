## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a menu of about 25 unconventional plays for retention, word of mouth and offer strength that do not rely on price cuts or ad spend: physical and handwritten touches, status and invite-only access, radical guarantees and keep-it refunds, founder-led "things that don't scale", anti-discount pricing, drops, community and co-creation, unboxing surprises, referral waitlists, and brand stunts. Each play has a case, a "why it works", a "how to run it", a cost where given, and a risk. It ends with a 4-stage rollout plan with a benchmark to pass before each expansion. It is a starting menu and a sequencing plan, not a tested method. Out of scope per the source: standard paid media and ROAS (06-MEA), SEO and AEO (15-SEO, 16-AEO).

## 2. Principles

1. **Do things that don't scale.** Treat manual, high-touch effort as a marketing investment, not an operating cost. Source calls Paul Graham's 2013 essay "the backbone of this whole folder". The claim is that algorithms and large competitors will not copy manual, human effort, so it is defensible. Condition: early stage, or high-value segments later; know when to "graduate a play to a system". Evidence: startup anecdotes (Airbnb, Stripe, Pinterest, Viaweb, Zappos), none ecommerce-controlled. [aa796ddf08a2]
2. **Best wild bets are cheap to run and hard to copy.** Source groups them as personal gestures, status plays, risk reversal and stunts. Condition: source opinion drawn from the case list. [24e7dd3a3012]
3. **Reserve the wildest bets for the top 1% of customers.** Source says the top 1% "drive most of your revenue". That share is stated, not shown. `[needs brand data: revenue share of top 1% and top 5% of customers by lifetime spend]` [8fc70ea68011] [2984f2673be5]
4. **Personal, physical gestures beat discounts for loyalty.** Mechanism given: reciprocity and "the feeling of being seen". Evidence: Chewy credits portraits, handwritten cards and sympathy flowers for customer-for-life loyalty (company claim). [e29188eb0294] [42d04b3223ba]
5. **Surprise only works while it is a surprise.** "The randomness is the point." Do not announce the gesture or make it a standard perk. It fades if routine or expected. [e29188eb0294] [63f18883754c] [24e7dd3a3012]
6. **Lasting value comes from the policy, not the one-off story.** A Joshie-style gesture has limited lasting impact unless an everyday policy (the Ritz-Carlton $2,000 Rule) stands behind it. [7ae163d5a82f] [ad5810cd8a68]
7. **Status and scarcity can replace discounts.** "Not everyone gets in" pulls harder than money off, and status rewards cost less. Condition: gating shrinks the funnel, so the reward inside must be worth the friction. Evidence: CRED and Harry's (reported figures). [11a4bf90d117] [8fc70ea68011]
8. **Risk reversal converts and can cut cost.** Lifetime warranties remove the "will it last?" objection. Keep-it refunds remove return hassle and skip reverse-logistics cost. Condition: product quality must be high and abuse controlled. [04f54549fb37] [1a706b82583a]
9. **Heavy discounting signals weakness.** Source cites Ritson, Binet and Field, and Ehrenberg-Bass (via a blog and Kantar): heavy discounting disillusions loyal customers and raises price sensitivity. Use bundles, gifts or price holds instead. Condition (card adaptation): assumes premium positioning or a real quality story; commodity categories may need promotional pricing. [7c689b80e439] [d2474fde6f0e]
10. **Proof beats claims.** Let the customer be the judge with a test in the box. Condition: only if the product truly wins the test. [574b9d94f465]
11. **Belonging makes switching socially costly.** Named communities, rituals and status markers lock customers in. Condition: needs real, ongoing investment and a consistent brand voice; "cannot be faked". [e6ea8581c19b]
12. **Stunts must fit the voice.** Irreverent stunts work for irreverent brands. "Copying the format without the voice usually flops." Liquid Death founder's rule: make things people would watch even if they were not ads. [f3d618345541]
13. **Values plays only work if the values are real.** Patagonia's anti-consumption ad was backed by a repair and resale program. Faked values invite greenwashing accusations. [d2474fde6f0e]
14. **Measure big swings on earned media, not direct sales.** Budget a stunt as a marketing cost. [f3d618345541] [24e7dd3a3012]
15. **Product or access rewards beat cash in referral.** Cash rewards attract fraud; exclusivity beats cash. [f58ea6021437]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Staged rollout (the source's master plan)

Source says: run in stages; each stage has a benchmark that tells you when to move on. [24e7dd3a3012]

1. **Stage 1, this month (low cost, low risk).**
   1. Rank customers by lifetime spend. Take the top 50. `[needs brand data: customer list with lifetime spend]`
   2. Send each a handwritten note or a 60-second founder video. Note cost about $1 to $3 plus time.
   3. Turn on keep-it refunds for items under $20.
   4. Hold out a control group (source names it but does not say how to pick it or how big). `[needs brand data: matched control group of similar customers]`
   5. Benchmark: if repeat-purchase rate or 90-day reorder rate of the top-50 group rises versus control, expand the touch to the top 200. `[needs brand data: repeat-purchase and 90-day reorder rate by cohort]`
2. **Stage 2, next 90 days (build status).**
   1. Launch a named, invite-only VIP tier for the top 1% of customers.
   2. Benefits: early access to drops, a direct founder channel.
   3. Add a surprise insert (sticker plus note) to every VIP order.
   4. Benchmark: if VIP retention and AOV beat non-VIP, formalize the tier and add a second tier below it. `[needs brand data: retention and AOV by tier]`
3. **Stage 3, next 6 months (demand engine).**
   1. Run a tiered referral waitlist for the next launch with product rewards, not cash.
   2. Test a limited drop on an SMS list. `[needs brand data: SMS list size and consent status]`
   3. Benchmark: if referral signups top 30% of total signups and drops sell through at full price, make drops a regular cadence.
4. **Stage 4, ongoing (one big swing).**
   1. Plan 1 stunt or values play that fits the brand voice: an anti-discount gesture, a proof-in-the-box test, or a pay-to-use-it stunt.
   2. Budget it as a marketing cost. Measure earned media, not direct sales.
   3. Benchmark: if it earns coverage or shares worth more than the spend, do 1 or 2 a year, no more. (Cards say "per season"; see section 9.)
5. **Adjust the plan:**
   - Thin margins: delay lifetime warranties; keep price locks narrow. `[needs brand data: gross margin by SKU]`
   - Product does not win a head-to-head test: skip proof-in-the-box.
   - Serious brand voice: skip punk stunts; lean on hospitality and status plays.

### 3.2 Handwritten notes and direct mail

1. Start with the top 50 to 100 customers by lifetime spend, not the whole list. [42d04b3223ba]
2. Target repeat buyers and high-value orders. Card adaptation: skip first-time buyers with no shown affinity. [42d04b3223ba]
3. For a yearly touch: send an annual or anniversary letter to repeat buyers with a VIP offer or club invite. Cost $1 to $5 per piece. [42d04b3223ba]
4. Include a private offer code (or a perforated tear-off referral insert) to measure response. Compare against a control group. [42d04b3223ba]
5. When volume grows, use a handwriting service (LettrLabs or similar). Keep it genuine; printed notes that pretend to be handwritten feel fake. [42d04b3223ba]

### 3.3 Personalized art and emotional gestures (the Chewy play)

1. Pick a personal artifact your product touches: a pet, a home, a car, a baby, a hobby item. [e29188eb0294] [63f18883754c]
2. Each week pick a small set of loyal customers: repeat buyers with an emotional tie to the category.
3. Commission cheap custom art or a personal physical gift. Cost $20 to $40 per piece.
4. Do not announce it. No purchase required.
5. For hard moments (Chewy: a pet dies), send a sympathy card or flowers, and tell the customer to keep or donate unopened product instead of returning it.
6. Card adaptation: track whether recipients post, share or buy again before scaling. `[needs brand data: repeat rate of recipients vs non-recipients]`

### 3.4 No-approval rep budget (the Ritz-Carlton rule, scaled down)

1. Give every support rep a standing no-approval budget, source example $50 to $200 per incident (Ritz-Carlton: up to $2,000 per incident, not per year). [7ae163d5a82f] [ad5810cd8a68]
2. Use it when a customer has a problem or a personal moment: solve the problem, then add a surprise.
3. Train reps to catch small cues in tickets, chats and calls and act on them with a tiny surprise (the $2 hot dog). [ad5810cd8a68]
4. Treat the spend as an investment against estimated customer lifetime value, not an operating cost. Card caveat: size the budget to your margins and AOV, not the Ritz-Carlton figure. `[needs brand data: customer LTV, AOV, gross margin]`
5. Make it a standing, everyday policy, not a one-off stunt.
Source is thin on how: no guidance on logging, abuse limits or reviewing rep spend.

### 3.5 Invite-only access and VIP club

1. Choose what to gate: a product tier, an early drop, or a club. Gate by application or spend threshold. [11a4bf90d117]
2. Rank customers by lifetime spend; invite the top 1% to a named club. [8fc70ea68011] [2984f2673be5]
3. Benefits: first access to drops, exclusive products, events, a direct line to the founder. [2984f2673be5]
4. Reward non-purchase actions too (The Body Shop "Love Your Body": reviews earn gifts and event invites). Card adaptation: tier by lifetime spend, let engagement drive movement within tiers. [6cb0a48f51d6]
5. For early access, use an invite-only waiting room or single-use link or queue fast-pass so members, not bots or resellers, get in. `[verify UI: waiting room and single-use link setup in Queue-it or the store platform]` [11a4bf90d117]
6. Send a 60-second founder video to new VIPs or after a big order. [2984f2673be5]
7. Add a surprise insert (sticker plus note) to every VIP order. [2984f2673be5]
8. Keep benefits clearly tied to loyalty so they do not look unfair if they leak. [8fc70ea68011]
9. Benchmark VIP retention and AOV against non-VIP before adding a second tier. [8fc70ea68011] [6cb0a48f51d6]

### 3.6 Lifetime warranty or repair for life

1. Start with your most durable SKUs only. `[needs brand data: defect and failure rates by SKU]` [04f54549fb37]
2. Prefer repair-first over replace-always (Patagonia Worn Wear); repair-first cuts abuse.
3. Publish a clear, friendly claims page.
4. Card adaptation: model expected claims, repair logistics and cost before promising; define scope and exclusions; verify current terms of cited brands instead of copying a "forever, any reason" claim. `[web check: current Osprey All Mighty Guarantee and Darn Tough terms]` `[web check: warranty and guarantee rules in the selling market]`
5. Thin margins: delay this play.

### 3.7 Keep-it (returnless) refunds

1. Set a price threshold. Source example: refund without return under $20. [1a706b82583a]
2. Target damaged, perishable or hard-to-resell goods.
3. Do not advertise it as the default policy. Card adaptation: handle it case by case. Reason: about 9% of returns are fraudulent (NRF/Happy Returns).
4. Compare repeat-purchase or 90-day reorder rate against a control group before raising the threshold.
Source is thin on how: no break-even formula (return shipping plus handling versus recoverable resale value), no fraud rules. `[needs brand data: cost per return, resale recovery rate, return reasons by SKU]`

### 3.8 Outcome guarantee (the Hormozi idea)

1. Identify a specific, measurable outcome the product produces. [3a74574fff82]
2. Promise that outcome with a clear remedy if it fails. Make terms specific; vague promises invite disputes.
3. Card adaptation: offer a product-appropriate remedy for a substantiated claim. The "keep working until you get the result" form comes from services and should not become an unsupported physical-product guarantee.
4. Track refund rate after launch before scaling. `[needs brand data: refund rate before and after]`

### 3.9 Founder-led, unscalable touches

1. Personally onboard or thank first or best customers. Call them. Fix their problems yourself. [aa796ddf08a2]
2. Send a 60-second, Loom-style founder video to new VIPs or after a big order. Cost is time only.
3. Treat long, untimed support as marketing spend (Zappos).
4. Graduate a play into a system once it outgrows manual effort. Source does not say when.

### 3.10 Price lock for loyal subscribers

1. Grandfather early or subscriber pricing for as long as the subscriber stays. [36d5dbe4d17c]
2. Ring-fence who qualifies. Market it clearly as a loyalty benefit.
3. Alternative: hold prices during inflation and frame the hold as a promotion without a discount (Old Navy, Bark). [7c689b80e439]
4. Card adaptation: model future cost increases before promising; test on an eligible cohort. `[web check: whether BarkBox still honors the lock]` `[needs brand data: cost trend per unit]`

### 3.11 Anti-discount swap and anti-consumption message

1. Replace a planned sale with a gift-with-purchase or a bundle. [7c689b80e439] [d2474fde6f0e]
2. For a values play, pair a genuine sustainability or quality message with a repair or resale program. Do not fake it. [d2474fde6f0e]

### 3.12 Limited drops and drop cadence

1. Release small, numbered batches on a set schedule to an SMS list (MSCHF: set Tuesdays). [f42a03d72af2]
2. Tell insiders (VIP tier or SMS list) the day before.
3. Gate access with a waiting room or single-use links so resellers do not capture the value.
4. Sell at full price.
5. For a habit loop, release new SKUs or bundles on a frequent, predictable cadence and announce it in advance. Needs a fast supply chain. [b7edaaaeed23]
6. Card adaptation: pair a fast cadence with a drop mechanic so novelty does not dilute full-price sales. Keep the cadence only if drop frequency tracks with repeat visits or purchase frequency; otherwise cut back and make fewer, better drops. [b7edaaaeed23] `[needs brand data: visit and purchase frequency by drop week]`

### 3.13 Named community and unboxing ritual

1. Name the community. Add lingo and hashtags that fit a consistent brand voice. [e6ea8581c19b]
2. Set up local chapters or online groups and rituals with status markers (badges, tiers, patches, pins).
3. Put a sticker and a welcome note inviting buyers into the community in every box (boAt).
4. Run UGC contests.
5. Plan ongoing programming, not a one-time launch.

### 3.14 Co-creation (submit and vote)

1. Put up a submit-and-vote page for new products or flavors. [6fd8b0b7de34]
2. Seed it with your existing community or VIP tier; it needs enough voters.
3. Give winners credit and a cut (LEGO Ideas: about 1% royalty of net sales if it ships).
4. State publicly what can and cannot ship.
5. Feed results into real product decisions so it does not feel performative.

### 3.15 Surprise inserts

1. Add a low-cost surprise (sticker, sample, small gift) to first orders or VIP orders. Cost cents to a few dollars. [9a0245f0095a]
2. Pair it with a handwritten thank-you note.
3. Make it personal; generic filler adds cost without delight.
4. Card adaptation: tie the insert to a measurable action: a referral code, VIP invite, or tracked reorder offer.

### 3.16 Proof in the box

1. Pick 1 product attribute you can prove and that wins against rivals. [574b9d94f465]
2. Put a customer-run test in the first box. Frame it as proof, not slogans.
3. Card adaptation: validate what the test measures, its limits and instructions. Exclude tests customers cannot run safely or read reliably. Do not present a selective comparison as full proof of safety or superiority.
4. Measure conversion or repeat rate against a control group before scaling.

### 3.17 Tiered referral waitlist

1. Gate a launch or drop behind a waitlist. [f58ea6021437]
2. Give each person a unique link.
3. Set escalating, reachable product rewards (Harry's: 5 referrals = shave cream, 10 = razor, and so on).
4. Show a live progress bar toward the next reward.
5. Use product or access rewards, not cash.
6. Benchmark: referral signups above 30% of total means formalize the mechanic for future launches.
Source is thin on how: no tool named for the waitlist page, no fraud filtering steps, though Harry's counted only over 85,000 of its 100,000+ signups as legitimate.

### 3.18 Brand stunt

1. Build a strong, specific brand voice first. [f3d618345541]
2. Pick a format: entertaining content people would watch anyway (Liquid Death), a stunt that dramatizes the product benefit (Wakefit), or an anti-promotional gesture on a retail holiday (Cards Against Humanity).
3. Scaled-down options the source gives: pay a few customers to test and document the product publicly; a deliberately anti-promotional gesture that fits the brand's humor.
4. Budget as marketing cost; measure earned media.
5. Serious voice: skip punk stunts.
Source is thin on how: no method for valuing earned media or shares against spend. `[needs brand data: method to value coverage and shares]`

## 7. Examples and case results

All are examples reported by secondary sources, not promises. Several are brand-reported or unaudited.

- **Chewy:** 1,000+ free pet oil paintings a week to select customers since 2013, no purchase needed, no announcement. Handwritten notes to new customers, holiday cards to all shoppers; once bought out its local post office's stamps, now preorders postage. Sympathy flowers when a pet dies; "keep or donate" unopened food. [e29188eb0294]
- **The Drinks List (small wine seller):** handwritten note in every order. [42d04b3223ba]
- **Ritz-Carlton Joshie:** lost toy giraffe returned with a photo binder of Joshie "on vacation". $2,000 Rule (1983, Horst Schulze), against about $250,000 guest LTV. [7ae163d5a82f]
- **Eleven Madison Park:** $2 street hot dog plated Michelin-style became the most talked-about dish and helped it reach number 1 in the world. [ad5810cd8a68]
- **CRED:** invite-only, credit score 750+, 13 million+ monthly active users (reported). [11a4bf90d117]
- **The Body Shop "Love Your Body":** rewards reviews with gifts and event invites. [6cb0a48f51d6]
- **The North Face, Rapha:** invite-only waiting rooms for drops. [11a4bf90d117]
- **Lifetime cover:** Osprey All Mighty Guarantee, Darn Tough, Patagonia Worn Wear, Gerber, Zippo, Le Creuset. LL Bean and Eddie Bauer pulled back generous guarantees. [04f54549fb37]
- **Keep-it refunds:** Amazon since 2017; Chewy, Walmart, Target, Glossier. O Positiv (supplements, 4 million+ customers): 30-day money-back with no return required; says it helped them scale faster. [1a706b82583a]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Running standard tactics (10% off, BOGO, free-shipping thresholds, points programs, abandoned-cart emails) and calling them wild bets. Skip them "unless there is a genuine twist". [24e7dd3a3012]
- Printed notes made to look handwritten. They feel fake. [42d04b3223ba] [9a0245f0095a]
- Sending costly mail to the whole list. Cost scales with list size; keep it to proven repeat buyers. [42d04b3223ba]
- Making the surprise routine or announced. It stops being a surprise and fades. [e29188eb0294]
- Using emotional art in a purely functional category. No hook, no share. [63f18883754c]
- One-off heroics with no standing policy behind them. [7ae163d5a82f]
- Gating without a reward worth the friction. [11a4bf90d117]
- Letting VIP benefits look arbitrary; perceived unfairness if they leak. [8fc70ea68011]
- Weak "secret link" setups that bots and resellers abuse. [11a4bf90d117]
- Lifetime guarantees on products that do not last. Claims "will bury you". [04f54549fb37]
- Advertising keep-it refunds as default. Invites abuse. [1a706b82583a]
- Vague outcome guarantees. They invite disputes. [3a74574fff82]
- Broad price locks on thin margins. [36d5dbe4d17c]
- Faked values in an anti-consumption play. Greenwashing accusations. [d2474fde6f0e]
- Drops with supply so low it frustrates real fans, or with no gating so resellers win. [f42a03d72af2]
- A fast drop cadence without the supply chain to back it. [b7edaaaeed23]
- Forced lingo with an inconsistent brand voice; a community with no real investment. [e6ea8581c19b]
- Co-creation with too small an audience, or promising designs will ship. [6fd8b0b7de34]
- Generic filler inserts. Cost without delight. [9a0245f0095a]
- Proof-in-the-box when the product does not truly win. [574b9d94f465]
- Cash referral rewards. They attract fraud. [f58ea6021437]
- Copying an edgy stunt format without the voice; more than 1 or 2 stunts a year. [f3d618345541]
- Judging a stunt on direct sales instead of earned media. [f3d618345541]
