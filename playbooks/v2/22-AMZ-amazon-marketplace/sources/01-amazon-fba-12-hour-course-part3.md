## 1. What this source is for

This part of the course helps an operator do two things: (1) build a realistic, itemized budget for starting an Amazon FBA business under different models (private label, wholesale, retail arbitrage, or an original "passion product"), including every named fee and startup cost category the presenter could think of; and (2) see that budget and a full first-year (or partial-year) P&L played out concretely across seven different real (as claimed) launches — including two that ended in the business shutting down — so an operator can compare their own numbers against a spread of realistic outcomes rather than a single idealized case. It is weaker as a technical how-to (keyword research mechanics, PPC setup, listing optimization) than parts 1-2 presumably are; this part leans hard into cost modeling, case studies, and motivational/sales framing.

## 2. Principles

1. **Budget in thirds.** Rule of thumb for a private-label product: roughly 33% of revenue to product cost, 33% to Amazon fees, 33% profit. Condition: presenter's general planning heuristic, not derived from a specific dataset; use as a rough sanity check, not a hard target. Evidence: his own product's 3.5-year totals ($1.2M sales; $172k referral fees; $150k+ pick & pack; $23k other fees; $319k product cost; $30k other business expenses; $380k+ profit) are roughly, not exactly, in thirds. `[example]` `[f9ed217597]` `[32df6696fa]`
2. **FBA over FBM, almost always.** Condition: applies once a seller has committed to selling on Amazon at all. Evidence: presenter's own product and multiple guests report 4-5x sales increases switching from self-fulfillment (FBM) to FBA, because FBA makes a listing Prime-eligible and Amazon's search algorithm favors Prime-eligible listings. Also: for one guest (Cam), FBA fees turned out to be cost-neutral or cheaper than postage he'd have paid doing it himself. `[example]` `[a4c6fdf106]` `[32df6696fa]`
3. **Pre-launch audience ("tribe") building drives launch-week velocity, which Amazon's ranking algorithm rewards.** Condition: works best for genuinely novel products where there's a real story to build anticipation around; weaker for undifferentiated private label. Evidence: appears independently across at least 5 of this part's case studies (Travis's own product, Carnivore Electrolytes, Connor, Juliana, Jeremy via Kickstarter backers/influencers) with presenter's best personal result being ~75 reviews in the first week. `[463691efbb]` `[f7ca6bfbed]` `[7f7fc935de]` `[5b52f3c9f1]` `[c5fd5ed5d1]`
4. **Being first-to-market with a genuinely new product beats out-competing an existing category.** Condition: applies to "passion products" (something that does not yet exist) more than commodity private label. Evidence: presenter's keto-nut-butter product grew, not shrank, when competitors entered (Y1 ~$300-360k sales/~$120k profit → Y2 ~$400k sales/~$150k profit — figures repeated with minor variance in this transcript, `[DATA QUALITY]` flag). Presenter's stated reasoning: first-movers "grow the pie" rather than lose share. `[example]` `[56fc5dcf9e]` `[58de052ddc]`
5. **Order enough inventory to avoid stockouts, but don't front-load all of it speculatively.** Condition: two competing pieces of guidance from within this same source that are in real tension — see section 9 (Caveats/Conflicts). General default given repeatedly: ~3-6 months of inventory, closer to 6 for anything perishable/seasonal-risk. Counter-lesson from a failure case (Cam): sending 100% of a large order to Amazon immediately, before validating sell-through, led to long-term storage fees. `[1b37755b2e]` `[24ca880132]` `[59201e11d9]` `[dd7da612975]`
6. **Price can be used as a deliberate inventory-throttle, not just a margin lever.** Condition: when a product is selling faster than supply chain can restock. Evidence: Carnivore Electrolytes case raised price twice ($24.99→$29.99→$34.99) specifically to slow sales pace, and "sales stayed the same" both times, increasing profit; Juliana's sports-bra case did the same after month 3. `[example, 2 cases]`
7. **Track PPC's net effect on profit, not just on sales.** Condition: always, but especially once a listing has organic momentum. Evidence: Carnivore Electrolytes case turned PPC off in month 6 as a deliberate test — sales dipped slightly, profit rose. Cynthia's failure case shows the opposite risk: PPC overspend without measurement drove most of her net losses. `[abf98f0a62]` `[cbf54bbe6b]` `[e507ae86a8]`
8. **Starting the business earlier is presented as the single highest-leverage decision, more than picking the "perfect" product or supplier.** Condition: presenter's own framing / opinion, restated as the "biggest mistake" in nearly every one of this part's case studies (Travis himself, Connor, Juliana, Daryl & Amanda). One case (Jeremy) shows this can also be supply-chain-driven delay, not just founder hesitation — the fix (finding a faster/more reliable manufacturer) differs from the fix for pure analysis paralysis. `[b35c0005507]` `[22226777874]`

### 3.3 Launching with $0 (7 tactics, in the order given)
1. Sell personal items you already own for quick capital.
2. Retail or online arbitrage: buy discounted/clearance items, resell on Amazon at a markup.
3. Cut personal living costs temporarily (e.g. cheaper rent) to free up savings.
4. Take a second job specifically chosen to build Amazon-relevant skills (e.g. help a local shop start selling on Amazon for a fee/percentage), rather than an unrelated job.
5. Find an individual investor for your product idea (friend, family, or — per presenter — his own course community) in exchange for repayment plus a share of ongoing profit.
6. Take a loan (family/friends, bank, or online lender) — presenter's explicit caveat: "don't recommend this in most situations."
7. Crowdfund via Kickstarter or Indiegogo — described as the most powerful of the seven: backers pre-pay, funds cover the first production run, product ships to backers after manufacturing. `[bd7b6cb000]` `[5c218c90e4]` `[cfd113d1ee]` `[482f30b7ca]` `[555c1a9c93]`

### 3.4 Finding a manufacturer (4 methods, repeated across multiple case studies)
1. Alibaba.com — largest pool, mostly Asia/Europe/India; filter to "Verified Supplier" (Alibaba has physically inspected the factory) `[verify UI: current verification badge meaning]`; use Trade Assurance where offered. `[2be091c6eb]` `[516fc94151]`
2. ThomasNet.com — for US-based manufacturers specifically.
3. Google search: "[product] + manufacturer" or "+ private labeler."
4. Personal network / course community referral — fastest in practice across the case studies, since a trusted referral skips a lot of vetting.
- **Consumables/ingestibles**: multiple case studies explicitly avoid Chinese sourcing for consumable products (electrolyte powder, caffeinated gum) and use US-based or Google-sourced manufacturers instead, without the source fully explaining the regulatory reasoning — treat as a stated practice, not a fully justified rule. `[needs brand data / web check: current FDA/consumables import and labeling requirements if sourcing a consumable overseas]`
- **Vetting steps drawn from the case studies**: prefer simple, transparent per-unit pricing (avoid ambiguous variable-cost quotes); prefer shorter lead times; request/commission a factory audit before large orders, especially in an unfamiliar country (Jeremy walked away from a manufacturer that scored a near-F on audit); consider hiring a local "sourcing agent" who can physically visit factories to confirm they are real (not a middleman) and to check labor conditions (Cynthia's case: a hired sourcer uncovered both a fake "manufacturer" posing as a factory owner, and a second, real factory mistreating workers). `[3aa386e7b6]` `[30cfab7b26]` `[458ab7dcf4]`

### 3.5 Inbound shipment to Amazon (steps as demonstrated)
1. In Seller Central, find the product listing → "Send/Replenish Inventory." `[verify UI: current flow name]`
2. Enter box dimensions and unit counts.
3. Print FBA shipping labels, apply to boxes.
4. Ship via chosen carrier (UPS mentioned) to the assigned Amazon warehouse(s).
5. Allow processing time (observed: roughly 1-2+ weeks) before units are checked in and sellable.
6. For a first shipment from an unfamiliar/overseas manufacturer, consider shipping to yourself first for a personal quality check, or paying for third-party inspection, before sending onward to Amazon.

### 3.6 Creating the Amazon listing (steps as demonstrated)
1. Seller Central → "Add a Product" → "I'm adding a product not sold on Amazon" → select category. `[verify UI: current flow]`
2. Fill in title (front-load the primary keyword, not the brand name, unless the brand is already famous), brand, bullet points (benefits before features), description.
3. Upload images (main image large/clean/minimal white space; product itself as the visual hero, not packaging/logo; secondary images show real use and benefits).
4. Set price and other listing details, publish.
5. Certain categories/keywords can trigger an automatic compliance flag (e.g. a "diet product" false-positive on an electrolyte product) requiring a Certificate of Analysis (COA) before the listing goes live — build buffer time into the launch schedule for this possibility. `[verify UI / web check: current supplement/diet-category compliance triggers]`

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Monthly P&L discipline**: every case study in this part separates revenue from product cost, referral fee, FBA fee, PPC spend, other/misc fees (storage, receiving, etc.), and net profit — an operator should replicate this exact breakdown monthly rather than looking at top-line revenue alone. `[e507ae86a8]` `[7e8e8bfd38]`
- **Stockout risk check**: watch sell-through velocity in Seller Central business reports; if inventory is on pace to run out before a reorder can arrive (factor in manufacturer lead time), rush a reorder immediately — a stockout risks losing sales AND getting deprioritized/"blacklisted" in search. `[6bea9b6ea5]` `[35387918cf]` `[2cca0ae31a]`
- **Long-term storage risk check**: monitor how long specific inventory units have sat in the warehouse (via Seller Central); if approaching the long-term threshold (365 days per the main presenter — but see the unresolved 180-day conflict flagged above, `[web check]`), either sell through faster, request removal/reshipment to reset, or accept the fee is likely cheaper than continuing to hold. `[e62d78f3c8]`
- **PPC eligibility check**: if a listing cannot run Sponsored Products campaigns despite otherwise-normal setup, the product/category or a specific keyword/image may be auto-flagged as "controversial" by Amazon; diagnostic approach used in this source (not guaranteed): strip the listing description entirely first, test; if that doesn't restore eligibility, remove images one at a time to isolate the trigger. `[web check: current Amazon restricted-content ad policies]`
- **PPC profitability check**: don't assume ad spend is generating incremental profit — test turning it off periodically once a listing has organic traction, and compare net profit (not just revenue) before/after, as in the Carnivore Electrolytes case. `[abf98f0a62]` `[4e2dab02af]`
- **Ad-spend attribution check**: verify actual conversion tracking before trusting that off-Amazon ads (e.g. Facebook) are working — Juliana's case found her Facebook ad spend was largely wasted only after implementing real tracking.
- **Fee-overcharge check**: periodically audit Amazon's own billing for storage/FBA fee errors; catch and dispute quickly, since old overcharges may not be reimbursed if reported too late. Consider a fee-auditing tool/service for this (unnamed in this part). `[c4af442b1b]` `[bf7de81b23]`
- **Trademark approval-odds check before filing**: a design mark (logo) is far more likely to be approved than a word mark (product/company name), especially if the name is descriptive/generic — assess this before committing to a rushed filing, since a denied trademark can force a costly rebrand (Juliana's case: est. $100,000+ opportunity cost from a denied, rushed word-mark filing). `[9ea0db787b]`
- **Manufacturer legitimacy check**: confirm an Alibaba "manufacturer" is an actual factory, not a middleman, ideally via a local sourcing agent who can physically visit — do not rely solely on the platform profile or the manufacturer's own claims. `[3aa386e7b6]` `[2be091c6eb]`

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

Deduplicated across all 7 case studies in this part (also see the cross-case pattern summary at the end of section 9):

1. Waiting to start / analysis paralysis — the single most repeated mistake in this entire part, cited by the presenter himself and by Connor, Juliana, and Daryl & Amanda as their single biggest cost item.
2. Selecting a bad or overly generic product (commodity private label vs. a genuine "passion product" gap).
3. Defaulting to FBM instead of FBA.
4. Under- or over-ordering inventory relative to actual demand and manufacturer lead time; specifically, shipping 100% of a large order to Amazon before validating sell-through (Cam's case).
5. Choosing a manufacturer on price/claims alone without vetting: no factory audit, no verification the "manufacturer" is a real factory and not a middleman, no in-person check on labor conditions when sourcing outside familiar regions.
6. Launching with unnecessary product variant complexity (multiple sizes/colors) when a single SKU would do, driving up UPC, listing, and inventory-management overhead (Cam's collar) — note the counter-case where variants are genuinely required by the category (Juliana's apparel sizing) and are not a mistake in that context.
7. Rushing a trademark filing as a word mark without assessing approval odds, risking a costly forced rebrand if denied.
8. Cheap, DIY, or unaccountable-vendor product photography, when photos are one of the highest-leverage conversion assets on the listing.
9. Spending on PPC (or Facebook ads) without real conversion tracking or platform-specific competence, and without ever testing whether turning it off changes net profit.
10. Choosing air shipping by default without comparing cost against sea freight for non-urgent inventory.
11. Not keeping every shipping document (e.g. Bill of Lading) in case Amazon disputes receipt of a pallet.
12. Explicitly ToS-risky review tactics the source itself flags as against the rules even while describing them: asking friends/family/co-workers to buy-and-review, reimbursing influencers via Venmo/PayPal for a "purchase + review," and misusing programs like Amazon Vine by seeding non-target-audience reviewers. `[POLICY FLAG — do not operationalize without explicit legal/brand sign-off]`

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Long-term storage fee threshold conflict**: the main presenter states repeatedly (elsewhere in this source) that the long-term storage fee applies after 365 days; one guest case study (Cam) states 180 days. This is a materially different number and directly affects inventory planning; `[web check — CRITICAL]` before advising a brand on this threshold.
- **Standard storage fee rate inconsistency**: given as both $0.70 and $0.75 per cubic foot per month within this same part.
- **Inventory-batching guidance is internally in tension**: the general default given throughout is "order 3-6 months of inventory up front," but the clearest failure case in this part (Cam) explicitly blames sending a large order to Amazon all at once, before validating sell-through, as one of his three named mistakes. The source does not reconcile these; treat "smaller first batch, then scale once validated" as the more conservative reading for a genuinely new/untested product, and "3-6 months up front" as more applicable once a product has some proven demand signal (e.g. from Helium 10/Jungle Scout data on an existing comparable).
- **UPC and Helium 10 pricing figures vary widely and inconsistently across case studies within this same source** ($20-$250 for UPCs; $40-$595 for Helium 10) — likely reflects real vendor tier/quantity differences, transcription/rounding noise, or dated pricing; do not treat any single figure as current without checking. `[web check]`
- **Several case-study dollar figures do not internally reconcile** (flagged inline as `[DATA QUALITY]` above): Cam's month-1 P&L numbers do not add up arithmetically; Cynthia's manufacturer-mistake cost is cited as both $2,300 and (implicitly, via the narrative) closer to $7,800; some month-by-month revenue figures for the presenter's own keto product differ slightly between mentions. These are likely automatic-speech-recognition transcription artifacts rather than deliberate claims — treat exact case-study dollar amounts as directionally illustrative, not precise.
- **Every case study in this part is also a testimonial for the presenter's paid course** ("Passion Product Formula"). The two failure cases (Cam, Cynthia) are framed to support the same conclusion — join the course / get a mentor — which should be read as the source's selling thesis, not neutral evidence. This does not mean the operational lessons inside those stories (manufacturer vetting, inventory batching, PPC tracking) are wrong, but the causal attribution ("this would have gone better with our course") is unverifiable.
- **Nothing in this part covers deep keyword-research mechanics, PPC campaign structure/settings, or listing-optimization detail at the level parts 1-2 of this source likely do** — those topics are only touched on narratively here (e.g. "use Helium 10 Magnet," "start with automatic PPC") without step-by-step settings. The card index below includes several cards on PPC campaign structure and keyword mining that likely trace to parts 1-2 rather than this transcript segment; cross-check against those parts' mini playbooks during the merge.
- **Unsourced statistics used throughout this part** (seller counts, "43%/40% of sellers make $100k+," "50% of Fortune 500 started in a recession," "66% wish they'd started sooner," "56% of shoppers start on Amazon," shipping cost up "23%") have no citation in the transcript. `[web check: verify or drop before using any of these in client-facing material]`
- **Compliance/legal claims are presenter opinion, not legal advice**: the NNN-vs-NDA claim for Chinese manufacturers, the "you don't need a lawyer for a DIY patent/trademark" framing, and the labor-condition/sourcing-agent guidance are all presented without legal sourcing. `[needs brand data: brand's own legal counsel guidance on entity structure, IP filing strategy, and international labor-sourcing due diligence before treating any of this as actionable]`
