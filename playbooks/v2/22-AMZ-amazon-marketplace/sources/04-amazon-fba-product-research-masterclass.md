## 1. What this source is for

A single YouTube creator's product-screening checklist for Amazon FBA sellers: what to rule out before spending on inventory, what to target instead, and one specific Helium 10 Black Box filter recipe plus a review-mining technique to turn a shortlisted product into a supplier brief. It stops at the point of picking a product to source; it does not cover landed cost/tariff/FBA fee calculation or supplier sourcing (see folder `06-OPS` / Supply Chain & Sourcing), PPC campaign execution (`04-MED` / Paid Media Execution), or listing image/packaging design (`03-CRE` / Performance Ad Creative).

## 2. Principles

1. **Screen out disqualifying products before evaluating upside.** The source frames 4 categories as elimination criteria, not trade-offs: cheap products, competitive products, seasonal/trendy products, and restricted or patented products. Evidence given: personal experience (he says he has sold restricted and patented products and then could not sell the inventory) and reasoning about PPC economics on cheap items. Card [680f24307e2ab558d2196eb2].
2. **Margin and price floor before anything else.** Require 30%+ net profit margin after landed cost (product, shipping, duties, taxes) and FBA fees, and a retail price around $25+. Evidence: his own stated experience that his most profitable items are all over $30, and reasoning that higher ticket price better absorbs PPC and margin pressure. Card [78d7c95b14532e4f24b238d7].
3. **"Dollars follow value": solving a real customer problem drives reviews, credibility and repeat demand**, which the source treats as more important than price competition once margin and price floors are met. No quantified evidence given, stated as opinion. Card [78d7c95b14532e4f24b238d7].
4. **Avoid brand-dominated categories.** If one legacy brand owns most category sales (his example: Adidas/Nike/Puma in soccer balls), a new seller cannot compete; prefer categories where sales are spread across many sellers. No numeric threshold given for "spread out." Card [78d7c95b14532e4f24b238d7].
5. **Keyword variability drives cheap organic ranking.** A product describable by dozens or hundreds of distinct search terms (his example: a phone case as slim, green, wallet, waterproof, for men, for women) gives many low-competition long-tail terms to target in listing copy and PPC; a product with only 1-2 monolithic keywords means one contested battle with no cheap entry point. Card [c7f835842a7659d4160fd222].
6. **Price disparity on page 1 signals room to compete on something other than price.** A tight page-1 price cluster means buyers choose on price and star rating alone (race to the bottom); a wide spread means buyers already pay more for perceived differences, which a new seller can exploit through positioning, bundling or quality. Card [aabcbafc75f4f712b66bf630].
7. **A weak filter stack surfaces weak competitors, not strong products.** The Helium 10 Black Box filters (below) are explicitly designed to find sellers who are under-resourced or unsophisticated (few reviews, few images), not to find the objectively best product. Card [c38799c1757bb3cdc3f8b752].
8. **Mediocre star ratings on a selling product are a spec sheet, not a red flag.** A product rated roughly 4.1-4.3 stars that still does meaningful revenue is proof of demand despite unresolved flaws; the 1-star reviews describe exactly what to fix before you brief a supplier. Card [d25b670164dce4954c0a8424].
9. **Giftability is a tie-breaker, not a qualifying criterion.** A product that works as a gift for multiple holidays and as a self-purchase captures Q4/holiday demand spikes without being purely seasonal, but the source says explicitly it "is not an absolute must." Card [8397ad752638ef02c73fd207].
10. **Pick a revenue band your capital can actually fund, not the biggest number on the page.** Matching a $30k/month product requires roughly 3 months of inventory plus heavy ad spend; a smaller budget should filter to a lower revenue band. He states outright that $1,500-$3,000/month products can still be viable, just smaller. Card [b640794fea47cef56b77b227].

## 3. Procedures

**Procedure: Helium 10 Black Box first-pass product scan** (source's worked example; he tells viewers not to copy these exact values). `[verify UI]` throughout, Helium 10's interface and filter set change over time.

1. Open Helium 10. Go to Tools -> Black Box -> Products tab. (Other Black Box sub-tools mentioned but not used in this walkthrough: Keywords, Competitor, Niche, Product Targeting.)
2. Set Category to one ungated category. His example: Patio Lawn & Garden. He explicitly tells viewers to vary the category so everyone watching does not end up chasing the same products; he also names Home & Kitchen, Industrial & Scientific, Kitchen & Dining, Office Products, Pet Supplies, Sports & Outdoors, and Tools & Home Improvement as other open categories. He names Baby, CDs and Vinyl, and some Electronics (battery-containing items) as categories that "cause you problems" (gating/restrictions). `[web check: current Amazon category gating list, since it changes]`
3. Set Review Count Max = 300, to exclude listings with tens of thousands of reviews (dominant competitors).
4. Set Price. He iterates on air: starts at $20-$40, revises to $25-$40, settles on $25-$60 for the walkthrough, and says to go higher with a bigger budget.
5. Set Monthly Revenue = $5,000 to $20,000 minimum-to-maximum. Reasoning: below ~$5k/month is not "crazy in demand"; above ~$20-30k/month requires a budget most new sellers do not have (3 months of inventory plus heavy marketing spend).
6. Set Listing Age Minimum = 8 months, to filter out fads/trends and find products with enough history to judge demand as evergreen.
7. Set Image Count Max = 4. Sellers with 4 or fewer listing images are, in his words, "clueless" about what they're doing on Amazon, i.e. low-hanging competitors.
8. Run the search. In his walkthrough (Patio Lawn & Garden, the filters above) this returned 500+ matching products.
9. Scroll the results manually for anything that looks interesting; there is no further automated filter in this source beyond what is listed above.

**Procedure: Review mining a shortlisted product into a supplier brief**

1. In the Black Box results list, sort by review rating from lowest to highest.
2. Look for products rated roughly 4.1 to 4.3 stars that are still generating $5,000+/month. (This can be run on results from the scan above, or on any shortlisted product.)
3. Open the product's Amazon listing. Read the 1-star reviews specifically, looking for concrete, recurring physical complaints: fit, size, missing color/size options, durability, missing features.
4. Note the percentage of 1-star reviews as a rough severity signal (his sun-shade example had 12% one-star reviews).
5. Also inspect the listing images for obvious quality gaps (his example: "photoshopped and don't look that great").
6. Translate each recurring complaint into a specific line item in a manufacturing/supplier brief (materials, dimensions, added color/size options) before contacting a supplier.
7. Repeat this same review-mining pass post-launch on your own listing and on competitors, to prioritize the next product iteration.

**Procedure: Page-1 price-disparity check** (manual, no tool named)

1. Search your candidate product's main keyword directly on Amazon.
2. Record the prices of the organic page-1 listings.
3. If prices cluster tightly (his example: a "tongue scraper" search returned $6.99, $7.62, $9.99, $6.99-ish and $12.99, i.e. roughly a $7-$13 band), treat this as a discard signal: the category competes on price and star rating alone.
4. If prices span widely on page 1 (his example: the same type of product appearing at both $20 and $100), treat this as a positive signal: buyers already pay for something besides lowest price, leaving room for positioning, bundling, or quality-based pricing.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Selling cheap ($7-$10) products: invites bad reviews on flimsy goods, low barrier to entry draws heavy competition, and ~$1/click PPC on a low-margin item can wipe profit within 2-3 clicks.
- Selling seasonal/trendy products: by the time the seller launches, the season has passed, demand disappears, and inventory sits and accrues Amazon long-term storage fees.
- Selling restricted or patented products: the presenter says he has personally ordered inventory in a category Amazon later blocked, or that turned out to be patented, and could not sell it.
- Not knowing your own numbers: "so many sellers have no idea what their profit is, they don't know their FBA fees, they don't know their landed cost" — sellers who skip the margin math end up unprofitable without realizing it.
- Competing head-on against a legacy brand that dominates a category (his example: soccer balls vs. Adidas/Nike/Puma).
- Picking a product with only 1-2 keywords describing it, which removes the low-hanging long-tail terms needed for cheap PPC and organic ranking.
- Chasing the highest-revenue number on the page (e.g. a $30k/month product) without the capital for the roughly 3 months of inventory and heavy marketing spend that volume requires.
- Copying his exact Black Box filter values verbatim: he explicitly warns that if every viewer uses the same category and filters, they will all surface the same products and compete with each other.
