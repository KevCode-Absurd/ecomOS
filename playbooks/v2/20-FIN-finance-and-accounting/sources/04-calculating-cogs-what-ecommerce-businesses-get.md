## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator or bookkeeper decide, line by line, whether a cost belongs in COGS (above the gross profit line) or in operating expenses (below it). It gives 1 main test ("all in until it's in"), 1 backup test (would you have paid this cost if you had not bought the goods), and a quick-fire list of 7 transaction types with their treatment. It also covers how to attach lump-sum freight and duty invoices to units so each SKU has a landed cost. It is strong on classification. It is thin on the how of allocation, gives no formulas, and names no allocation basis.

## 2. Principles

1. **You cannot know margin or profit without correct COGS.** The guest says COGS and margin should anchor strategy: which channels you sell on and how you promote. Without it, businesses are "flying blind". Opinion, no data. [a0e16cf2802e]
2. **COGS = every cost to get the product from the supplier's door onto your shelf, ready to sell.** Phrase: "all in until it's in". Costs up to that point are COGS. Costs after that point are expenses. Condition: the guest frames this for a business that buys products in and resells them. For manufacturers "your cost of goods sold is going to have another layer", which the source does not explain. [a0e16cf2802e]
3. **Backup test: would you have paid this cost if you had not bought that item?** If yes (warehouse rent, which you pay whatever you buy), it is an expense. If no (the container, the duty, the freight for that shipment), it is COGS. [a0e16cf2802e] [4b0ff6e78865]
4. **Before-sale cost vs after-sale cost.** The guest uses sale timing as a working test: the product box is a cost before you sold it (COGS), the outbound shipping and brown box are costs after you sold it (expense). Note: card 4b0ff6e78865 adds that "timing before or after sale alone is not the classification test". That caution is the card's, not the source's. See section 9. [4b0ff6e78865] [df753e79eb2d]
5. **Classify consistently so gross profit is comparable.** The point of the above-the-line and below-the-line split is "good comparison figures" across periods and channels. [4b0ff6e78865]
6. **Costs arrive in lumps but must end up per unit.** Freight and duty come on big invoices from different parties. They must be attributed "down to a unit by unit cost" so you see the total cost of each unit. Where 1 shipment holds 3 or 4 different products, the cost "needs to be weighted or attributed differently" across them. The source does not say by what (weight, volume, value, or count). [427dd537598d] [366fbbf40de6]
7. **People and process come before systems and software.** Even though the guest recommends software, he says good COGS "is about people and process before it is about systems". [366fbbf40de6]
8. **Statutory COGS and internal KPIs can differ.** The rules in this source are for statutory or compliance accounts. Some businesses, especially in apparel, also track contribution margin and returns cost KPIs that use different cuts. Do not force every internal cost view into 1 COGS number. [bb8589d09789] [4b0ff6e78865]
9. **Free shipping and free returns are getting harder to fund.** The guest says they cut "into your profits significantly". COGS margin matters, but you need all costs of doing business to judge profit. Opinion. [bb8589d09789]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Classify any cost (decision sequence from the source's tests)
1. Is the cost part of buying the product or getting it onto your shelf ready to sell? If yes, COGS. [a0e16cf2802e]
2. If unsure, ask: would I have paid this cost if I had not bought these goods? If I would have paid it anyway, it is an expense. [a0e16cf2802e]
3. Is the cost incurred after the sale (delivery to the customer, the shipping carton)? Then it is an expense, unless 1 of the 2 outbound shipping exceptions applies (see 3.3). [79cf39d0f59a]
4. Is it a reduction in the price the customer pays (coupon)? Then it reduces revenue. It is not COGS and not an ad expense. [4b0ff6e78865]
5. Check the quick-fire table in section 4 for the common cases.
6. The source's own caveat: "there's no [rule] that's 100%". For statutory accounts, confirm the treatment with the accountant. `[needs brand data: the brand's written accounting policy and its accountant's treatment of outbound shipping, packaging and returns]`

### 3.2 Build landed unit cost (the source's components, written out)
The source gives no formula. Written out from what it lists:

- Landed cost of a shipment = purchase price of the goods (net of supplier discounts) + inbound freight (including the container cost) + import duty paid + any other cost to get the goods into your warehouse and onto your shelf. [a0e16cf2802e] [61551ff00944]
- Product packaging (the retail box) is also part of product cost. [df753e79eb2d]
- Landed cost per unit of a SKU = that SKU's purchase price per unit + that SKU's share of the shipment's freight + that SKU's share of duty + product packaging per unit. The "share" is set by an allocation weighting that the source does not name. [427dd537598d]
- Duty: the guest says "tariff is the percentage of the import duty and then the actual import duty is what you pay". So the tariff rate is a percentage and the duty in COGS is the amount actually paid. The source does not say what value the percentage is applied to. `[web check: customs value basis and duty rules for the brand's import country]`
- Gross profit = revenue (net of coupons and price discounts) minus COGS. Everything below that line (fees, ads, outbound shipping, storage) is operating expense. [4b0ff6e78865]

Steps:
1. When raising a purchase order, record the agreed unit price. [366fbbf40de6]
2. When the supplier invoice arrives, check its price against the PO price before it goes into the financials. Prices can change with commodity markets and currency moves, so the price is not always clear up front. [366fbbf40de6] [a0e16cf2802e]
3. When a freight or duty invoice arrives, identify which PO or POs it belongs to and record the cost against them at that time. Do not file it to sort out later. [366fbbf40de6]
4. If 1 freight or duty invoice covers several products, weight the cost across them. The source says the weighting differs per item but gives no basis. Card 427dd537598d mentions weight or value; card a0e16cf2802e mentions weight, volume or value. Pick 1 basis, document it, and use it for every shipment. [427dd537598d] [a0e16cf2802e]
5. If one supplier invoice mixes product boxes and shipping cartons, split the line items: product boxes to COGS, shipping cartons to shipping expense. [df753e79eb2d]
6. Apply supplier discounts to product cost (see 3.5).
7. Make the stock adjustment in the accounting system so purchases sit as inventory, not as a straight expense. The source names the failure (purchase invoices posted with no stock adjustment) but does not give the steps. [a0e16cf2802e] `[web check: inventory and COGS journal steps in the brand's accounting system, such as Xero, QuickBooks or NetSuite]`
8. Recalculate landed unit cost when supplier price, exchange rate or freight changes. (Card instruction, grounded in the source's note that prices and currencies change.) [a0e16cf2802e]
9. As the business grows, move allocation from a spreadsheet to inventory management software that computes weighted landed cost per item. Check the software's totals tie back to the actual freight and duty invoices. [427dd537598d] [366fbbf40de6]

### 3.3 Outbound shipping to customers
1. Default: expense, not COGS. [79cf39d0f59a]
2. Exception A: the shipping is "included in the price", meaning you do not charge for it (free shipping). The guest says it "could be considered as part of your COGS". [79cf39d0f59a]
3. Exception B: drop shipping, where the supplier charges you for what is effectively outbound freight. The guest says that "could also be considered a cost of goods sold". [79cf39d0f59a]
4. If neither exception applies, it is an expense. [79cf39d0f59a]
5. Whatever the accounting treatment, include all merchant-paid delivery cost when comparing order contribution for stocked vs dropshipped products (card instruction). [79cf39d0f59a]

### 3.4 Customer returns
1. Cost of shipping the return back to you: overhead (expense). [bb8589d09789]
2. Cost to repackage or refurbish the item so it can be sold again (for example a new box): "can go into" COGS. [bb8589d09789]
3. Cost of bringing it back and getting it ready to ship out again (without making it sellable): not COGS. [bb8589d09789]
4. If returns are large (the guest names apparel), track return KPIs and contribution margin alongside gross margin. `[needs brand data: return rate, return shipping cost per return, repackaging cost per return]` [bb8589d09789]
5. Optional commercial step: at the return, offer a voucher, a discount, an alternative item, a trade-up or an upsell to save the sale. The guest reports "great success" with this. He also warns it makes the accounting harder: you must work out what was actually sold and at what margin after the voucher or discount. Observation, no numbers. [bb8589d09789]
6. Optional: prepaid returns. The host says "a prepaid return [gets] a slightly discounted rate if you prepay it". It is unclear from the transcript who gets the discount. Card bb8589d09789 turns this into "negotiated discounted rate", which is the card's reading. [bb8589d09789]

### 3.5 Supplier discounts and credits
1. A discount on goods you are buying reduces their cost, so it reduces COGS. [61551ff00944]
2. A credit note needs checking: it may be for stock you sent back, in which case there is a stock movement and a credit note together. [61551ff00944]
3. The guest's general view: treat supplier credits against the supplier cost in COGS. He hedges ("it depends", "generally speaking"). Confirm with the accountant. [61551ff00944]

## 7. Examples and case results

Examples only. No numbers are given anywhere in the source.

- **Apple box example:** the white retail box of a phone is COGS. The brown box it is delivered in is shipping expense. [df753e79eb2d]
- **China container example:** a product shipped by container from China carries the container cost into COGS, and that container cost must then be allocated to each product. [a0e16cf2802e]
- **Warehouse rent example:** you would pay rent even if you had not bought that shipment, so it is not COGS. The container, duty and freight you would not have paid, so they are COGS. [a0e16cf2802e]
- **3 or 4 items on one shipment:** freight and duty must be weighted differently across them. [427dd537598d]
- **Returns to resale:** the guest reports "great success" when brands encourage a purchase at the return step, since the customer often wanted something, just not that item. No figures. [bb8589d09789]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Not calculating COGS at all: purchase invoices dumped into Xero, QuickBooks or NetSuite with no stock adjustment. [a0e16cf2802e]
2. Not allocating freight and duty to each unit. [a0e16cf2802e] [427dd537598d]
3. Keeping a pile of freight and duty invoices that are never matched to the products they were for. [366fbbf40de6]
4. Mixing up inbound freight (COGS) with outbound shipping (usually expense). [79cf39d0f59a]
5. Putting Amazon fees or advertising into COGS. [4b0ff6e78865]
6. Getting confused by shipping income vs shipping expense. The guest names this as a confusion but does not explain how to treat shipping income. [4b0ff6e78865]
7. Coding a mixed invoice (product boxes and packing boxes) to a single account. [df753e79eb2d]
8. Treating all returns costs the same way. [bb8589d09789]
9. Not checking the supplier invoice price against the PO price. [366fbbf40de6]
10. Accepting "close enough" COGS. The host's framing: close enough is not good enough because margin drives strategy. [a0e16cf2802e]
