## 1. What this source is for

It teaches a marketer or founder to run paid media from the P&L instead of the ad account. It gives: a DTC P&L layout with 3 expense buckets, correct gross margin (not product margin), the CM1/CM2/CM3 ladder, a bottom-up pricing waterfall, break-even ROAS and CAC under discounts, CAC and LTGP:CAC with bands, a 3-tier measurement pyramid, the profit frontier, cash vs profit (inventory grades, cash conversion cycle, financing risk), and separate new vs returning revenue forecasts. It is strong on definitions and worked examples. It is thin on incrementality test design, cohort data prep and exact KPI targets.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 The ad account is not the business; paid media must reconcile to the P&L
Platform metrics are a proxy. If the ad account looks good but the business is not improving, "something is fundamentally broken". Many teams think they have an ads problem when they have a measurement, margin or cash flow problem. Condition: applies to all brand sizes; presenter's opinion from agency work. [4ca15f364bbb] [d5c2a3d880d9]

### 2.2 Revenue is not profit, and profit is not cash
Example: a $10M business at 5% net makes $500K. A $5M business at 20% net makes $1M. The presenter would rather own the $5M business: less stress, double the profit. He calls 20% net "healthy in ecom". He says many founders chase revenue expecting margin to expand, and for many early-stage founders it never does. Net profit also does not equal cash flow in ecommerce (see 2.24). [4ca15f364bbb] [ff3c54a4afb8]

### 2.3 The DTC P&L has 3 expense buckets: cost of delivery, marketing, OPEX
Layout top to bottom: Gross revenue > Net revenue > Cost of delivery (COD) > Gross margin > Marketing (MEA) > Contribution margin > OPEX > EBITDA > Interest > Net profit. The biggest expense is usually COD, the second is marketing. Intended scaling shape: OPEX stays roughly flat, marketing and COD rise with revenue, so profit expands as a percent. What the presenter says actually happens at most seven-figure brands: founders are "not good capital allocators", OPEX rises faster than marketing and COD, and profit stays flat in dollars for 3 to 4 years. [4ca15f364bbb] [7c524289c2d3] [8cc010473338]

### 2.4 Read gross to net, not just net revenue
Net revenue = gross revenue minus returns, refunds, chargebacks and sometimes a discount allowance. Shipping charged at checkout sits in the gross-to-net stack too. Shopify shows gross to net. The source says the breakdown usually does not flow into Xero or QuickBooks, which show a net or cash read only. So a net revenue change in accounting software can hide its cause. Worked example: net $100K then $110K; gross was $120K both months; refunds fell from $20K to $10K; discounts unchanged; shipping free. The growth came from refunds, not demand. Alternative example: refunds stable, but $10K of discounts ran the prior month and not the next. [c9ccfa76430a]

### 2.5 Refunds, discounts and shipping collected are profit levers
Lower refunds, fewer discounts and more shipping collected at checkout each raise net revenue with no extra traffic. The source claims "most people under-collect" shipping at checkout. Condition: raising shipping charges is an offer change; the card says test conversion impact first (card caveat, not the source). [c9ccfa76430a]

### 2.6 COGS is the full landed cost, and COD adds shipping, fulfillment and fees
COGS = product cost + import taxes and duties + freight to your warehouse (landed cost). Outbound shipping to the customer (and duties or tariffs on international orders) sometimes sits in COGS and sometimes in "shipping and fulfillment". Both sit under cost of delivery. Transaction fees belong in COD because they vary with revenue; the source calls putting them in OPEX "a mistake". Warehouse and 3PL costs, and warehouse staff who pick, pack and ship, also belong in COD. [db1cf398804d] [7c524289c2d3]

### 2.7 Product margin is not gross margin
Product margin (CM1) = (price minus manufacturer unit cost) / price. Gross margin (CM2) = (price minus all cost of delivery, including an allowance for refunds and discounts) / price. Brands quote product margin as "gross margin" and then set marketing budgets off it, which can drive a product at an unprofitable efficiency. Worked example in 7.1: 70% product margin became 24% gross margin. The presenter calls this "takeaway number 1". [db1cf398804d] [e8db824002f5] [8d570963a07c]

### 2.8 Gross margin sets how much you can spend on marketing
At a true 70% gross margin, the presenter says he "would honestly probably be fine" spending 20% to 25% of revenue on marketing. If the real gross margin is much lower and nobody knows, the same spend is unprofitable. Condition: his judgment, one example. [db1cf398804d] [e8db824002f5]

### 2.9 Split marketing into variable (MEA) and fixed (OPEX)
MEA holds anything that varies with revenue: paid ads, influencer payments, events, out-of-home, and agency or short-term creative help that flexes with demand. A permanent in-house creative team is fixed, so it goes in OPEX. Reason: content demand flexes with the sales calendar (flat Q1, a June end-of-financial-year spike in Australia, big November and December). A fixed team of 4 is underworked in the troughs, overworked at peaks, and right-sized maybe once a year. Cover the flex with agencies or 3-month hires. Some brands put all creative in MEA because it drives revenue; the presenter accepts this as a brand-level choice. Card caveat: pick one convention and keep it consistent. [8cc010473338]

### 2.10 Contribution margin (CM3) is the best metric to index performance over time
CM3 = revenue minus cost of delivery minus all marketing. It is 1 step above net: CM3 minus OPEX = EBITDA ("net" in his simplified view). Because OPEX should be stable, a CM3 target can be back-solved from a net profit target (see 3.1). Naming: CM1 = product margin, CM2 = gross margin, CM3 = contribution margin. People say "contribution margin" and mean CM1 or CM2; confirm which a report means. [4ca15f364bbb] [7ca062ce13b6]

### 2.11 OPEX is everything not in COD or marketing; keep it tight
Big 3 OPEX buckets: people, software, office (non-fulfillment space). Everyone not directly fulfilling orders (head of marketing, head of operations) sits here. In ecommerce the way to "reinvest in the business" is marketing, not headcount, software or a bigger office, which "generally" do not drive revenue. The presenter also says people are the biggest lever in any business and the revenue ceiling is set by team talent, but seven-figure brands usually cannot yet attract or identify great hires. So the advice is to delay heavy people spend, not to never hire. In a large business the CFO guards OPEX; in a small one, the owner. [7c524289c2d3]

### 2.12 Interest goes below EBITDA, never in OPEX
Interest is leverage to accelerate growth, not an operating expense. A buyer wants EBITDA without interest because they will zero out the loans. EBITDA = the earning power with no loans. Net profit = what is left after interest (and, strictly, tax, depreciation and amortisation). The source says putting interest in OPEX is a common mistake on seven and eight figure P&Ls. [7c524289c2d3] [ff3c54a4afb8]

### 2.13 A missed target is caused by a line above it
Fault rule: missed EBITDA = OPEX, MEA or COD. Missed CM3 = MEA or COD. Missed gross margin = COD or revenue (gross and net, so refunds, discounts, shipping collected). If MEA creeps up, marketing efficiency is falling and squeezing the bottom. If gross margin compresses, check discounting and returns first. The presenter calls identifying the constraint "the single purpose" of the P&L (takeaway 5). [4ca15f364bbb] [7ca062ce13b6] [8d570963a07c]

### 2.14 DTC unit economics are per basket, not per unit
Retail unit economics look at 1 unit. DTC carts hold about 1.5 to 1.7 units (units per transaction), so compute unit economics on the basket. [9a77dac5a3ba]

### 2.15 CAC = total ad spend / new customers; never platform CPA and never per channel
Calculate over any period (day, week, month) from actual ad spend and actual new customers. Platform CPA runs on attributed numbers and "will end up showing you a better number" than reality. Channel-level CAC is "impossible" because it relies on attribution. [9a77dac5a3ba]

### 2.16 CAC means nothing without first-purchase gross profit on new customers
"What is a good CAC?" is the wrong question. A $100 CAC against $10,000 first-order gross profit (high-ticket furniture) is excellent. A $100 CAC against $20 GP (low-ticket sweets) loses $80 per customer. Use first-purchase GP of new customers, not blended AOV x blended margin: returning customers raise blended AOV, and new customers often buy on discount-led front-end offers. Worked check in 7.4. [9a77dac5a3ba]

### 2.17 Price with a bottom-up waterfall, then decide whether to sell the product at all
Keystone (cost x 2) ignores variable fulfillment and marketing costs; the presenter guesses 40% of startups price this way and calls it "probably the worst way you could ever price". IMU (initial markup, cost x 3, 4 or 5) is still an arbitrary multiple with no link to price elasticity, competitors or the real cost structure. Instead work up from targets: net target > CM target > CAC > return and discount allowance > shipping and fulfillment > COGS = price. Then check competitors. If the required price is far above market, either position as premium (a "blue ocean") or do not sell the product. Procedure in 3.2. [9ee62d2b55d0]

### 2.18 Discounts raise break-even ROAS sharply; run the math before any public discount
Break-even ROAS = 1 / gross margin %. Break-even CAC = gross margin $ per order. A 30% discount with unchanged costs can take break-even ROAS from 2.5 to 7 (source says 10; see 9.1). If the required efficiency is impossible (his example: 2x to 12x), the discount will not lift demand enough; rethink the offer. If break-even CAC falls to $10, the discount "will never work for acquiring new customers" and "shouldn't go out publicly". Discounts are allowed; blanket discounts need this check. [c49b93d6bb68]

### 2.19 Prefer bundles and gift-with-purchase to flat discounts
Bundling: going from 1 to 2 units does not double shipping and fulfillment; the source says costs rise about 20%. So gross margin improves and some can be given back as the offer without hurting GM %. Bundles also raise AOV, which supports a higher CAC. Gift with purchase: a low-cost gift with a high perceived value (see 7.6). Flat discounting is for grade C (slow) inventory, even at break-even, because it was not going to sell anyway. Card caveat: a gift does not guarantee the extra sale; measure incremental conversion. [c49b93d6bb68]

### 2.20 LTV must be time-boxed and measured as gross profit (LTGP)
LTV keeps rising as the window extends, even as it flattens (example: $100 at 6 months, $110 at 12, $120 at 18). So restrict the window: the source suggests 90 or 180 days here, and 30, 90 and sometimes 365 days for LTGP:CAC (see 9.4). Use gross profit, not revenue; many tools report LTV as revenue. In software, LTV is close to gross profit; in ecommerce it is not. [285ed6bb4705] [55599aea266f]

### 2.21 Most profit usually comes from returning customers
Acquisition is expensive; returning customers come back for product, experience and brand with little spend. In the source's example the smaller returning revenue stream made more profit than new customers (see 7.7), which the presenter calls "super typical". In CPG you may not be profitable on first purchase; competitors with high LTV can pay $200 CAC and outbid you. Agencies run a 3 to 6 month CAC payback; CPG, fashion and retail also do this. Hesitation: most people calculate LTV wrong, so be careful using repeat profit to subsidise acquisition. [30261689958c] [285ed6bb4705]

### 2.22 Put spend where contribution per sale is highest, not where CPA is lowest
Maximize conversions (the default on Meta, Google, TikTok) optimises for the lowest CPA, so spend flows to the cheapest-to-acquire product, which may make less contribution per sale. Maximize conversion value will not fix it if the higher-contribution product also has the worse ROAS. Fix: segment campaigns by product category using product-level economics. Also ask: would the cheap-CPA product sell without spend? If so, stop spending on it and move budget to products that need paid help for sell-through. Example in 7.5. [3827dc99f5ed] [ac6ec12672a6]

### 2.23 Measurement pyramid: platform metrics < finance-grade KPIs < incrementality
Bottom: ROAS, CPA, CTR, multi-touch attribution (Triple Whale named). Fast, lowest reliability, use only for directional campaign and ad reads inside 1 platform. Middle: acquisition MER, MER, profit contribution, CAC, calculated from actual new customers, new customer revenue and ad spend. Top: incrementality (geo holdout) tests. Moving up, reliability rises and speed falls, so read finance-grade metrics over longer windows. Never compare ROAS across platforms: different attribution models, funnel positions and incremental impact. The source says incrementality testing "probably isn't applicable for 80%" of viewers; it matters for the 20% "doing over $10 million a month" with a US or multi-market or omnichannel presence (see 9.6). [d5c2a3d880d9] [08a2587ba438]

### 2.24 All attribution is correlation, and it over-credits the bottom of the funnel
An ad click then a purchase is correlation, not proven cause (analogy: ice cream sales and swimming deaths both rise in summer). A billboard and TV ad warm someone up; a Facebook ad gets the click and all the credit. Current pattern the source sees at some large retail brands: TikTok drives new-market impressions with few click-offs; Meta retargets those pixel visitors; Google captures the brand search. Raise TikTok spend and Meta and Google ROAS rise, TikTok's does not. A C-suite reading raw ROAS would cut TikTok and move money to Google, which kills top-of-funnel demand; bottom of funnel "will stop working" without it. Condition: the TikTok pattern is time-bound and "not applicable for everyone". [08a2587ba438] [bc14184eea60]

### 2.25 MER is contaminated by returning customers; use acquisition MER
MER = revenue / ad spend (a multiple; some compute spend / revenue as a percent). $100 revenue on $50 spend = MER 2. It looks like a good index of total paid performance, but it includes returning revenue that "would have returned anyway", so marketing takes credit for it. Acquisition MER (aMER) = new customer revenue / ad spend. In the example aMER "might" be 1.4, which may not be profitable. Always split finance-grade metrics into new-customer versions. [8aab49e8f65e] [8d570963a07c]

### 2.26 LTGP:CAC is the best single efficiency metric, inside a fixed window
LTGP:CAC = gross profit per customer / CAC. Unrestricted windows (for example 6 years) lead to overspending on cash you may not see for 4 years. Measure 30-day and 90-day, and 365-day only when acquisition is aggressive. Bands in section 4. Below 1 is usually poor acquisition efficiency, not a strategy; the source says most brands it audits that lose on acquisition lack the retention to support it. Below 1 can also mean first-order GP is too thin (under about $70 GP on first order "is probably a problem"). Above 3 is also a mistake: you are under-using paid media. "If you think your retention is good, it's usually mediocre." [55599aea266f] [e8db824002f5]

### 2.27 Keep view-through conversions and existing customers out of reporting
On Meta use 7-day click for optimisation and reporting. The source says 7-day click ROAS is "super correlated" with acquisition MER "in most businesses" because it drops view-through conversions. View-through: a user sees the ad, does not click, buys within 24 hours, and Meta claims it; many would have bought anyway. Existing customers are not very incremental; platforms "always want to spend here" and widen attribution windows because more attributed revenue means more spend. [de7656f52701]

### 2.28 Budget existing-customer ads from customer count x frequency x CPM, not a percent of spend
Percent-of-budget framing breaks for big brands (a large nine-figure brand gets 80% of revenue from existing customers). Instead: how many existing customers, how many times a month to show them an ad, and the CPM for that segment. Result is usually far lower than teams assume. Formula and example in 3.8. [10c558b5c0d8]

### 2.29 The profit frontier: fund the next best dollar, stop at incremental break-even
Question every founder or head of marketing must answer: if we spend an extra $10,000 next month, where does it get the best incremental return? Also the inverse: can $10,000 moved from one channel return more in another? Push each channel until the marginal dollar makes no more profit, which is where incremental acquisition MER hits break-even, then stop. Only after the 3 primary channels hit the frontier add tertiary channels. The source says the only reliable way to know marginal returns is incrementality tests; MMM or intuition can stand in at seven-figure scale. [c60eaf1ca845]

### 2.30 Past a scale point, brand is needed; budget it as a P&L line
From $1M to "probably" $20M revenue you can brute-force growth with performance ads ("$1 in, $4 out"). Past about $30K to $40K a day in ad spend, returns diminish and brand is how you go further. Evidence: a $300M/year fashion ad account shows better CPMs, CTRs, CPCs, conversion rate and ROAS than an $8M/year one despite far more spend, which he attributes to associations built outside the ad account. Two personal purchases (2XU for perceived premium quality, 247 Represent because running influencers wear it) came from brand, not performance ads. Brand activations will not show direct profitable ROI. Condition: opinion and anecdote. [e4043b0af395] [090525149bfe]

### 2.31 Inventory on the balance sheet can make a profitable brand cash-negative
Example: P&L shows $2M profit, but $3M of inventory was bought and never sold, so the business is down $1M in cash. Assets in ecommerce are mostly unsold inventory and cash. Even at $1M to $2M profit, the founder may take nothing because it is all reinvested in future stock. This is why paid media in ecommerce is harder than in services, SaaS or info products. [69c220a278fc] [beb1703b3d17]

### 2.32 Grade inventory A to D and use paid media to turn slow stock back into cash
A = fast-moving (only worry about stock-outs). B = medium sell-through. C = slow. D = not moving. Product launches are a main growth lever for most brands because, unlike supplements (Grüns, AG1, IM8 can reposition 1 product into many needs), most products cannot be repositioned into a big market. Many launches fail: an initial pop from organic and existing customers sells 30% to 40% of stock, then nothing. Each failed launch adds cash to the balance sheet. So rationalise SKU count as new products arrive. Paid media can clear C and D stock at break-even or a slight loss. An agency KPI'd only on profit will starve B, C and D stock because it has poor ROAS; the marketing team needs the inventory position. [69c220a278fc] [4d29da5108ca]

### 2.33 When the CFO wants to cut media because stock is stuck, cutting may be wrong
Scenario: November stock still unsold in February. CFO sees revenue dip, MEA rise from 25% to 30%, profit fall from 30% to 25%, and proposes a 40% media cut. Two problems: if marketing drives revenue, cutting it drops revenue further and starts a loop of cuts. And clearing C and D stock needs more spend, not less. The source's fix: raise MEA short term from 30% to 35%, with the extra 5% only for clearing C and D stock back into cash. Condition: only when cash is the constraint; the source calls the CFO reaction logical from the P&L alone. [0c1ff48b0d7b] [4d29da5108ca]

### 2.34 The cash conversion cycle, not ad efficiency, often caps growth
CCC = DIO (days inventory outstanding) + DSO (days sales outstanding) - DPO (days payables outstanding). Compounding: sell 10 units, make $50 GP, can only buy 11 units next, make $55, buy 12. The limit is cash for stock, not ads. With a 90-day lead time and full payment up front, you stock out for 90 days while waiting for cash. Fixes: better supplier terms, shorter lead time, or inventory financing (see 3.12). The presenter says "you can't triple an ecom business in a year" without multi-million self-funding, outside funding, or financing. [beb1703b3d17]

### 2.35 Financing is expensive and risky; slower growth at better efficiency can earn more
Lenders usually want the founder as collateral. Debt bets on selling future units at an efficiency that repays the loan. Pulling efficiency down to grow faster raises both marketing cost and debt, and can squeeze net margin to zero. The presenter says you are often "better off growing slightly slower" at a better MER with less interest. Anecdote in 7.10. Agencies working with seven-figure brands should assume the client is using financing. [beb1703b3d17] [8d570963a07c]

### 2.36 Forecast new and returning revenue separately, by their inputs
The source's "number one rule" of DTC forecasting. New customer revenue is driven by marketing inputs (ad spend, plus events, influencers, other channels). Returning revenue is driven by product launches, marketing events such as discount periods, email and SMS, and other reasons to come back; ads add only a little. Forecast the inputs and derive the output. Procedures in 3.9 and 3.10. [db6a44e073b2] [4870955af64e]

### 2.37 Forecasting is about the gap between the realistic number and the target
There will always be a delta. Example: the board wants $50M, the model says $40M. Then name the inputs that close the $10M: a marketing event, a new product, a new channel, tripled creative production. [0d00eb5d6d39]

### 2.38 Five closing takeaways (source's own summary)
1) Know gross margin and calculate it correctly (variable costs, product vs gross margin, flow into CM). 2) Define metrics and keep live dashboards of acquisition MER, profit contribution and ideally LTGP:CAC, not daily or weekly ROAS reports, which the presenter calls "not productive at all" for core decisions. 3) Separate new and returning metrics: new customer economics, aMER, new customer profit contribution, new customer revenue, new customer cohort size. 4) Know whether you are in a cash play or a P&L play; if the CCC is the limit, pushing budgets only adds interest and lowers MER; share SKU inventory position so marketing can trade efficiency for cash turnover. 5) Use the P&L to find the constraint. [8d570963a07c] [7ca062ce13b6] [4d29da5108ca] [e8db824002f5]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Back-solve a contribution margin target from a net target
1. Set the net profit target (percent or dollars) `[needs brand data: owner's net target]`.
2. Forecast OPEX (percent or dollars) `[needs brand data: OPEX run-rate]`.
3. CM3 target = net target + OPEX.
4. Examples: 20% net + 15% OPEX = 35% CM3. $1M net + $1M OPEX = $2M CM3 for the year. Another: CM3 50% - OPEX 10% = 40% net.
[4ca15f364bbb]

### 3.2 Waterfall pricing (bottom to top)
1. Start at the bottom: net target and OPEX give the CM3 target (3.1). Source example: 10% net + 10% OPEX = 20% CM3 target.
2. Estimate CAC for this product from the ad account and similar products. Example: $30. `[needs brand data: CAC for comparable products]`
3. Set a return (shrink) allowance and a discount allowance as a percent of price. Example: 3% returns + 3% discounts.
4. Get shipping and fulfillment per order from the courier. Example: $11 (quoted by Australia Post).
5. Get COGS. Example: $6.
6. Solve price. The card gives the formula: P = C / (1 - target CM rate - f), where C is fixed dollar costs per order and f is the sum of percent charges. Example: C = 30 + 11 + 6 = $47, f = 6%, target 20%: P = 47 / 0.74 = $63.51. The source first tried $100 (leaves about $47 to $49 contribution, far above target) and then guessed "probably like a $70 price" without doing the math. At $70 the contribution is $18.80 (26.9%). The exact answer is $63.51. The denominator must be positive. [9ee62d2b55d0]
7. Research competitor prices `[web check: competitor prices for this product]`.
8. If the required price is far above market (example: everyone at $30 to $40): can you position as premium where nobody sells a premium version? If not, do not sell the product.
9. After launch, stress-test CAC, return rate and fulfillment cost with real data (card addition). [9ee62d2b55d0]

### 3.3 Compute true gross margin per product or basket
1. Price paid by customer. Remove pass-through sales tax (VAT or GST).
2. Subtract product unit cost from the PO.
3. Add inbound freight and duties per unit (PO freight cost / units).
4. Add outbound shipping per order, pick and pack per order, and payment fees (percent of revenue).
5. Add a refund and discount allowance per order. `[needs brand data: refund rate and average discount by category]`
6. Gross margin = (price - total COD) / price.
7. Do it per basket where UPT is above 1 (2.14), and per SKU and channel (card addition).
8. Document the definition. The card's version removes tax from the denominator and says to remove any refund dollars counted in the allowance from the revenue base too, so net and gross bases are not mixed. [db1cf398804d]
Worked example in 7.1.

### 3.4 Reclassify warehouse staff into cost of delivery
1. Take a warehouse staff member's cost per day. Example: $400.
2. Divide by packages shipped per day. Example: 100. Result: $4 per order.
3. Have the bookkeeper tag these staff in the accounting software and build a custom P&L report that moves them into COD `[verify UI: Xero or QuickBooks tracking categories]`.
4. Result: monthly gross margin includes the people who fulfill orders. [7c524289c2d3]

### 3.5 Monthly P&L review (constraint diagnosis)
1. Build the ladder with dollars and percent of revenue for each line: gross revenue, refunds, discounts, shipping collected, net revenue, COD, gross margin, MEA, CM3, OPEX, EBITDA, interest, net.
2. Set a target for each line `[needs brand data: targets]`. The source gives no universal targets.
3. Compare month on month: which percents expand or contract?
4. For any line below target, look only above it (fault rule 2.13) and find the lever: refunds, discounts, shipping collected, landed cost, pick and pack, fees, media efficiency, OPEX creep.
5. Pull that lever instead of cutting budgets by reflex.
[4ca15f364bbb] [7ca062ce13b6]

### 3.6 Break-even ROAS and CAC for any offer
1. Gross margin % at the offer price (use true GM from 3.3).
2. Break-even ROAS = 1 / GM %. Equivalent: price / GM $.
3. Break-even CAC = GM $ per order.
4. Repeat at the discounted price with costs unchanged.
5. If the new break-even is out of reach on paid platforms, do not run the discount publicly; consider a bundle, gift with purchase, or restrict it to C-grade stock.
6. Card caveat: break-even excludes OPEX and profit, so set the operating CAC ceiling below it.
[c49b93d6bb68]

### 3.7 Product-level economics table and campaign split
1. For each product or category: price, COGS, gross margin $, CAC (or CPA from the scaling campaign), contribution per sale = GM $ - CAC. The source suggests Google Sheets. `[needs brand data: SKU costs and CPA by product]`
2. Rank by contribution per sale, not CPA.
3. If a lower-contribution product has the lower CPA, maximize conversions will favour it; split campaigns by product category so high-contribution products get deliberate budget.
4. For a product with an unusually low CPA, ask whether it sells without ads (virality, product-market fit, retail stores). If yes, cut its paid spend (the card says validate with a holdout first) and move budget to products that need paid for sell-through.
5. Build it into the regular decision routine, not a one-off sheet.
[3827dc99f5ed] [ac6ec12672a6]

### 3.8 Existing-customer ad budget
1. Count existing customers `[needs brand data: customer file size]`.
2. Choose monthly frequency. The source suggests 3 to 6.
3. Get the existing-customer CPM from the platform's audience segment breakdown `[verify UI: Meta audience segments breakdown]`.
4. Monthly spend = customers x frequency x CPM / 1,000. Example: 300,000 x 3 x $2.50 / 1,000 = $2,250. (The source and card drop the "/ 1,000" in words but their $2,250 result includes it.) The source says $2.50 is "super low"; real CPMs will be higher.
5. Sanity check: the source says even big brands with millions of customers are usually fine at $10K, up to $30K to $40K a month at 3 to 6 frequency, and it has seen tiny businesses spend $40K a month on existing customers.
6. Check 7-day frequency on Meta; above 8 means overspending (see 5.4).
[10c558b5c0d8] [de7656f52701]

### 3.9 Returning customer revenue forecast (cohort method)
1. Build a table of new customers acquired each month back to your first data (example: from Jan 2021). `[needs brand data: monthly new-customer counts and repeat orders]`
2. From cohort analysis, get the average repeat rate by customer age in months. Example curve: 7% in month 1, then 6%, 4%, 2%, 1%, tapering to a small floor.
3. For each cohort: cohort size x repeat rate for the month it is about to enter x returning-customer AOV. Example: 100 customers entering month 32 at 0.1% x $100 AOV. The source says this gives $1; the arithmetic gives $10 (see 9.1).
4. Sum all cohorts. The source says this lands within about 10% accuracy.
5. To tighten to 1% to 2%: apply a seasonality factor from the average of the last 3 years of returning revenue by calendar month, then add expected revenue from marketing events and launches that differ from last year.
[c87f67f6310b] [db6a44e073b2]

### 3.10 New customer revenue forecast (spend vs aMER curve)
1. Plot about 1 year of daily data: x = ad spend, y = acquisition MER. `[needs brand data: daily spend and new-customer revenue]`
2. Remove outliers: sale periods, November and Black Friday (they overstate BAU efficiency), and spend levels below your realistic minimum (source: under $1,000; he says "a month" while using daily points, see 9.5).
3. Fit a logarithmic or linear regression, whichever fits best.
4. Read expected aMER at a planned spend (example: $8,000 a day).
5. Back-propagate from the goal: example target $100,000 new customer revenue at 4 aMER; find 4 on the curve and read the spend. Check whether that spend is possible at that efficiency.
6. If not, name the input that will beat last year's average (event, launch, channel, creative volume).
7. Influencers and other non-paid channels need their own models; the source gives none.
[0d00eb5d6d39]

### 3.11 Geo holdout test (outline only; the source says do not DIY)
1. Split the country by states (in Australia, by commuting zones because there are too few states).
2. Pick a test area, often 2 adjacent states.
3. Choose a design: hold out (raise spend everywhere except the area), raise spend only in the area, or cut spend entirely in the area.
4. Example: double budget for the under-attributed TikTok campaign in the test states for 30 days.
5. Measure new customer revenue lift vs control and compute incremental ROAS.
6. The presenter warns: a "Google sheet" version will "pretty much always say states aren't incremental", over- or under-spend, and not reach statistical significance. You need data-science skills. Card da12ab245d95 adds a predicted counterfactual from pre-test history; that step is not in the transcript.
[da12ab245d95] [d5c2a3d880d9]

### 3.12 Fix a cash conversion cycle squeeze
1. Map lead time and payment terms per supplier `[needs brand data: supplier terms and lead times]`.
2. Negotiate a smaller deposit. Source example: 20% up front, rest on shipment; after selling 2 units you can place the next order, and the stock-out shrinks from 90 days to the 20-day shipping time.
3. Or find a manufacturer with a shorter lead time (example: 90 to 30 days).
4. Or use inventory financing: a loan pays for stock, sales repay it, then a new loan funds the next PO. Weigh cost, collateral (usually personal) and the risk of slower sales. The card adds: compare real quotes and stress-test slower sales before borrowing.
[beb1703b3d17]

### 3.13 Clear C and D inventory with paid media
1. Grade SKUs A to D by sell-through. Shopify auto-ranks inventory; the source thinks it is "under the products tab" `[verify UI: Shopify ABC inventory report location]`.
2. For each slow SKU, compute days of inventory on hand. Example: 400 days.
3. Put the SKU in its own ad funnel at break-even or a slight loss. It does not need to sit on the main site. Use whitelisted (creator) pages.
4. Option the source used for a client: sell D stock in a new country where heavy discounts will not hurt brand equity in the main market.
5. Judge by cash recovered, not ROAS. Keep this separate from profitable acquisition (card).
6. Rationalise SKU count as new products launch.
[69c220a278fc] [4d29da5108ca] [0c1ff48b0d7b]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

### 5.1 Is "gross margin" really product margin?
Check: ask how the number was calculated. If it is (price - PO unit cost) / price, it is product margin. Good: includes landed freight, duties, outbound shipping, pick and pack, fees, refund and discount allowance, minus tax. Bad: PO cost only. Fix: rebuild with 3.3, then reset marketing and CAC targets. [db1cf398804d] [e8db824002f5]

### 5.2 Why did net revenue move?
Check: gross revenue, refunds, discounts and shipping collected per month (Shopify gross-to-net; accounting software usually shows only net). Good: movement explained by gross demand. Bad: movement from refunds or discounts. Fix: work the lever (refund causes by product and cohort, discount code use, shipping pricing). [c9ccfa76430a]

### 5.3 Is CAC honest?
Check: is it total spend / new customers from the store, or platform CPA? Is it paired with new-customer first-order GP, or blended AOV x blended margin? Good: store-based, new-customer only. Bad: platform CPA, per channel, blended AOV. Fix: 2.15 and 2.16. Requires clean new vs returning tagging `[needs brand data: new vs returning customer flag]`. [9a77dac5a3ba]

### 5.4 Is spend leaking to existing customers?
Check on Meta: audience segment breakdown for existing customers' share of spend; frequency column over the last 7 days `[verify UI]`. Good: frequency under 8; existing-customer spend near the 3.8 formula. Bad: frequency over 8; tens of thousands a month on a small base. Fix: cap existing-customer spend with the formula; report on 7-day click. [de7656f52701] [10c558b5c0d8]

### 5.5 Is MER flattering paid media?
Check: MER vs aMER in the same period. Bad: MER 2 while aMER 1.4 (source example), or aMER below break-even ROAS. Fix: manage to aMER and new customer profit contribution. [8aab49e8f65e]

### 5.6 Is budget drifting to bottom of funnel because of ROAS?
Check: are budget moves driven by cross-platform or top vs bottom funnel ROAS (example: Meta 2x vs Google 6x)? Bad: moving money to the 6x branded search or retargeting line, then no growth. Fix: use aMER and, where budget allows, a controlled test before large reallocations. The source hedges with "likely" and "probably". [bc14184eea60] [08a2587ba438]

### 5.7 Is OPEX eating the growth?
Check: OPEX as a percent of revenue over 12 months, and profit in dollars. Bad: revenue up, OPEX percent flat or rising, profit dollars flat. Fix: freeze non-revenue hires, software and office spend; put growth money into marketing. [7c524289c2d3]

### 5.8 Is interest in the right place?
Check: where loan interest sits in the P&L. Bad: inside OPEX. Fix: move it below EBITDA. [7c524289c2d3] [ff3c54a4afb8]

### 5.9 Is the brand a cash play or a P&L play?
Check: inventory on hand vs profit; days of inventory by SKU; CCC; debt and interest trend `[needs brand data: balance sheet, inventory aging, loan terms]`. Bad: profit on the P&L but cash falling; C and D stock over hundreds of days; interest rising while MER falls. Fix: 3.12 and 3.13; slow growth to a better MER. [beb1703b3d17] [69c220a278fc] [4d29da5108ca]

### 5.10 Should we cut media because cash is tight?
Check: does media drive revenue here? What share of stock is C or D? Bad: cutting spend, revenue falls, more cuts. Fix: consider a short-term MEA lift aimed only at clearing C and D stock (2.33). [0c1ff48b0d7b]

### 5.11 Can this discount work?
Check: break-even ROAS and CAC at the discounted price (3.6). Bad: required ROAS jumps several times (the source's "2x to 12x") or break-even CAC falls to a level never seen on paid. Fix: bundle, gift with purchase, or limit to C stock. [c49b93d6bb68]

### 5.12 LTGP:CAC band check
Check: 30-day and 90-day LTGP:CAC on new customers. Below 1: fix acquisition efficiency or first-order GP. 1 to 2: tolerable only with proven strong retention or funded growth. 2 to 3: scale. Above 3: scale harder. [55599aea266f]

## 7. Examples and case results
All are examples with illustrative numbers, not benchmarks.

### 7.1 $100 t-shirt: 70% "gross margin" is really 24%
Product cost $30 -> product margin 70%. Add outbound shipping $11, pick and pack $4, transaction fee $3 (3%), sales tax $9 (pass-through), inbound air freight $4 per unit, refund and discount allowance $15 (about $5 discounts + about 10% refunds). Extra costs $46, total $76. Source: (100 - 76) / 100 = 24%. Card db1cf398804d treats tax as removed revenue instead: $91 revenue, $24 contribution, 26.4%. The presenter calls it "an incredibly extreme example". [db1cf398804d]

### 7.2 Gross to net
Gross $120K both months; refunds $20K then $10K; net $100K then $110K. [c9ccfa76430a]

### 7.3 P&L waterfall
Revenue $100; COD $40; gross margin $60 (60%); MEA $20 (20%); CM3 $40 (40%); OPEX $20 (20%); EBITDA $20 (20%); interest $10; net $10 (10%). [4ca15f364bbb] [ff3c54a4afb8]

### 7.4 CAC target built on the wrong numbers
Blended AOV $100 x assumed 70% GM = $70 GP, "so we're happy with $35 CAC". New-customer AOV is really $90, discount-led offers push first-order GP to about $50. The CAC target is wrong. [9a77dac5a3ba]

### 7.5 Hoodie vs t-shirt
Hoodie: $90 price, $21 COGS, $69 GM, $45 CAC (Advantage+ scaling campaign CPA), $24 contribution. T-shirt: lower price, similar COGS, $27 GM, $15 CAC, $12 contribution. The hoodie makes 2x contribution per sale but has the higher CPA and the worse ROAS (hoodie ROAS is 90/45 = 2.0), so both default bid strategies favour the t-shirt. If the t-shirt sells without ads, cutting its spend lifts its contribution by $15, to $27 per order. (Card ac6ec12672a6 says "by $15 to $27" as a range; the source means from $12 to $27.) [3827dc99f5ed] [ac6ec12672a6]

### 7.6 Discount and offers
$100 price, $40 GM: break-even ROAS 2.5, break-even CAC $40. At 30% off ($70), costs $60 unchanged: GM $10; break-even CAC $10; break-even ROAS 70/10 = 7. Gift with purchase: 2 beach towels at $100 each, about 70% margin; free bag "valued" at $50 to $60 costs $4; source says you give up $4 margin to gain $100 extra revenue and roughly double AOV. [c49b93d6bb68]

### 7.7 New vs returning profit contribution
$200K monthly revenue: $140K new, $60K returning. Ad spend $50K. At 50% GM: new = $70K - $50K = $20K. Returning = $30K - $2.5K (Klaviyo and a retention agency) = $27.5K. The smaller stream made more profit. [30261689958c] [db6a44e073b2]

### 7.8 LTGP:CAC
$100 GP / $33 CAC. Source says 3.33; arithmetic gives 3.03. [55599aea266f]

### 7.9 Inventory and launches
P&L $2M profit, $3M unsold stock bought: down $1M in cash. Launch ordered at $1M sell value, sold $100K, 400 days of inventory on hand. [69c220a278fc]

### 7.10 Agency growth that broke the business
The presenter says he has seen it about 5 times: an agency took spend from $20K to $200K a month and revenue from $2M to $7M a year, while the founder took millions in inventory financing. Interest grew, ads became less efficient, and net margin fell to near zero; the founder went from taking an income to near-zero net. [beb1703b3d17]

### 7.11 CFO cut scenario
COD 30%, marketing 25%, OPEX 15%, profit 30%. February: marketing 30%, profit 25%. CFO proposes a 40% media cut. Source's alternative: MEA to 35% for a short period to clear C and D stock. [0c1ff48b0d7b]

### 7.12 Profit frontier
Current spend Meta $100K, Google $50K, TikTok $70K (the source does not say which figure is which channel precisely; it lists them in that order). Test +$10K per channel. Example: the marginal $10K on Google returns 2x; another $10K on Meta returns 4x on new customer revenue, so move it. [c60eaf1ca845]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Quoting product margin as gross margin, then setting marketing budgets off it. [db1cf398804d]
2. Reading only net revenue from accounting software and missing refund, discount and shipping levers. [c9ccfa76430a]
3. Putting transaction fees in OPEX (they vary with revenue). [db1cf398804d]
4. Leaving warehouse staff in OPEX, which overstates gross margin. [7c524289c2d3]
5. Putting interest in OPEX. [7c524289c2d3]
6. Growing OPEX (people, software, office) faster than revenue in the name of "reinvesting"; profit stays flat for years. [7c524289c2d3] [8cc010473338]
7. Chasing revenue growth expecting margin to expand later. [4ca15f364bbb]
8. Using platform CPA as CAC, or computing CAC per channel. [9a77dac5a3ba]

11. Running public discounts without recalculating break-even ROAS and CAC. [c49b93d6bb68]
12. Using open-ended or revenue-based LTV. [285ed6bb4705] [55599aea266f]
13. Accepting LTGP:CAC below 1 as "a strategy" when it is poor efficiency or thin GP; also sitting above 3 and under-spending. [55599aea266f]
14. Letting maximize conversions pour spend into the cheapest-CPA product. [3827dc99f5ed]
15. Paying to advertise products that would sell anyway. [ac6ec12672a6]
16. Comparing ROAS across platforms; cutting top-of-funnel channels (TikTok) because their ROAS is low. [08a2587ba438] [d5c2a3d880d9]
17. Moving budget to the highest-ROAS bottom-of-funnel line and then wondering why growth stops. [bc14184eea60]
18. Using blended MER to judge paid media. [8aab49e8f65e]
19. Counting view-through conversions and over-serving existing customers (frequency over 8). [de7656f52701]
20. Allocating existing-customer budget as a percent of spend; tiny brands spending $40K a month on existing customers. [10c558b5c0d8]
21. DIY geo tests in a spreadsheet; they "pretty much always" show no incrementality. [da12ab245d95] [d5c2a3d880d9]
22. Spreading spend across tertiary channels before the 3 primaries hit the profit frontier. [c60eaf1ca845]
23. Daily or weekly ROAS reports as the core decision tool. [8d570963a07c]
24. Treating profit as cash; ignoring inventory on the balance sheet. [69c220a278fc]
25. Agencies KPI'd only on profit starving B, C and D stock of spend. [69c220a278fc]
26. Launching products without rationalising SKUs. [69c220a278fc]
27. Cutting media by reflex when stock is stuck, starting a spiral. [0c1ff48b0d7b]
28. Scaling ad spend with no view of the client's CCC and financing; trying to triple in a year. [beb1703b3d17]
29. Lowering efficiency to grow faster while debt rises. [beb1703b3d17]
30. Forecasting total revenue in one line instead of new and returning separately; extrapolating revenue instead of inputs. [4870955af64e] [db6a44e073b2]
31. Leaving sales periods and BFCM in the spend-efficiency regression. [0d00eb5d6d39]
32. Relying only on performance marketing past the plateau with no brand budget. [e4043b0af395] [090525149bfe]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

### 9.1 Arithmetic slips in the source (use the corrected numbers)
- Discount example: the source says break-even ROAS becomes "1 / 10% = 10", "5x" the original 2.5. Correct: GM % at the $70 price is 10/70 = 14.3%, so break-even ROAS = 7, which is 2.8x the original. Card c49b93d6bb68 already uses 7. Break-even CAC of $10 is correct.
- LTGP:CAC example: 100 / 33 = 3.03, not 3.33.
- Cohort example: 100 x 0.1% x $100 = $10, not $1. Card c87f67f6310b repeats the $1.
- Waterfall pricing: the source's "about $70" is a guess; the exact price for its inputs is $63.51 (3.2). Its $100 walk-through subtracts loosely ($83 called "about 85").
- Existing-customer formula needs "/ 1,000" for CPM; the $2,250 result is right. Card 10c558b5c0d8 writes the formula without it.
- Brand example: $300M vs $8M is about 37.5x, not the "20x the volume" he says.
- Gross margin example: 24% on $100 (source) vs 26.4% on $91 (card) depends on whether tax is removed from revenue or counted as a cost. Pick one convention and state it.

### 9.2 Card errors and additions to watch in the merge
- Card 8aab49e8f65e says the 1.4 aMER comes "from $100 revenue and $50 spend". In the source, $100/$50 is the blended MER of 2; the 1.4 aMER is a hypothetical with no new-customer revenue stated.
- Card ac6ec12672a6 reads "$15 to $27" as a range; the source means +$15, to $27.
- Card 69c220a278fc names "Grenade"; the transcript says "Grunes", most likely Grüns, alongside AG1 and IM8.
- Card 0c1ff48b0d7b says "MAR"; the source means MEA (marketing as a percent of revenue).
- Cards 8d570963a07c, 7ca062ce13b6 and 4d29da5108ca carry an added bullet ("interest alone does not change MER; model sales and spend separately"). That is a card edit, not in the source.
- Card ff3c54a4afb8 adds an EBITDA to net income to cash bridge, loan principal in the cash forecast, and not using EBITDA alone for distributions. The source only says interest sits below EBITDA and net profit is not cash.
- Card da12ab245d95 adds a predicted counterfactual built from pre-test history; the transcript only compares test states against control. The source header ("Look for") also mentions counterfactual revenue.
- Card beb1703b3d17 adds "compare financing quotes and stress-test slower sales"; the source only says financing is expensive, needs collateral and is risky.
- Card 9ee62d2b55d0 supplies the closed-form price formula; the source does not.
### 9.3 Internal inconsistencies
- "Net profit" is used for EBITDA in the first waterfall, then corrected. Tax, depreciation and amortisation are named but never modelled.
- The pricing waterfall uses "contribution margin" after CAC (CM3-style per order), while the P&L section defines CM3 after all marketing. For a single product these are the same idea at unit level.
### 9.4 LTV window
Earlier the source recommends 90 or 180-day LTV; later it measures LTGP:CAC at 30 and 90 days (365 when aggressive). Not a contradiction in method, but the merge should keep both and state which window a target uses.
### 9.5 Unit slips in thresholds
- Frequency: "over the last 7 days it's under an eight", then "more than eight times a month". The 7-day reading matches the Meta column he names; treat the month phrasing as a slip.
- Regression cut-off: "a minimum of $1,000 a month" while plotting daily points; likely $1,000 a day.
### 9.6 Scale heuristic that looks off
"The 20% that's doing over $10 million a month" for incrementality testing may mean $10M a year. Keep as stated and flag `[web check: industry guidance on minimum spend for geo-lift]`.
### 9.7 Conflicts with common ecommerce advice
- The frequency cap of 8 over 7 days for existing customers, the "above 3 is a mistake" LTGP:CAC band, and the view that ROAS is only a within-platform metric are strong single-presenter opinions. Common advice often treats LTV:CAC of 3 or more as the healthy target; this source calls 2 to 3 optimal and above 3 under-investment.

- "Can't triple an ecom business in a year" is presented as a rule; it is his view without data.
