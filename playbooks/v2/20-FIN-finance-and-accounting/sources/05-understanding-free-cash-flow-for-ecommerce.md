## 1. What this source is for

It teaches an operator to explain why a store that is profitable on the P&L can still run out of cash, and why a loss month can grow the bank balance. The method: read the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement together, start the cash flow from net income, adjust for balance sheet changes, and arrive at free cash flow. For ecommerce, the swing items in the example are inventory buys, capitalised software build, quarterly tax payments and loan principal. Use it for a monthly cash review, a "why is cash down when we made a profit" investigation, and before a big inventory buy, a platform build or a new loan.

## 2. Principles

1. **Net income is often misleading for a startup. Free cash flow shows what is really going on.** Source says net income "roughly" tells you whether the unit economics should make money. It does not tell you whether cash is left. Condition: applies to all business models (software, marketplace, retail); ecommerce was chosen because it shows nearly every cash driver (inventory, assets on the balance sheet, debt). Evidence: the case model. [652ee8dcf710] [6fab761526bc]
2. **Free cash flow answers one question: once the business has funded 100% of its operations and needed investments, is any cash left over?** After free cash flow, the business has no other obligations except financing ones. That leftover cash can pay down debt, pay owner distributions, or anything else. [652ee8dcf710] [059ee049c6fc]
3. **Most operating expense lines equal their cash outlay. Depreciation and product cost do not.** Personnel (96k a month in the model), ad spend, other marketing, consultants, insurance, office rent, software and miscellaneous: the cash paid equals the P&L figure. Depreciation spreads an asset purchase over 3 or sometimes 5 years, so the P&L hides the cash that left. Product cost on the P&L is recognised as goods sell, but cash goes out in big lumps when inventory is bought. Card caveat: prepaid or accrued items can also break the "cash equals expense" link. [b4b11ba26397]
4. **Inventory is bought in chunks, so cash and profit move at different times.** An inventory balance increase is cash out. A decrease means stock was sold through and turned into cash. So some months produce a lot of cash from sell-through and some months lose a lot to stock buys. Card caveat: made-to-order, dropship and pre-order stores have very different timing. [6a8332853dd6] [059ee049c6fc]
5. **Capitalising software development moves payroll off the P&L onto the balance sheet.** The cash leaves now; the P&L shows only the depreciation (spread over the useful life, 36 months for most software per the source). Accounting purpose: match the cost to the useful life of the asset. Effect: large cash drain with small P&L cost. [7d9e989e15be]
6. **Debt principal repayments never show on the income statement.** Only interest shows (in the interest and taxes section). An interest line tells you debt exists. Principal movements show only on the balance sheet (loan balance) and in the financing section of the cash flow. [4f714af151a4]
7. **Taxes are accrued monthly on the P&L but paid in lumps.** The model books tax at 25% each month, builds a tax liability on the balance sheet, and pays it quarterly as an estimated payment. Cash leaves in the payment month, not the accrual month. Card caveat: the tax calendar varies by country and entity; do not assume quarterly. [416bbd9031ed] `[web check: tax payment calendar for the brand's country and entity type]`
8. **Gross margin % must be watched every month, not just gross profit.** In the model it is stable except in the sale month, when orders nearly doubled and gross margin % fell because of the discount, then recovered after. Condition: the source gives no safe discount depth or target margin band. [02bbff7623e0]
9. **Net cash flow (after financing) is a holistic view, not an operating view.** It tells you whether the business can pay its debts and run itself at the same time. Free cash flow is the operating view, because whether a business has a loan is "unrelated to the operations of the business itself". Source says net cash flow is "slightly lower" than free cash flow in the model because the business has leverage (it is repaying principal). See section 9 for the limit of that statement. [4f714af151a4]
10. **A profitable store that is losing cash is usually moving inventory, capitalising development, paying down debt or timing tax.** Card framing of the case's opening contradiction, used as a diagnostic template. [6fab761526bc] [cc661026f56a]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Read the 3 statements in order (source's walk-through)
1. **Orient on high-level metrics first:** revenue per month, net income per month, free cash flow per month, total cash balance. Note months where net income and free cash flow point in different directions, and months where cash falls while profit is positive. [6fab761526bc] `[needs brand data: monthly P&L, balance sheet and cash flow for at least 6 months]`
2. **Income statement:** walk orders, net revenue, the COGS lines (product cost, import duty, inbound freight, pick and pack, shipping, merchant fees), total COGS, gross margin and gross margin % of revenue. Then operating expenses line by line. Mark which lines are cash-equal and which are not (depreciation, product cost). Note if depreciation is rising over time: that signals asset purchases. Note an interest line: that signals debt. Note the tax line: accrued, not necessarily paid. [b4b11ba26397] [02bbff7623e0]
3. **Balance sheet:** check assets = liabilities + equity (a mathematical rule; if it does not balance, the model is wrong). Then read the movement in: cash, inventory, accounts receivable, other current assets, capitalised software (or other long-term assets), accounts payable, tax liability, line of credit or loans. [6fab761526bc]
4. **Cash flow statement, operating section:** start with net income linked from the income statement. Then adjust:
   - Accounts receivable up = negative to cash (people owe you). Down = you got paid, positive.
   - Accounts payable up = positive to cash (you have not paid yet). Down = you paid, negative.
   - Depreciation = add back (it never left the bank).
   - Inventory up = negative to cash. Inventory down = positive.
   - Tax liability: payment months take cash out; non-payment months keep cash in.
   [059ee049c6fc] [cc661026f56a]
5. **Investing section:** long-term asset purchases, such as the software platform build, equipment, or shares in another company. A monthly salary is an expense (operating), not an investment. [059ee049c6fc]
6. **Arrive at free cash flow** (operating cash flow less investment spending; see the formula in section 4).
7. **Financing section:** loan drawdowns and repayments, money lent in by owners or others, owner distributions. This is capital structure, not trading. Add it to free cash flow to get net cash flow, the change in the bank balance. [059ee049c6fc] [4f714af151a4]

### 3.2 Reconcile a profitable month with negative free cash flow
1. Take the month's net income. [cc661026f56a]
2. Apply each balance sheet adjustment from 3.1 step 4.
3. Subtract that month's investing spend (for example capitalised development).
4. Name the drivers. Source example: net income 77k, free cash flow -95k; main driver a very large inventory purchase, plus payments to engineers building the software platform.
5. Card-writer additions (not said by the presenter): rank the causes by size (inventory, capitalised development, tax timing, debt repayments are the usual candidates); if the shortfall was a planned inventory build, confirm the sell-through timeline and cash runway; if unplanned, fix that driver before repeating the spend; look at several consecutive months before concluding, since one month can be distorted by seasonal or one-off items. [cc661026f56a]

### 3.3 Card-writer checks for inventory cash timing (not in the source text)
1. Compare COGS of units sold with inventory purchased and supplier payment dates. [6a8332853dd6]
2. Track deposits, balances due, inventory receipts and payables. An inventory balance increase is not always an equal cash payment in that month: supplier credit and prepayments change the bridge. [6a8332853dd6]
3. Forecast cash at planned reorder dates and stress-test slower sell-through. [6a8332853dd6] `[needs brand data: PO schedule, supplier deposit and balance terms, sell-through rate by SKU]`

### 3.4 Card-writer checks for capitalised software (not in the source text)
1. Separate software costs expensed now from costs that qualify for capitalisation under the applicable policy. [7d9e989e15be]
2. Record actual cash paid, even where the P&L shows only amortisation later.
3. Reconcile asset additions, amortisation, payables and non-cash changes; the asset balance change alone is not cash spending.
4. Subtract capitalised development cash in the stated free cash flow definition. Confirm treatment with the accountant. `[web check: capitalisation rules and useful-life norms in the brand's jurisdiction]`

### 3.5 Card-writer checks for tax cash (not in the source text)
1. Get the entity's real tax payment calendar and amounts from the accountant. [416bbd9031ed]
2. Forecast payments in the months they are due.
3. Reconcile tax expense, tax payable and payments.
4. Keep cash reserved for upcoming tax out of spending decisions. `[needs brand data: tax calendar and last 4 payments]`

## 4. Rules, thresholds and numbers

Formulas as the source and cards state them:

- Free cash flow (source's words) = operating cash flow minus investing cash flow. Read this as operating cash flow less cash spent on investments. [652ee8dcf710]
- Free cash flow (card's common definition) = operating cash flow - positive cash capital expenditure. State which definition you use. Do not subtract a negative investing figure (that would add it back), and do not assume every investing line is capex. [652ee8dcf710]
- Operating cash flow = net income + depreciation - increase in accounts receivable + increase in accounts payable - increase in inventory +/- change in tax liability (and other working-capital lines). Signs reverse when balances fall. [059ee049c6fc] [cc661026f56a]
- Net cash flow = free cash flow + financing cash flow (loan drawdowns in, principal repayments and distributions out) = change in the cash balance. [4f714af151a4]
- Balance check: assets = liabilities + equity. [6fab761526bc]
- Monthly depreciation of a capitalised cost = cost / useful life in months. Source example: 100,000 / 36 months. [7d9e989e15be]

## 7. Examples and case results

All from 1 made-up model. Examples only, not benchmarks.

- **Setup:** ecommerce startup, revenue about 415k a month, orders scaling from about 3,000 to about 5,000 a month over 6 months. Buys and sells inventory, pays off a loan, capitalises software development. [6fab761526bc]
- **Opening contradiction:** net income positive every month (transcript reads roughly "17k, 77k, 141..." for the first months); free cash flow positive in only 2 of 6 months (transcript reads "negative 22, 94..."); cash balance falls when it should rise and jumps in other months. [652ee8dcf710]
- **Month with net income 77k and free cash flow -95k:** driven mainly by a very large inventory purchase, plus payments to software engineers. [cc661026f56a]
- **Month with net income 140 and free cash flow 272 (units as spoken; likely thousands):** sold through a lot of inventory without buying more, paid little to the engineering team, and made no tax payment, so cash built up. [cc661026f56a]
- **Sale month:** orders nearly more than doubled, gross margin % dropped due to the discount, then recovered in later months. In a sell-through month, free cash flow can exceed net income even as margin % falls (card framing). [02bbff7623e0]
- **Software:** 240k spent in 6 months on the platform, only 40k shown as depreciation; the rest hits the P&L over the next 3 years. [7d9e989e15be]
- **Worked capitalisation example:** engineering team paid 100,000 in month 1; instead of a 100,000 expense, spread over 36 months. [7d9e989e15be]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Judging startup health on net income alone. [652ee8dcf710]
2. Reading only the P&L: it hides inventory buys, capitalised software, debt principal and tax payment timing. [652ee8dcf710] [6fab761526bc]
3. Treating depreciation as cash spent that month, or treating product cost on the P&L as the month's cash spent on stock. [b4b11ba26397]
4. Treating debt as an operating issue: whether the business has a loan is a financing choice, so judge operations on free cash flow, and use net cash flow for "can we pay debts and run". [4f714af151a4]
5. Watching revenue volume in a sale month without watching gross margin %. [02bbff7623e0]
6. Card-writer additions: subtracting a negative investing number in the free cash flow formula; assuming every investing item is capex; treating borrowing as improved cash health (it raises cash but not free cash flow); using the model's 36 months or 25% as your own policy or tax rate. [652ee8dcf710] [7d9e989e15be] [416bbd9031ed]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Formula wording is loose.** The source says "operating cash flow minus investing cash flow". Investing cash flow is usually shown as a negative number, so the correct arithmetic is operating cash flow plus investing cash flow, or operating cash flow minus capex as a positive number. The card fixes this. Do the arithmetic in code with signs stated. [652ee8dcf710]
- **Internal contradiction in the setup.** The setup says the store runs out of money in profit months and gains cash in loss months, but the model shows net income positive in every month. The real point is profit vs cash mismatch; there are no loss months in the example.
- **"Net cash flow is slightly lower than free cash flow because of leverage"** holds only when the business is repaying principal (as in the model). In a month the business borrows, net cash flow is higher than free cash flow. [4f714af151a4]
- **Free cash flow vs other definitions.** The source counts capitalised software as investing. Other analysts define free cash flow differently (for example, some include or exclude certain items); state the definition in any report. [652ee8dcf710]
- **Thin on how:** no guidance on how much inventory to hold, cash runway targets, minimum cash buffer, safe discount depth, target margin %, or how to forecast cash forward. It explains the past month; it does not give a forecasting method. Cards add forecasting checks the presenter did not give (3.3 to 3.5).
- **Tax example is US-style** (quarterly estimated payments). Other countries use other schedules. `[web check: the brand's tax and indirect tax payment dates]`
- **Capitalising software is less common for stores on hosted platforms** that pay monthly app fees (these are operating expenses). The source's software driver may not apply to a small DTC brand. This is an observation, not something the source says. `[needs brand data: does the brand capitalise any build cost]`
- **Conflict with common ecommerce advice:** much ecommerce advice judges health on ROAS, contribution margin or net profit. This source says those can look healthy while cash falls, and free cash flow is the check. They do not conflict on unit economics; the source adds the timing layer.
- **Neighbour sources:** cash forecasting, inventory planning, pricing and discount strategy likely live in other sources in this folder and in merchandising and retention folders; merge in pass 2.
