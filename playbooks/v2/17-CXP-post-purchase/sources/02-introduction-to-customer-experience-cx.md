## 1. What this source is for

This is an orientation talk on general customer experience (CX) theory, not an ecommerce how-to. It gives a mental model (feeling drives behaviour), an evolutionary maturity scale (random / intentional / differentiated), a 3-part framework (strategy / measurement / people), and a first pass at "who is your customer" and "map the journey, not the process." The presenter is explicit that the applied methodology (persona depth, journey-mapping mechanics, measurement tool set) is reserved for her paid workshop, so treat this source as concepts and a shared vocabulary, not a procedure manual. The source's own curator note states it is "100% baseline (general customer experience concepts and theories)" with no ecommerce content from the presenter herself; every ecommerce framing in the 14 cards below (PDP, cart, post-purchase email, merchandising meetings) is the card-writer's translation on top of general theory, not something the presenter said. Do not extend that translation further without treating it as an inference.

## 2. Principles

1. Emotion drives behaviour. The way a customer feels during an interaction is what determines what they do next: return, advocate, or leave. Evidence: presenter's assertion plus a live audience reflection exercise (recall a recent experience, ask what happened / how did it make you feel / what did you do). No measured ecommerce link is given. [30663f02bf6d5]

2. Work backwards from the desired customer emotion and behaviour to the proposition, instead of building a proposition first and hoping customers adopt it. Consider both rational and emotional needs when shaping the proposition. Presenter frames pure business-metric optimisation (e.g. urgency tactics) as a trust risk if it overrides the customer's felt experience. [30663f02bf6d5]

3. Put the customer literally at the centre of the organisation, not just in language. Illustrated by the practice she attributes to Amazon: an empty "customer chair" in every meeting room, tested against "would our customer like this?" for every decision. A stated side benefit: acting as if the customer is present also tends to make meetings more efficient. This is a described Amazon practice, not a measured result. [6edd7693ad4a]

4. Three simultaneous market shifts are driving CX's rise: customers increasingly buy from companies that "do good" (not just for the world, but for them); employees increasingly want to work for companies that do good; investors increasingly want social impact alongside financial return. Presenter states, without citation, that "doing good" companies are also profitable and sustainable. [ff0c754a9777a]

5. CX maturity runs through three evolutionary phases, and every organisation sits somewhere on this line whether it has chosen to or not:
   - Random: outcomes vary by who handles the interaction; unpredictable; the presenter says this carries unnecessary cost.
   - Intentional: a shared vision, strategy and cross-functional commitment exist, so everyone can "think customer first"; this is where profitability starts, per the presenter.
   - Differentiated: everyone, everywhere, acts in the customer's interest all the time; the company stands apart and is (per the presenter) also sustainable.
   She estimates roughly 80% of companies are random, ~15% intentional, ~5% differentiated (citing Amazon and Disney as differentiated examples), and that the random-to-differentiated journey takes about 8 years from scratch. CX is not a one-off annual project; it keeps evolving. [2d05c23909e5]

6. In a live poll of this session's audience on what makes a "favourite brand," the reported order was: (1) corporate attitude / doing good, (2) easy to do business with, (3) helpful when there's a problem, (4) attitude of the people, (5) personalisation, then (6) product or service, then consistency. Product only appears sixth. The presenter notes the order likely depends on category, so this is not offered as a universal ranking. [fe0fd05d0bc2]

7. Product, price and quality are hard to differentiate today because competitors copy fast and cheaply; CX is harder to copy and is where the presenter says a business can compete. All three — product, process and people — are needed together to create "something to be experienced"; CX is not a substitute for the other two. [ff0c754a9777a]

8. Word of mouth is framed as "the new marketing." The presenter cites 92% of customers trusting family/friend recommendations more than any other form of marketing, unsourced in this session. Being customer-centric does not mean always doing what the customer asks, but the experience should be good enough that word of mouth spreads on its own. [ff0c754a9777a] [bdadd07890fc]

9. Know the customer beyond demographics before deciding what the experience should be: their key motivators generally (not only around your product), their attitudes toward your products/services, what they value in a business relationship, and their personal purposes. Sequencing matters: decide who the customer is and what you do for them, then decide the intended experience. [bdadd07890fc]

10. Distinguish "customer strategy" (which segments/markets to target — commercial) from "CX/experience strategy" (what you want customers to feel across the whole journey). The experience strategy sits under business purpose, corporate objectives and business strategy, acting as an umbrella over the whole organisation. It should include: what the experience is (the feeling you want), the customer principles you live by, and what each principle means in practice, plus the actions that make it real. Three completeness questions: do you know who your customers are emotionally; do you know what experience you want them to have; does everyone in the org know their part in delivering it. [0eea90f3c62d] [fa4cf546d38a]

11. The 3-part CX framework — strategy, measurement, people — is presented as a "global best practice" the presenter learned from an unnamed global partner, not her own invention.
    - Strategy: the three questions in principle 10.
    - Measurement: journey mapping plus other measures; measure from the customer's perspective, not only internally; know your improvement priorities (her company name's "Q" nods to the quality tools she uses here).
    - People: build the culture and environment that lets people act in the customer's interest; ask whether people know the strategy, act as ambassadors of it, and whether processes enable them to deliver it. This still applies to a one-person company.
    The three parts are puzzle pieces: having pieces without connecting them means you can't see the picture. Per the presenter, silo thinking (functions working apart) is the single biggest thing that breaks a CX strategy, and the source of random experiences. [fa4cf546d38a]

12. Customers experience a journey, not a process: "if they cannot feel it, hear it, see it, touch it, it is not a journey, it is a process." Journey mapping gives an outside-in view of the real experience and shows where to focus for both customer and business results, rather than fixing whatever is easiest. Before mapping: ask how capable current processes are of delivering the intended journey, and whether the intended experience has actually been decided. While mapping: run "what if" risk scenarios and prepare staff guidance (e.g. checklists) in advance, even for scenarios that may never occur. Journey mapping is an ongoing management tool, not a one-time workshop deliverable. [feb07cfa31b9]

13. On measurement method, the presenter states an explicit contrarian position ("in opposition to many of my global colleagues"): do not measure customers with things that annoy them, such as surveys — "we hate those surveys." Start with your own homework: find the facts and measures you already hold (she names Lean Six Sigma quality tools as useful here). Ask employees what they think customers think before surveying customers; they are closest to reality and feel valued when asked — but only ask if you already have a plan to act on the feedback, since asking and then doing nothing damages employee trust and performance. Walk in the customer's shoes directly: talk to them instead of sending a survey. Her view: most companies that measure satisfaction do it either to flatter their own ego (with no budget or commitment to fix what surfaces) or to "whip employees." Example given: a hotel post-stay survey asking if the room was in good shape — her view is that's the director's job to check personally, not the guest's job to report. [b24643e22b8c]

14. CX ownership should not be answered with a vague "everyone." Mature organisations have a dedicated CX function that works cross-functionally, similar to a quality function, rather than treating CX as a marketing-only activity (marketing only covers part of it). The principle extends into production/fulfilment: people assembling or packing must act in the customer's interest through the right quality and by treating each other well, because "how employees are treated is often a reflection of how customers get treated." Ultimate accountability sits with the CEO and leadership team — CX is called a leadership question, the way quality is everyone's responsibility within a system. [8c2c0d95481a]

15. Employee experience must be explicitly connected to customer experience delivery, not run as a separate initiative — the presenter says most companies disconnect the two. From her own time managing Customer/Product Support at ABB (robotics): engineers loved the product "like a baby," and the needed shift was from "helping the product" to "helping the customer with the product" — a mindset change she says takes years. Build a shared, common, consistent strategy together with the team covering both what you want customers to experience and what you want employees to experience. For staff handling a customer who asks for one thing but needs another: rely on the open culture and processes built through journey mapping (e.g. give staff a checklist: "if this happens, do ABC") rather than over-scripting. She states she is not a sales expert on this specific question. [41d67b919c4c]

16. B2B and B2G buyers get the same CX principles as B2C, per the presenter: "there is no real difference," because every customer is a human being, whatever the organisation. She rejects the objection "we're in B2B, we don't have to think about CX," since every customer still has a journey with you regardless. She simultaneously notes B2C practice is "somewhat more advanced" in CX, meaning more external examples exist there to borrow from. [e984e2fe9282]

17. Experience is a deliberate design choice, not an accident. Every customer already has some experience with an organisation whether or not that organisation intended it. Historical example: Steve Jobs at Apple in 1997, when sales were in steady decline and the company had too many products it was "hoping people would buy" — he told employees to start with the customer experience and work backwards to the technology, not the other way around. The presenter treats Apple's later success as validating this, without giving a mechanism. [b4c441cd3922]

18. Customer tolerance for friction has eroded because of experience leaders like Amazon, Airbnb and Uber ("it just works"), which raises the bar for whatever a customer buys next, in any category. Watch what leaders in your category deliver and use your own intended experience as the standard against which new tools and tactics are judged before you adopt them. [b4c441cd3922]

## 3. Procedures

The source gives few full procedures; the presenter repeatedly defers the applied "how" (journey-mapping mechanics, persona depth, measurement tool set) to her separate paid workshop. What is given:

**A. Personal feeling-to-behaviour reflection** (used live as an audience exercise): [30663f02bf6d5]
1. Recall a recent customer experience of your own, good or bad (she suggests a restaurant visit, travel, or a recent purchase).
2. Ask: what happened?
3. Ask: how did it make me feel?
4. Ask: what did I do about it?
5. Use the answers to see, in your own experience, that feeling drove the resulting action.

**B. "Draw your company" exercise** (used live; the source's own curator note flags this as one of the interactive exercises to skip when building cards, so it is documented here for completeness but was not turned into a card):
1. On paper, draw how you would explain your company graphically to someone else.
2. Common patterns she reports seeing: an org chart/structure; a KPI/metrics dashboard; a product-centred diagram with marketing, finance and "other stuff" drawn as extras around the product.
3. The most customer-centric organisations, she says, draw the customer in the centre with everything else (products, processes, people) around it.
4. Use the drawing to ask: where does the customer actually sit in how you picture your own organisation?

**C. The 3-part framework, applied as a question sequence:** [fa4cf546d38a]
1. Strategy: do you know who your customers are, emotionally? Do you know what you want their experience to be? Does everyone in the organisation know what part they play in delivering that journey?
2. Measurement: what is the journey that brings the intended experience to life? Do you measure it from the customer's perspective, not just internally? What are your improvement priorities?
3. People: do your people know the business and CX strategy? Are they ambassadors of the intended experience? How do you enable people and processes to deliver it?
4. Treat the three as linked, not separately owned, to avoid silo thinking.

**D. Recommended sequence for understanding customers, instead of surveys:** [b24643e22b8c]
1. Do your own homework first: identify facts and measures you already hold that answer the question (she names Lean Six Sigma quality tools as a source of these).
2. Ask your employees what they think customers think; they are closest to reality.
3. Before asking employees, have a plan ready to act on whatever feedback comes back.
4. Talk to customers directly ("walk in their shoes") rather than sending a survey.

**E. Journey mapping, at the level of detail given in this session (full method is reserved for her paid workshop):** [feb07cfa31b9]
1. Ask whether you have decided what service or product experience you intend to deliver.
2. Assess how capable your current processes are of delivering that intended journey.
3. Map the journey end to end from the customer's side, not your internal process steps.
4. Use the map to identify where to focus, based on what matters most to the customer and to business results.
5. During mapping, run "what if" risk scenarios and prepare staff guidance (e.g. checklists) for them in advance, even for situations that may never occur.
6. Repeat and update over time; do not treat the map as a one-off deliverable.

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- Check: does the customer appear on the leadership agenda? How: ask for the latest board or leadership meeting protocol and look for the customer as an explicit topic. Good: customer impact is a standing agenda item, checked before revenue impact. Bad: leadership is defensive about sharing the protocol, or the customer is not on it at all — the presenter says this is very often the case. Next: add a standing "customer impact" item to the agenda ahead of financial items. [6edd7693ad4a]
- Check: which CX maturity phase are you in? How: look for inconsistent reply times, variable packaging or service quality, and support answers that depend on which person handles the interaction (signs of "random"). Good: a shared vision, strategy and cross-functional commitment are visible and acted on (intentional), or the whole company visibly acts in the customer's interest (differentiated). Bad: outcomes vary by who is involved. Next: set milestones over years, not a single quarter, given the ~8-year timeline the presenter cites. [2d05c23909e5]
- Check: is your VoC/measurement programme actually useful, or performative? How: ask honestly whether surveys exist to make the company feel good about itself, or to pressure employees, versus being budgeted and acted on. Good: findings are funded and acted on; you already gather facts from your own data plus direct employee and customer conversations. Bad: surveys are sent with no plan or budget to fix what they find. Next: replace or supplement surveys with the sequence in Procedure D. [b24643e22b8c]
- Check: is CX organised in a silo? How: see whether CX responsibility sits only with one department (commonly marketing) rather than being cross-functional and leadership-led. Good: a dedicated, cross-functional CX function reporting into leadership. Bad: silo thinking — named by the presenter as the single biggest cause of CX strategies failing to come alive, and the root of "random" experiences. [8c2c0d95481a]
- Check: is employee experience linked to customer experience delivery? How: see whether employee-experience initiatives are designed and run separately from CX delivery work. Good: one common strategy covers both, explicitly connected. Bad: the two run in isolation — stated as a common company mistake. [41d67b919c4c]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Starting with a product/proposition and hoping customers adopt it, instead of working backward from the desired customer behaviour and emotion.
2. Saying "we put the customer at the heart of everything we do" as a slogan with no action behind it — called out as commonly said, rarely done.
3. Letting business metrics (AOV, urgency messaging) dominate design choices to the point that customer trust is compromised.
4. Leaving the customer experience to chance ("random") instead of designing it.
5. Leadership being defensive or dismissive when asked whether the customer appears on the board or leadership agenda.
6. Treating CX as a one-off annual project instead of something that keeps evolving.
7. Assuming product/service quality alone retains customers, when it ranked only sixth in this session's own poll of favourite-brand drivers.
8. Running satisfaction surveys mainly to flatter internal ego or to pressure ("whip") employees, without budget or commitment to act on the results.
9. Asking employees for feedback without first having a plan to act on it, which damages their trust and performance.
10. Outsourcing a judgement the business should make itself (e.g. a hotel checking room quality) to a customer survey instead.
11. Assuming CX is marketing's job alone, when marketing only covers part of it.
12. Running employee-experience work disconnected from customer-experience delivery.
13. Silo thinking in general — called the single biggest reason a CX strategy fails to come alive, and the source of random experiences.
14. Over-scripting front-line staff for edge cases instead of building open culture and processes plus lightweight, journey-mapping-derived guidance.
15. Assuming B2B or B2G buyers don't need CX thinking because "we're not consumer-facing."
