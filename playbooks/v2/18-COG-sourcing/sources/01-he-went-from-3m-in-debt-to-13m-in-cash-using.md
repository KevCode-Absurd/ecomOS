## 1. What this source is for

Gives a physical-goods operator a method for turning the factory relationship from a fixed cost into a negotiable, financeable one: build a bill of materials (BOM) down to the component and sub-vendor level, use it to win volume price breaks, use payment terms and supplier financing to build cash float, and use the resulting free cash flow discipline to take distributions instead of only reinvesting. The worked example throughout is Roman's own jewelry business (Linear) plus one acquired kids-accessories business made in India.

## 2. Principles

1. COGS, not marketing or overhead, is the biggest controllable lever in a mature ecommerce P&L, because overhead is sticky/predictable and marketing is a same-day switch, but a placed purchase order cannot be cancelled instantly. [f79ca98129]
2. A bill of materials (BOM) is the foundation everything else sits on — cost visibility, price breaks, payment terms and cash distributions all depend on first having accurate, component-level cost data. [615c598815, 6c57759a7fd2]
3. Seeing cost by component (not one lump "factory cost" line) opens product-design decisions, not just savings — e.g. choosing a cheaper input tier or adding a premium tier priced above its real cost delta. [31e1f32748]
4. Negotiating leverage comes from redundancy prepared in advance (alternate suppliers already vetted, competitor products reverse-engineered, category margin benchmarks known), not from asking harder. [89a60c40d3]
5. Cost-down requests land better framed as a shared-growth partnership (translate the ask into unit volume the factory cares about) than as a squeeze for savings you plan to keep. [769835fae6]
6. Volume price breaks are forward credits tied to the supplier's fixed-cost utilization (idle machine time, idle dedicated labor), not blanket discounts — they apply after you hit a threshold, not retroactively. [2cb8e11885]
7. A factory's own sub-vendors have their own volume breakpoints; those savings should be tracked and asked to pass through to you. [2cb8e11885]
8. Commodity and market-priced inputs (gold, lab-grown diamonds, chipsets) need ongoing monitoring, not a one-time quote, because supplier markup on them stays hidden unless you check spot prices yourself. [2d39c2fabf]
9. When tariffs, duties or commodity swings break your unit economics, deliberately holding growth flat is an acceptable response — pushing volume into broken margins is not required. [b1afff921b]
10. Payment terms should be evaluated as float (terms minus production lead time), because float is what buys you error tolerance when things go wrong, not a way to "get more stuff" cheaply. [520876950b]
11. Consignment (pay only when you sell) is the strongest form of supplier financing available, but it depends on the supplier being able to reclaim or resell unsold stock economically — it is not universally available. [520876950b]
12. Supplier-side credit insurance and invoice factoring mean your own financial hygiene (clean books, on-time payment history, no unresolved disputes) directly affects the terms a factory can extend you. [8b29d3a119]
13. Financing cost should be an explicit, negotiable BOM line item, not an invisible markup buried in unit price. [8b29d3a119]
14. Distributing cash requires first knowing free cash flow (not EBITDA) and the working capital needed for planned growth; only what's left after that reserve should go out as a distribution. [43a500ef77]
15. Component/supplier concentration (one supplier carrying a large share of COGS) is a risk to manage, whether via dual sourcing or by internalizing the concentrated input — internalizing trades supplier risk for direct commodity-price risk. [2d39c2fabf]

## 3. Procedures

### A. Build a bill of materials (BOM) for a SKU
1. List every physical component of the finished unit (source's example: a gold solitaire ring = diamond, gold, labor, factory overhead, factory margin). [6c57759a7fd2]
2. For each component, capture six data points: component cost, sub-supplier/source, lead time, country of origin, payment terms, and labor & overhead (kept as separate lines, not folded into "factory cost"). Labor and overhead are the hardest to get — the source says it "takes a lot of balls to ask how much you're paying labor" — but rapport over time makes this easier.
3. Physically disassemble the product with the supplier (zipper, button, lining, raw material) and verify sourcing claims directly (e.g. test whether "organic cotton" really is organic) rather than trusting the label.
4. Identify the sub-vendor layer beneath the factory — the factory may not make its own inputs (lab-grown diamonds, gold, zippers, chipsets are all sourced from someone else).
5. For components with a public or fast-moving market price (gold, diamonds, chipsets), track price monthly (or more often) so a supplier's markup on top of spot price stays visible.
6. Review the BOM with the supplier at least yearly and ask, per line, what volume unlocks the next price break.
7. When acquiring or auditing an existing store's supply chain, request the BOM first, before anything else — if none exists (the source claims ~99 of 100 founders it deals with in M&A have none), building one is the starting point of any cost work. [6c57759a7fd2]
8. Track packaging as its own BOM line rather than an assumed lump figure — the source says mentees who did this cut packaging cost by more than 50%. [215ef9f95360b]

### B. Open a cost-down negotiation with a factory (existing or new)
1. Before talking to the factory, do the homework: buy and reverse-engineer competitor samples, map at least one credible alternate supplier per key component, and where possible learn category-standard factory margins by talking to people who worked procurement at larger comparable brands. [89a60c40d3]
2. Enter the conversation naming the change plainly — e.g. "there's new ownership / I plan to grow this 5x" — and state explicitly that you want the supplier to win too, not just cut their margin. [769835fae6]
3. Share your P&L only in abstracted percentage terms (e.g. "20% COGS, 40% marketing, 10% profit") — you do not need to disclose full financials to build trust.
4. Translate the growth plan into unit volume, since factories respond to units, not marketing language: "we buy 1M units now, we want 10M units by [year]."
5. Go through the BOM line by line and ask what volume unlocks a price break on that specific component.
6. Offer to share a specific cost (e.g. financing cost, see procedure E) to demonstrate the partnership is two-sided.
7. If the factory won't move and you have redundancy already built (an alternate factory independently vetted), be prepared to switch. If you don't have that redundancy, build it before pushing hard — leverage entered late is difficult to recover. [89a60c40d3]

### C. Negotiate and enforce volume price breaks
1. For every BOM line item, ask the utilization question directly: how much of your dedicated equipment/labor is idle, and what volume would raise utilization enough to lower this cost?
2. Confirm the mechanism explicitly: these are forward credits applied after you hit an agreed volume threshold, not retroactive discounts — get the trigger written into the agreement. [2cb8e11885]
3. Have your production/ops lead sit with the supplier and reconcile invoices line-by-line to confirm the agreed credits are actually being applied (the source does this personally for its own brands).
4. Ask whether the factory's own sub-vendors give it volume breaks (example given: a large zipper maker), and negotiate for that saving to pass through to you.
5. Once volume justifies it, consider internalizing procurement of a specific raw material (buying gold/diamonds/chips directly and supplying the factory, leaving it as a pure assembler) to capture the sub-vendor's margin — only if you can absorb the resulting commodity price risk. [2d39c2fabf]

### D. Set payment terms and build float
1. Map the actual dates: when the deposit/PO is placed, when production starts, when goods ship, when they land, when they are actually sellable, and when the payment/processor payout happens.
2. Calculate float = payment terms (days) − production lead time (days). Worked example: 90-day terms minus a 30-day production/logistics lead time = 60-day float. [520876950b]
3. Push for longer net terms as your track record and volume grow — the source's own progression was net 7 to net 90 over years, and it cites net 180 as an extreme some operators use.
4. Ask specifically about consignment: does the product category have resale/reclaim value (the source's example is gold's melt value), and is your fulfillment geography compatible with the supplier reclaiming unsold stock (the source fulfills US orders domestically but non-US orders from a Hong Kong hub, which made reclaim practical for its supplier)?
5. If full consignment isn't available, consider a hybrid: the invoice states standard net terms on paper, but you actually pay in a smaller, regular cadence (the source pays weekly) and are not penalized for your own overordering mistakes.
6. Build a buffer into your float assumption for real-world ordering mistakes (e.g. wrong size mix) — these consume float that the nominal terms-minus-lead-time formula does not account for.

### E. Use supplier financing (credit insurance / invoice factoring) to unlock better terms
1. Ask the supplier directly whether they use export credit insurance to backstop their receivables (named examples in the source: Sinosure, a Chinese state-linked insurer, and Allianz) — this lets them extend longer terms without carrying all the default risk themselves. [8b29d3a119] [web check: current credit-insurance providers and terms in the supplier's country]
2. Understand that you (the brand) will be credit-graded — typically A/B/C — by an independent audit firm, based on management accounts, payment history and any past disputes or bankruptcies. The source itself was graded "C" once due to an 8–9-year-old unresolved supplier dispute.
3. Keep books clean, resolve or disclose old disputes, and pay on time to protect or improve your grade — this can take real time and effort to repair.
4. Request a written quote for extended terms with the financing charge shown as a separate line from the product price.
5. Add financing cost as its own explicit BOM line (e.g. "$6/unit now, $5/unit if we double volume") so a cost-sharing conversation is concrete rather than abstract.
6. Negotiate a specific cost split rather than assuming either side bears all of it — the source's own example is an 80% brand / 20% factory split.
7. Before treating this as automatically cheap capital, express the true cost in currency terms over the financed period and as a percentage of revenue (not just of COGS), and compare it against other lending options available to you. [63dcc0f46e]

### F. Respond to a tariff, duty or commodity shock
1. Pull the country-of-origin column from your BOM to see your real exposure.
2. If the shock breaks unit economics, decide explicitly to hold volume flat rather than push growth at broken margins — the source paused growth for about a year on an India-sourced business during a period of roughly 50% tariffs it describes. [b1afff921b]
3. Use the pause to open a renegotiation: if your BOM shows the supplier was running an abnormally high margin for the category (the source's example: ~25–35% on textiles, which it calls "abnormal"), ask for a temporary concession.
4. When the shock eases, be ready to give some margin back to the supplier as reciprocity — the source explicitly did this once tariffs were removed.
5. Longer term, evaluate relocating production to reduce the specific exposure — the source moved the affected line's production to Thailand.

### G. Sourcing audit for anyone taking over or auditing a supply chain
1. Ask for the BOM first. If none exists, that is the starting point, not a footnote.
2. Check the BOM's country-of-origin and supplier list for concentration risk (one supplier carrying a large share of COGS) and tariff exposure.
3. Ask when COGS on this SKU last declined. If the answer is "never" on a mature (e.g. 10-year-old) business, assume there is room to negotiate. [294e057145]
4. (Secondary, not COG's scope) Also check whether ad spend and channel mix look under-invested relative to revenue — the source treats "marketing spend" and "bill of materials" as the only two levers it checks when evaluating an acquisition. [294e057145] — full marketing-lever detail belongs in 10-MCR, see section 9.

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

| Check | How | Good | Bad | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do you have a BOM per SKU? | Ask for a component-by-component cost breakdown including sub-vendors and labor/overhead | Full breakdown exists and is reviewed at least yearly | No BOM, or "factory cost" is one lump line | Build it before any negotiation (Procedure A) |
| What share of COGS sits with one supplier? | Sum COGS by supplier from the BOM | Diversified across suppliers | One supplier carries a large share (source's own case: ~70%) | Build redundancy or bring the concentrated input in-house, weighing the added commodity risk |

## 7. Examples and case results

All figures below are Roman Khan's own reported operating numbers for his businesses (primarily Linear, plus one acquired company), presented as examples, not benchmarks or promises.

- Linear (jewelry): gold price ran roughly $2,000 → $5,000 in the period referenced, forcing about 6 price increases in 18 months; the business reportedly kept growing, which the source attributes to taking share from luxury competitors and to lab-grown diamond costs falling at the same time. Linear's COGS: ~$12M this year, projected $20M+ next year; one main supplier concentrates ~70% of COGS (bundles gold + diamond procurement). Current EBITDA margin ~15%, targeting ~20%; raw gold cost alone is ~15% of Linear's revenue as a separate line.
- Acquired kids-accessories business (India-made, bought June 2024): TTM revenue ~$24M, TTM EBITDA ~$3M, spending only ~$1M/yr on ads at acquisition (cited as the acquisition's "green field"). Growth was paused for about a year during a period of ~50% India tariffs; the supplier (previously running ~25–35% margin) conceded some margin during the pause; growth resumed once tariffs lifted, and Roman agreed to pay a bit more again; production later moved to Thailand.
- China credit-insurance/factoring example: on ~$12M/yr spend, roughly 5% (~$600k/yr) financing cost; about $6/unit at 100,000 units/yr, projected to fall to $4–5/unit at ~20M units/yr; an 80% (brand) / 20% (factory) cost split was negotiated.
- Packaging: the source says it cut some mentees' packaging cost by more than 50% once they had a real BOM for that line, and that his own "Lux" tier packaging runs $8–9/unit versus a ~$12/unit figure he calls a "dummy number." One brand reportedly saw ~15% higher LTV after a packaging upgrade. This is also the stated origin of his packaging supplier venture, Petra Sparks.
- Personal turnaround (headline example, explicitly marked by the source as an outcome, not a promise): at 32, Roman had −$3M net worth after a "growth at all costs" period at Linear (single channel — Meta only, ~10 creatives, no negotiated payment terms, inventory bought on personal loans, since neither Wayflyer-type lenders nor Shopify Capital existed yet at that time). By 40 (8 years later): $13M in total distributions taken from his companies, while also using his own balance sheet to acquire 3 additional companies. He attributes the turnaround fundamentally to the BOM-driven approach described in this playbook.
- Cross-border logistics (self-interested example — Roman is an investor/promoter of the named provider): reports it is cheaper for him to ship US orders from Hong Kong via Quinc Logistics than to fulfill end-to-end domestically from Dallas for Linear, averaging ~4.5 days to a US customer.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Not having a BOM at all (the source's own experience: ~99 of 100 founders it deals with in M&A had none).
- Treating factory cost as one lump line instead of breaking it into components, sub-vendors, labor and overhead — this hides exactly where money and negotiating room are.
- Entering a supplier relationship without redundancy or leverage prepared in advance, so the factory holds most of the power once the relationship is set.
- Negotiating cost-downs as a pure squeeze rather than a shared-growth story — the source says suppliers respond better to a credible growth narrative than to pressure alone.
- Confusing volume price breaks with blanket discounts; if the "credit after threshold" mechanism isn't written down and reconciled, it may never actually apply.
- Ignoring sub-vendor-level economies of scale that a factory earns on your behalf but does not automatically pass through.
- Not tracking country-of-origin/tariff exposure until a shock hits, instead of building it into the BOM from the start.
- Pushing growth into a broken-tariff or broken-margin environment instead of deliberately pausing until economics are fixed.
- Confusing the payment terms written on an invoice with the actual cash timing that governs your float — the real cash mechanism can differ from the paper terms.
- Ignoring inventory/SKU-mix ordering mistakes (e.g. wrong size mix) that quietly consume float the nominal terms-minus-lead-time formula assumes you have.
- Treating a factory-financing rate at face value without expressing it as an annualized, revenue-relative cost and comparing it to alternatives.
- Roman's own founding-era mistake, told as a cautionary example: growth-at-all-costs on a single acquisition channel, with no BOM and no negotiated terms, funded by personal loans — led directly to his −$3M net worth at 32.
- Overpaying for a specific cost line (packaging, in the source's example) simply because it was never run through a BOM.
