## 1. What this source is for

A single presenter's soup-to-nuts walkthrough of physical-product sourcing for e-commerce: locking product specs before contacting a factory, finding and vetting suppliers, understanding true landed cost and negotiating early runs, choosing freight and running pre-shipment QC, picking a 3PL, and setting reorder points so stock doesn't run out. It is written as a personal-experience narrative (five "chapters") rather than a manual, so procedures are described in prose, not checklists, and several numbers are anecdotal rather than benchmarked.

## 2. Principles

1. **Lock product specs before any supplier contact.** Condition: any new product, or a reorder that has shown inconsistency. Evidence: presenter's own blanket shipment arrived in three different shades of gray and different weights because no spec sheet was sent. [aa2d7527a54d, b760f0e0abc2]
2. **Treat Alibaba as a research and shortlisting tool, not a decision.** Condition: sourcing new suppliers for a new product or category. Evidence: opinion, framed as "totally fine" for pricing/materials/MOQ research but explicitly not a final-decision tool. [f8417709361e]
3. **The sample progression is a trust-and-reliability test as much as a quality check.** Condition: choosing between final candidate factories and before green-lighting bulk. Evidence: opinion — presenter says to watch who follows instructions and sends updates unprompted, not just what the sample looks like. [8254bc7180c2]
4. **Landed cost, not the factory quote, is the only number that matters for margin decisions.** Condition: any pricing, ad-spend, or go/no-go decision on a product. Evidence: presenter's dog bed business — an assumed-profitable margin (~60%) fell to about 20% once freight, duties, insurance, warehousing, and fulfillment fees were added. [c52be45b5fec]
5. **In the first few production runs, flexibility beats the lowest unit price.** Condition: negotiating your first 1-2 production orders. Evidence: opinion/anecdote — founders who saved about $2/unit ended up with warehouses of unsold stock. [acd555dedcb7]
6. **Freight choice should be hybrid and planned backward from lead time, not chosen on price alone.** Condition: moving any finished production run from factory to warehouse. Evidence: presenter's stated transit-day ranges and the warning that the cheapest forwarder may leave goods sitting at port to fill a container. [6e280217d1d8]
7. **A pre-shipment QC inspection is treated as effectively mandatory.** Condition: any container about to leave the factory. Evidence: illustrative cost/benefit ($200 to catch problems worth up to $20,000), and the claim that once goods are on the water, problems cannot be fixed. [6356c16830fd]
8. **3PL selection must be evaluated on documented performance (accuracy, speed, communication, scalability), not price or promises.** Condition: choosing or replacing a 3PL, or stress-testing a current one ahead of a demand peak. Evidence: presenter's own Q4 3PL collapse during a forecast $45-50M sales period, which cost "tens of millions" in lost revenue and drew public complaints. [60146e443b29]
9. **The reorder point formula ties replenishment to actual sales velocity and total lead time, not to gut feel.** Condition: setting a reorder trigger for any SKU. Evidence: stated formula plus one fully worked numeric example. [8bf409e6c3d5]
10. **Forecasting must run as a weekly discipline, not a one-time calculation, regardless of team size.** Condition: any operator, solo or team, running a sourced-product business. 11. **Treat a good supplier as a long-term partner, but keep IP (tech packs, artwork, source files) on your own side regardless.** Condition: after a final supplier is selected, and especially once at volume. Evidence: opinion. [a09ac2dd3ea0]
12. **A structured quote request (not just "what's your price") makes suppliers comparable and gives negotiating leverage.** Condition: after a shortlist exists, before ordering samples. Evidence: opinion. [e929a7dceb46]

## 3. Procedures

### A. Build the tech pack (before contacting any supplier)
1. Draft one document defining exactly how the product must be built, from materials through packaging.
2. Include: product drawings/photos from every angle; exact materials and finishes including GSM, trims and hardware; measurements for every size/variant; colorways with Pantone codes; label, packaging and care instructions; a clear product name, version and date.
3. If you are not a designer, hire one. Source's example: an experienced product designer on Upwork can build a tech pack for about $200. `[web check: current Upwork rates for tech pack design]`
4. Maintain the spec in a living document (Figma, Canva or Notion templates named as options) so every reorder points at the same version.
[b760f0e0abc2, aa2d7527a54d]

### B. Shortlist and vet suppliers on Alibaba
1. Search by exact product type; filter to verified suppliers and trade assurance. `[verify UI]`
2. Message at least 10-15 factories. Expect drop-off; you are testing responsiveness, professionalism, and prior experience making similar products.
3. Get shortlisted factories on a video call. Ask them to walk through the facility or show sample products.
4. Watch for red flags on the call: vague answers, poor communication, evidence of operating from a home/living room.
5. Verify: how long the factory has been active, its export countries, and request ISO or BSCI certification documents.
6. If scaling, consider a sourcing agent or platform that physically inspects factories for you (named in the source as "Quimma" and "Easy IMAX" — spelling unconfirmed). `[web check: confirm real platform names]`
7. Consider diversifying outside China (Vietnam, India, Turkey named) only once the business is at "the right size" — the source gives no explicit revenue/order-volume threshold for this. `[needs brand data: current order volume/maturity vs. this undefined threshold]`
[f8417709361e]

### C. Request a structured quote
1. Send the tech pack to each shortlisted factory.
2. Request, together, not just unit price: unit cost per SKU/variant; MOQ per SKU; lead time to manufacture (days); packaging details/specs; payment terms.
3. Compare shortlisted suppliers on identical specs, quantities and delivery terms.
[e929a7dceb46]

### D. Run the sample progression
1. Order samples from every supplier still in consideration — this tests communication/reliability as much as product quality.
2. Strike-off / small test sample: approve materials and prints first.
3. Pre-production sample: must match the tech pack exactly. Treat this as the gold-standard reference for bulk comparison. For apparel, get multiple pre-production samples here to confirm size grading (S/M/L proportions).
4. Keep one approved sample in-house and leave one at the factory as their reference copy.
5. After selecting the final supplier, order a production sample built immediately before mass production.
6. If the production sample does not match the pre-production standard, do not proceed to bulk. (Source presents this as a hard stop, with no stated exception.)
7. Once at volume: sign a manufacturing agreement and register trademarks. Keep original tech packs, artwork and source files on your own side throughout, not the factory's.
[8254bc7180c2, a09ac2dd3ea0]

### E. Build the landed-cost view
1. Do not use the factory's quote (the "X-factory price") as a margin number — it covers physical production only.
2. Build a spreadsheet covering: product cost, freight, duties/taxes, insurance, warehousing, packaging, fulfillment fees, platform fees, transaction/payment-transfer fees, and (if you want landed cost to the customer) courier fees.
3. Source's own shortcut: prompt a general AI chatbot (referred to in the transcript as "Chat TBT," almost certainly ChatGPT) to list the hidden cost categories, then fill in a template. Verify every category against real invoices before trusting it.
4. Run this math before manufacturing, not after — the source says even larger companies skip this step and rush from design straight into production.
5. Use the resulting landed cost to inform pricing, ad spend, offer strategy and supplier negotiation.
Note: the source never walks a single product through a full numeric landed-cost total (unlike the reorder-point example below) — it lists categories and one hypothetical retail-price collapse, not a worked total. Treat this procedure as a checklist of cost lines, not a formula with sample numbers.
[c52be45b5fec]

### F. Negotiate early production runs
1. In the first 1-2 runs, accept a slightly higher per-unit cost in exchange for flexibility to test, pivot and resize orders.
2. Use the early runs to learn: sell-through speed, quality consistency, and where delays happen.
3. Once you have that data, negotiate with it — share your forecast and demonstrate you're serious about scaling.
4. Get a competing quote or sample from at least one other supplier to keep negotiation honest, but do not squeeze a supplier to the point of retaliation (cheaper materials, skipped QC, slower timelines).
[acd555dedcb7]

### G. Choose freight and plan lead time backward
1. Pick air for small/urgent batches or small products; sea for larger or heavier items and for scaled restocking.
2. Most brands the source describes hybridize: air a small first batch to start selling, then sea-restock at lower cost.
3. Plan backward from total lead time: sea transit 30-40 days door-to-door, air 7-10 days, plus roughly 20-30 days of production time. Start planning the next shipment the moment the current one lands.
4. Use a freight forwarder for container booking, customs and delivery coordination. Do not default to the cheapest forwarder quote — it may leave goods waiting at port to fill a container.
[6e280217d1d8]

### H. Book pre-shipment QC
1. Before the container leaves the factory, book a third-party QC inspection (~$200 in the source's example). `[web check: current inspection provider rates]`
2. Have the agent test random units, check measurements, verify packaging/labeling, check carton weight, and photograph everything, flagging even small inconsistencies.
3. If problems are found, the factory fixes them before departure — this is the last point at which that is possible.
4. Approve or reject the shipment based on the QC report; release final payment only once it passes.
[6356c16830fd]

### I. Select and manage a 3PL
1. Evaluate on: accuracy (order error rate), speed (peak-volume processing and how they achieve it), communication (a named direct contact for problems), scalability (documented handling of prior Q4 surges for other brands).
2. Ask how they handle returns, damages and restocks, and whether they flag issues proactively.
3. Visit the warehouse in person before deciding; after touring two or three, the source says the real operators become apparent.
4. Confirm integration with your stack (source names Shopify and warehouse management tools as common integrations).
5. Once running: track inventory turnover weekly, keep a shared dashboard with the warehouse, and hold monthly reviews with the 3PL.
[60146e443b29]

### J. Run the weekly forecasting/reorder rhythm
1. Track daily sales velocity from order history (source names Shopify specifically), watching for slow movers and seasonal spikes.
2. Track total lead time (payment/PO release to usable stock at warehouse/3PL), including production, freight and clearance.
3. Set a safety-stock buffer sized to your own spike/delay risk (source gives no formula for sizing this beyond "your insurance buffer").
4. Compute: Reorder Point = (Daily Sales Velocity x Total Lead Time in days) + Safety Stock.
5. Review this weekly; communicate stock/forecast position to suppliers before problems appear, not after.
6. If possible, put one person on operations/planning specifically rather than splitting the role across other duties.
[8bf409e6c3d5, 94c5d797f80b]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Supplier video call:** ask to see the facility or sample products. Bad signs: vague answers, poor communication, visibly operating from a home/living room. Good signs: clear answers, prior experience with similar products, professional setup. Next step if bad: drop from shortlist.
- **Supplier verification:** check years active, export countries, and request ISO/BSCI certification documents. No certification or refusal to provide -> treat as a red flag, do not proceed without one.
- **Sample stage:** at each stage (strike-off, pre-production, production) check for exact match to the tech pack. Good: matches on materials, measurements, colorways, packaging. Bad: any deviation at the production-sample stage -> do not proceed to bulk (source states no exception).
- **Pre-shipment QC report:** check random units, measurements, packaging/labeling, carton weight, with photos of everything. Good: passes with no material inconsistencies -> approve shipment and release final payment. Bad: defects found -> factory fixes before the container leaves; never approve once goods are already on the water.
- **3PL evaluation:** ask their order error rate, peak-season processing speed and method, named direct contact, and track record through prior Q4 surges. Good: documented answers and a warehouse visit that confirms them. Bad: price/promises only, no named contact, no surge history -> the source's own $45-50M Q4 failure is the cautionary case.
- **Weekly forecasting check:** review daily sales velocity, lead time and current stock position every week (source names Shopify order history as the data source). Bad: no one weekly-reviewing this -> source's own stated failure mode ("we failed to plan at all") led to a near-two-month stockout.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Contacting a factory before specs are locked, leaving the factory to guess (and take shortcuts) on materials, colors, measurements and packaging.
- Clicking the first professional-looking Alibaba listing instead of shortlisting and vetting 10-15 candidates.
- Treating the factory's X-factory quote as if it were the final unit economics.
- Rushing from design straight into manufacturing without checking whether the numbers (cost vs. target price) work at all.
- Chasing the lowest possible unit cost on early runs and ending up with cash trapped in unsold inventory.
- Squeezing a supplier too hard on price/terms, which the source says gets made up elsewhere (cheaper materials, skipped QC, slower timelines).
- Choosing a freight forwarder purely on the cheapest rate, risking goods sitting at port for weeks.
- Letting a container leave without a pre-shipment QC inspection — once goods are on the water, defects can no longer be fixed.
- Choosing a 3PL on price or promises alone without checking documented accuracy, speed, communication and Q4-surge history, or without visiting the warehouse.
- Treating forecasting as an afterthought, or having no one inside the business responsible for reviewing lead times and stock levels regularly.
- Letting the factory be the sole holder of tech packs, artwork or source files.
