## 1. What this source is for

Helps an operator either start a sourcing agency from zero or run product sourcing for their own ecommerce brand. Covers how to land a first paying client cheaply, how to negotiate with factories without a written contract advantage, how to write specs so a factory cannot misinterpret them, how to vet a new factory with samples before committing to a launch, and how to decide which country to manufacture in when diversifying away from China.

## 2. Principles

1. **Leverage comes from visible alternatives, not from silence.** Telling your current factory that you are actively quoting competitors, and showing them the competing price, makes the factory act like an A-level supplier because it fears losing the account. Condition: applies when you have at least one other vetted factory that could actually take the business (a bluff without a real backup is weaker). Evidence: presenter's stated approach, no data given, explicitly framed as "my opinion." [3d269be19e27]
2. **Every decision you leave open in a spec is a decision the factory will make for you, and they will often make it wrong.** Vague terms like "centered" fail because the factory does not share your mental model, and centering is ambiguous by axis (centered left-right vs. top-bottom). Condition: applies to every dimension, color reference and imprint location in a tech pack, not just placement. Evidence: presenter's stated production rule, reinforced by an internal ~80-step production checklist his team runs. [4047ed4ac497]
3. **A physical reference sample is the fastest way to specify quality a client already trusts.** When a client loves an existing product but finds it too expensive, sending that exact physical item to an overseas factory to reverse-engineer removes the ambiguity of a written spec alone. Condition: works when you (or the client) hold an authorized reference item; the source flags IP or trademark risk if that item is a branded product. Evidence: presenter's stated sourcing tactic. [3fe60e173a5f]
4. **Never pitch a factory-made product to a buyer before you have inspected a physical sample from that specific factory.** A factory's word on quality is not enough; samples are the mandatory gate before pricing or listing commitments. Condition: applies to any new factory or new product variation, even one similar to a previously approved SKU. Evidence: presenter's stated production rule, presented as how his agency avoids selling low-quality product. [101cb1f841d9]
5. **Choosing a sourcing country is a trade-off between lead time and cost basis, evaluated across four factors.** The four factors named are: where the raw material comes from, labor cost, overall taxes, and where the country sits on its development trajectory. Condition: applies when relocating or diversifying manufacturing away from a single country. Evidence: presenter's framework, illustrated with one worked example (Egypt), not a measured study across many countries. [a0d9c84c5f13]
6. **The scarce, valuable skill in sourcing is finding the best factory in a category, not finding a factory at all.** Anyone can filter Alibaba to a "decent" product; building a curated shortlist of best-in-class vetted factories per category is what lets an agency (or a brand) plug in new clients or SKUs quickly and is a defensible moat. Condition: this is a multi-year asset, not something a beginner can shortcut. Evidence: presenter's stated business model and his own description of curating factories as his agency's core differentiator. [f759ab73c73c]

## 3. Procedures

**A. Start a sourcing agency from zero (corporate swag entry point)**
1. Target local businesses that already buy branded merchandise (t-shirts, water bottles, hats, giveaways/swag) — low volume, low risk, a comfortable first category.
2. Ask the prospect what they currently pay and for what products (e.g., "tell me the products you're making right now and let me price it out for you").
3. Most businesses buy through a domestic supplier network (a US warehouse that stocks bulk product, decorates it, and delivers), not factory-direct.
4. Quote the same product sourced factory-direct (e.g., via Alibaba). The source's example: a domestic supplier charges $12 for a hat; the same hat factory-direct on Alibaba costs about $3; sell it to the client around $7 — undercutting the domestic price by roughly 30% while still taking a healthy margin.
5. Pitch on price to win the first clients — the presenter says this is not his preferred long-term sales style, but calls it "the best way" specifically for getting started.
6. Deliver reliably on this low-stakes category to build comfort managing sourcing and delivery end to end, then grow into bigger corporate accounts, then into ecommerce brand clients (a multi-year progression, not a fast jump). [web check: current Alibaba factory-direct pricing and filtering interface, since platform UI and typical price gaps change]

**B. Negotiate and hold leverage with an existing factory**
1. Line up at least one other vetted factory (or agent) for each core product before you need it — a real backup, not just a claim.
2. Tell your current factory directly that you are getting quotes elsewhere.
3. Share the competing price where useful, framed as market reality rather than a threat.
4. Use that leverage to push on price and quality at the same time.
5. Re-quote periodically even with happy, long-term suppliers to keep pricing honest. [3d269be19e27]

**C. Write a spec sheet / tech pack that a factory cannot misread**
1. For every measurement, decoration placement, or color reference, write an exact number, never a descriptive word.
2. Example given: instead of "centered," write "4 inches from the top, 4 inches from the bottom, 2 inches from the side, 2 inches from the side."
3. Repeat this exactness for every dimension and imprint location in the document — leave the factory zero decisions to make on your behalf.
4. Route the finished spec through a checklist-style production process (the source's internal version has about 80 checkpoints the team must tick off) before the run ships. [4047ed4ac497]

**D. Vet a new factory before committing a client's product**
1. Get the client's current, approved reference sample in hand (the actual physical product they already buy and like).
2. Send that reference sample plus written specs (materials, dimensions, tolerances) to the candidate factory.
3. Have the factory produce its own sample attempting to replicate it.
4. Compare the factory's sample against the original reference sample directly.
5. Only after that comparison passes, commit to pricing, bulk production, or a launch date to the client. [3fe60e173a5f] [101cb1f841d9]

**E. Decide where to manufacture (diversify out of China)**
1. List candidate countries. Source's named examples: Vietnam, Cambodia, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Egypt, Pakistan, and other parts of Northern Africa.
2. For each candidate, assess: raw material origin, labor cost, overall taxes, and the country's development trajectory.
3. Run the trade-off as a lead-time-vs-cost calculation. Worked example: moving to Egypt was estimated to increase lead time by about 30% while decreasing cost basis by about 30%.
4. Decide which factor (speed or cost) matters more for that specific product — the source states his own lean toward cost basis, but calls it his call, not a universal rule.
5. Build sourcing contingencies across more than one region so a single country's disruption (the source cites COVID exposing this) does not break the whole supply chain. [a0d9c84c5f13]

**F. Win inbound clients through organic content (agency growth, not a generic marketing framework)**
1. Early education-focused content underperformed; the presenter says it took "a while of trial and error" to find a format that worked.
2. The format that worked: a series showing a popular product and what it actually costs to make.
3. Once a working series format was found, they repeated it rather than diversifying content types.
4. Later added more formats once the base was working: travel content (factory visits in Vietnam, China), long-form YouTube.
5. Reported result at time of interview: about 120,000 followers gained organically in one month, and roughly 300 sourcing inquiries per month, of which the agency takes on only 1 to 2 clients. ## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Backup-supplier check.** Ask: do I have at least one other vetted factory for this core product, right now, not hypothetically? If no, the "leverage" talk with your current factory is a bluff and carries relationship risk if called. [3d269be19e27]
- **Spec-sheet check.** Scan the tech pack for any descriptive word standing in for a number ("centered," "about," "similar to"). Any such word is a place the factory will guess. Replace it with an exact measurement before sending. [4047ed4ac497]
- **New-factory-sample check.** Before quoting a client or committing a launch date: do you have a physical sample from *this* factory (not a sister factory, not the same factory's other product)? Does it match the client's approved reference sample on the attributes the client actually cares about? If either answer is no, do not commit to pricing or a date. [3fe60e173a5f] [101cb1f841d9]
- **Country-diversification check.** For a candidate country, can you state: where does the raw material come from, what is labor cost, what are overall taxes, and where is this country on its development trajectory? If you cannot answer all four, the lead-time/cost trade-off estimate is not yet reliable. [a0d9c84c5f13] [web check: current labor cost, tax and lead-time data for the specific candidate country, since the source gives one illustrative example (Egypt) and figures move over time]
- **Factory-ownership temptation check.** Before buying into or opening a factory: are you prepared to live on-site, and do you have a specific engineering/quality gap (like the presenter's father's power-inverter quality problem) that off-the-shelf factories cannot solve? If not, the source says most people who buy factories on impulse "have failed miserably." [needs brand data: whether a genuine, unmet quality gap exists for your product before considering this path]

## 7. Examples and case results

- **Fan-selling origin story (example).** Presenter sold a $5 phone fan gadget at a college party, then took a bulk order of 2,000 units with a client's logo at 20% off, netting about $6,000 — the moment he says he realized selling to businesses beat selling to individual students.
- **Hat pricing example (example).** A domestic supplier charges a business $12/hat; the same hat sourced factory-direct on Alibaba costs about $3; sold to the client around $7, framed as roughly 30% cheaper than the domestic price while still profitable.
- **Egypt trade-off (example, illustrative).** Moving a product's manufacturing to Egypt: lead time up ~30%, cost basis down ~30%. Presented as a case to reason through, not a guaranteed outcome for any given product.
- **Third/fourth year of the agency, COVID (example).** Ecommerce-brand friends' supply chains broke during COVID while their ad-driven sales kept growing; they called the presenter to take over entire supply chains. Presenter frames this as the hardest sourcing period he'd faced but says the agency handled it successfully, and that afterward brands started asking for built-in contingencies as standard.
- **Hong Kong contact (secondhand anecdote, unverified).** Presenter describes someone who started sourcing at a similar age, bought minority stakes in headwear factories, landed clients like New Era, and now reportedly runs "hundreds of millions of dollars a year" in sourcing business, with an "$80 million house in Hong Kong." No source or verification given; explicitly framed by the presenter as something he's heard/knows secondhand.
- **Presenter's father's factory (example).** Opened an electronics factory in China specifically to solve a quality problem (highest-quality power inverters) that he could not get from existing Chinese suppliers for his own ecommerce brand, in order to win big-brand licensing deals.
- **Bali manufacturing (example, mixed).** The presenter says Bali is good for testing fabrics and small runs (cites access to fabric markets and skilled tailors) but calls it "slow, expensive, hard to scale" for real production, because it's an island (most fabric still comes from China anyway), has higher relative labor costs, and heavy holiday downtime. He says he is currently helping about 8 friends with Bali-based brands move manufacturing out, mostly to China.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Assuming your current factory doesn't need a backup or competing quote — the source says this is exactly when a factory is most likely to take advantage of you. [3d269be19e27]
- Being afraid that shopping other quotes will damage the factory relationship — the source argues the opposite is true (silence is the risk, not competition). [3d269be19e27]
- Using vague descriptive language ("centered") in a spec sheet or tech pack instead of exact measurements. [4047ed4ac497]
- Letting a factory make any placement, dimension, or color decision you didn't specify. [4047ed4ac497]
- Pitching a client on a factory-made product before getting and inspecting a physical sample from that exact factory. [3fe60e173a5f] [101cb1f841d9]
- Trusting a factory's word on quality instead of a sample in hand. [101cb1f841d9]
- Treating a good sample as a guarantee of good bulk production quality (the source flags this as a false sense of security, not a stated failure case). [101cb1f841d9]
- Reverse-engineering a client's reference sample without checking IP/trademark exposure if the original is a branded product. [3fe60e173a5f]
- A beginner (or anyone without a specific, unmet quality/engineering gap) buying into or opening a factory — the source says most people he knows who did this "have failed miserably" and burned themselves financially.
- Basing manufacturing scale-up in Bali long-term — good for early testing and small runs, poor for scaling (slow, expensive, holiday-heavy labor market, most raw material still imported from China anyway).
- Assuming Alibaba-filtered sourcing gets you the *best* product in a category — the source says it only gets you a decent/mediocre one; best-in-class requires the years-long curated shortlist work. [f759ab73c73c]

- Selling on price to win a first client is presented as good advice for beginners specifically, while the presenter says elsewhere that price-led selling "isn't how I love to sell" long-term — a stated internal tension, not a contradiction to silently resolve.
- Country-selection guidance (section 5, procedure E) gives one framework and one worked example (Egypt) but no data for Vietnam, Cambodia, Sri Lanka, Thailand, or Pakistan beyond naming them as destinations the agency uses. Numbers for those countries are missing from this source. [web check: labor cost, lead time and tax comparisons for Vietnam, Cambodia, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Egypt, Pakistan]
