# What I learnt from sourcing e-commerce products from China

## 1. What this source is for
Helps an operator who is moving from small dropshipping-style batches to bulk sourcing from a Chinese supplier via Alibaba: how far unit cost can fall with volume, how to plan for defects, how to choose freight for heavy items, how to vet a cheaper supplier without breaking the current one, how to keep suppliers reachable, and how to pay safely. It is a lessons-learned narrative, not a step-by-step manual.

## 2. Principles
1. Ordering more volume lowers per-unit cost, sometimes sharply, but the discount curve should be checked across several quote tiers rather than assumed from one quote. Condition: negotiating a bulk order. Evidence: author's item went from $7.80 (weekly 500-unit batches) to quotes ranging $4-$10 depending on volume, down to $2.00 at 10K units. [51903576b417]
2. Alibaba does not perform quality control for the buyer the way Amazon does; the buyer must inspect before shipping to customers. Condition: any bulk Alibaba order. Evidence: author's 10K-unit order returned roughly 300 defective units. [bd4fa1a7ea61]
3. Do not switch your whole order to a cheaper supplier at once; validate with a small test batch first. Condition: a competing supplier quotes materially lower. Evidence: a 500-unit test order at ~20% lower price came back mostly defective. [28b7e903d4ef]
4. Pay through Alibaba's own payment gateway (Trade Assurance) rather than PayPal or a direct/off-platform transfer. Condition: any supplier payment. Evidence: author calls it "seamless" with a "great grievance redressal system"; a commenter directly disputes this (see section 9). [626f4be665de]
5. Secure a direct communication channel (WhatsApp, WeChat, email) with the supplier in addition to Alibaba chat. Condition: as soon as first contact is made. Evidence: author states suppliers "will go offline without prior notice" on native Alibaba chat. [d923141a8806]
6. Match shipping method to item weight/bulk and to how far ahead of the selling season the order is placed. Condition: heavy or bulky items, or an order placed close to peak season (Q4 cited). Evidence: a 1.2 kg stainless-steel item drew a ~$2,000 air-cargo quote (rejected as too expensive with Q4 near) versus a ~1-month sea-freight option. [e3609fde578c]
7. Treat the first supplier quote as an opening position and negotiate. Condition: any bulk quote. Evidence: author negotiated $6.00 down to $4.80 per unit, saving roughly $3,500 per order at their volume. [c7f05c514633]
8. On large orders with an established supplier relationship, ask for premium courier (FedEx/UPS) instead of default ePacket. Condition: large order plus a good relationship. Evidence: supplier upgraded a large order from ePacket (10-14 days) to FedEx (4-5 days to California). [e3609fde578c]
9. Sourcing agents can reach the manufacturer directly, arrange customisation and sometimes get better prices than trading-company quotes, but must be vetted; the source gives no concrete vetting criteria. Condition: quoted prices look inflated, or customisation is needed. Evidence: author's own blanket statement "use a better sourcing agent," with no criteria given. [0b6b46366bee]
10. (Commenter's view, not the main author's) Unused volume or freight-consolidation discounts can sometimes be redirected into a cash-flow-friendly structure instead of taken as a straight price cut. Condition: a recurring/replenishment order where the full volume tier would strain cash flow. Evidence: commenter's own client example (see section 7). [b2b02a1e47e6]

## 3. Procedures
The source is mostly narrative advice, not a numbered how-to. It gives only two reconstructable sequences; everything else in this playbook is stated as a principle or rule, not a procedure, because the source is thin on how.

**A. Getting a real volume-price picture** (reconstructed from the narrative, not literally numbered in the source):
1. Confirm the SKU already sells at a healthy margin before scaling (author's case: $7.80 cost / $25.99 retail from 500-unit weekly batches).
2. Post Request For Quotation (RFQ) listings on Alibaba for the same product. [verify UI: RFQ posting flow not described in the source]
3. Compare quotes across volume tiers rather than accepting the first one (source saw $4-$10 depending on volume, $2.00 at 10K units).
4. Weigh the lower unit cost against the cash tied up and the defect risk of the larger order before committing.
[51903576b417]

**B. Testing a new, cheaper supplier before switching:**
1. Keep the incumbent supplier active; do not cancel or reduce them yet.
2. Place a small test order with the new supplier (source's example: 500 units).
3. Inspect the full test batch before scaling further.
4. Only shift volume to the new supplier once its defect rate is proven comparable to the incumbent's.
[28b7e903d4ef]

No procedural detail is given for posting payment through Trade Assurance, choosing a freight carrier, or vetting a sourcing agent — the source states the conclusion, not the steps. ## 5. Diagnostics and checks
- Check: is the SKU's margin already healthy before scaling to bulk? How: compare current cost to retail (author's case: $7.80 vs $25.99). Good: wide margin supports the cash lock-up of a bulk order. Bad: thin margin means the volume discount may not offset the risk. Next: get RFQs at several tiers before committing. [51903576b417]
- Check: defect rate on a bulk shipment. How: inspect the full shipment yourself, or pay a third-party inspector (~$200 cited). Good: defect rate at or below the buffer ordered. Bad: defects exceed the buffer. Next: the source's own case absorbed the loss via the buffer rather than returning goods, because it judged returns not worth the time — decide case by case. [bd4fa1a7ea61]
- Check: a new/cheaper supplier's real defect rate before switching. How: run a small test batch (source's example: 500 units) and inspect it fully. Good: defect rate comparable to the incumbent's. Bad: high defects (source's case: "most" defective). Next: do not migrate volume; keep the incumbent as primary. [28b7e903d4ef]
- Check: is the supplier reachable when it matters? How: try the direct WhatsApp/WeChat/email channel, not only Alibaba chat. Good: responsive. Bad: supplier goes dark on Alibaba chat without notice (stated as a known pattern). Next: escalate on the direct channel. The source does not say what to do if a supplier refuses to give a direct contact — treat that refusal itself as a diligence flag.
- Check: item weight/size and remaining lead time before choosing freight. How: weigh sea freight's ~1-month transit against air cargo's cost (~$2,000 in the source's case) against how close you are to your selling season. Good: enough lead time to use sea freight. Bad: a rush order close to peak season forcing an expensive air-cargo choice. Next: order heavy or bulky items well ahead of the season. [e3609fde578c]
- Check: payment route before sending funds. How: confirm the order and payment both run through Alibaba's gateway/Trade Assurance rather than PayPal or a direct wire. Good: specifications, communications and payment are all on-platform, per the author's stated preference. Bad: paying off-platform for a discount, as the commenter proposes, drops the platform's grievance system. Next: weigh the direct conflict in section 9 against your own risk tolerance and experience level. [626f4be665de]

## 7. Examples and case results
All figures below are single-case results from the anonymous author (a US jewellery seller) except the last, marked as the commenter's.
- Jewellery item: cost fell from $7.80 (weekly 500-unit Aliexpress batches) to as low as $2.00 at a 10K-unit Alibaba order; quotes across tiers ranged $4-$10. [51903576b417]
- Same 10K-unit order: about 300 units arrived defective; the author judged returning them to the supplier not worth the time and absorbed the loss instead. [bd4fa1a7ea61]
- A competing supplier quoted about 20% less; a 500-unit test order came back mostly defective, so the author kept the incumbent. [28b7e903d4ef]
- Kitchen-product example: a 1.2 kg stainless-steel item drew a ~$2,000 air-cargo quote, rejected as too expensive with Q4 approaching; sea freight (~1-month transit) was chosen instead. [e3609fde578c]
- Direct negotiation dropped per-unit cost from $6.00 to $4.80, saving roughly $3,500 per order at the author's volume. [c7f05c514633]
- A good supplier relationship on a large order got the author upgraded from ePacket (10-14 days) to FedEx (4-5 days to California). [e3609fde578c]
- Commenter's example (not the main author, unverified): a client reordering the same SKU several times a year at ~$10 landed cost. The commenter added their own capital to increase that order to a full year's supply, unlocking the volume discount while the client kept paying $10/unit; the client received the full year's stock and only paid the commenter for units as they were consumed, removing lead time and MOQ pressure and improving the client's cash flow. [b2b02a1e47e6]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about
- Assuming Alibaba does quality control the way Amazon does; it does not, and the buyer must inspect. [bd4fa1a7ea61]
- Ordering the exact quantity needed with no buffer for defects. [bd4fa1a7ea61]
- Switching your whole order to a cheaper supplier at once, without a test batch. [28b7e903d4ef]
- Relying only on Alibaba's native chat for time-sensitive communication. [d923141a8806]
- Committing to a heavy or bulky item late in the season and being forced into expensive air cargo. [e3609fde578c]
- Accepting the first quoted price without negotiating. [c7f05c514633]
- Paying via PayPal, WeChat or a direct transfer instead of Alibaba's payment gateway (the author's stated position; a commenter disagrees — see section 9). [626f4be665de]
- Using a sourcing agent without vetting them (source only warns to "use a better" one, with no specific criteria). [0b6b46366bee]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing
- Direct conflict on payment method: the author says to always use Alibaba's Trade Assurance/payment gateway and avoid PayPal, WeChat or direct transfer. A commenter (self-described US-based sourcing agent/contract manufacturer with a manufacturing and engineering background) says the Trade Assurance grievance system "is a joke" in their own experience and that they would rather pay by direct transfer (TT) and negotiate a discount, since the supplier then avoids the platform fee — but they attribute their own comfort with this to having sourcing expertise and confidence in the specific suppliers they choose. This is not resolved in the source; a buyer without that background should weigh the author's more conservative default more heavily. [626f4be665de]
- The 10% defect buffer is the author's own practice from one 10K-unit order (300 defects observed), not stated as an industry formula, and is not tied to product category, material or a specific supplier's track record. [bd4fa1a7ea61]
- No concrete vetting checklist is given for choosing a sourcing agent, only "use a better sourcing agent." [0b6b46366bee]
- The claim that ePacket ended "back in 2019, right after COVID" is the author's own recollection, not sourced to an official notice. [web check: current China-US small-parcel shipping options]
