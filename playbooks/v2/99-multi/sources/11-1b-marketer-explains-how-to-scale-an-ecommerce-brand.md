## 1. What this source is for

A single free-flowing podcast interview, not a structured course. It gives one agency owner's mental model for the two numbers he says decide whether a brand can scale paid traffic profitably (a conversion-rate-times-AOV screen, and 12-month lifetime gross profit versus Day-1 CAC), one aggressive merchandising tactic for lifting AOV by gating a hero SKU, a short but concrete description of an AI-creative and AI-automation workflow, and a fuller-than-usual treatment of agency/team operations (channel-based org structure, SOP compliance, hiring entrepreneurial staff, and a company-wide AI-automation intake process). It is most useful for an operator deciding what to prioritize before scaling paid spend, and for anyone setting up an internal AI/automation function. It is thin on execution detail almost everywhere: no landing-page copy or wireframes, no statistical method for the ad-angle test, no walkthrough of any software's actual interface. It gives the model and one supporting example per idea, not a manual.

## 3. Procedures

Grouped by domain so a folder merge can route each part to the matching module playbook.

### Product

1. Before pivoting a struggling product or audience, confirm real effort has actually been made: ads run, UGC produced, an email backend built, the site optimized. Eddie's illustrative horizon is about two years — an example, not a hard cutoff. `[8bbceb08f413]`
2. Diagnose which of two problems you have: an unappealing product, or selling to the wrong person. Eddie's framing: if it's genuinely hard to get someone to want your product, one of the two is true. `[8bbceb08f413]`
3. If you suspect the wrong audience, test multiple distinct demographics with multiple angles. If friction persists across all of them, treat it as a product-market-fit problem, not a messaging problem, and do more customer research before launching another product. `[8bbceb08f413]`
4. To sharpen positioning, layer the demographic definition step by step (e.g., "fat burners" → "for women" → "for women over 40") until the messaging reads as unmistakably built for one reader. Check the resulting segment is still large and has real purchasing power before locking it in. `[ff32df14c8b0]` `[8bbceb08f413]`
5. Pull the narrower positioning from real signals — customer interviews, reviews, returns, purchase data — not guesses. Eddie's example: reviews and purchase data on a protein brand showed ~90% female buyers and tumbler use, even though ads and PDP imagery targeted everyone with a shaker bottle. `[a335ae58c3ad]` `[ff32df14c8b0]`

### Offers

1. Identify a hero SKU from sales data: find the single item responsible for a disproportionate share of revenue. Eddie's case: ~40% of an apparel brand's revenue came from one hat. `[fd5777d14d59]` `[7555f8b740ac]`
2. Set a gift-unlock threshold roughly 30-40% above current AOV. Eddie's case: AOV ~$65 → threshold ~$85. `[fd5777d14d59]`
3. Disable direct purchase of that SKU entirely; it becomes obtainable only as a free gift once the cart crosses the threshold. `[fd5777d14d59]` `[7555f8b740ac]`
4. Display the item at its normal retail price marked "sold out" so shoppers register its value, while your actual landed cost stays low. Eddie's case: shown at $35-40 retail, actual cost ~$2-2.50. `[7555f8b740ac]`
5. Promote the gift everywhere — ad messaging, homepage/site banner, cart — not only a checkout progress bar most shoppers never see. `[7555f8b740ac]`
6. Before shipping this, re-check unit economics: the giveaway's cost must be covered by the incremental AOV and margin on everything else added to the cart, and the item must be genuinely coveted — Eddie's counter-example is a cheap tote/branded bag, which does not move behavior. `[fd5777d14d59]` `[7555f8b740ac]`
7. For consumables/subscriptions, do not default to a large bulk multi-unit offer just to raise AOV; check whether the product actually gets consumed. Eddie's stockpile failure mode: a "buy six" offer raises AOV, but customers use one or two bottles, stop, and never reorder. `[9bf05e0dda65]`
8. Prefer a smaller offer (one unit plus a complementary product) that lands the customer on a recurring/subscription cadence they actually use, even if it cuts first-order AOV. Pair it with the daily usage-habit email sequence (see Email domain) until reordering becomes automatic. `[9bf05e0dda65]` `[bf5f23012c88]`

### Creative

1. Start at the ad level, not the landing page. Write 4-5 distinct psychological angles. Eddie's example pair: "never drink from a shaker cup again" versus "tastes like lemonade, you'll never know it has protein." `[6b9ad04ab9c3]` `[a335ae58c3ad]`
2. Run all angles against the same existing/generic product page so only the ad angle varies. `[6b9ad04ab9c3]` `[a335ae58c3ad]`
3. Read CTR within 1-2 days to find the winner. Eddie's case: ~12% CTR winner versus ~1% for the others. Treat CTR as an interest signal only, not proof the angle converts on-page. `[6b9ad04ab9c3]` `[a335ae58c3ad]`
4. Only then rewrite the whole landing page — headlines, imagery, copy — to match the single winning angle. Eddie's case result: the example brand's revenue reportedly tripled in one month from the angle switch alone; treat as a single case, not a guarantee. `[6b9ad04ab9c3]` `[a335ae58c3ad]`
5. AI-assisted competitor and creative workflow Eddie describes his team using: a bot logs into the ad account → pulls the store's own top-performing creatives by KPI → checks named competitors' ad libraries for their top performers → feeds findings into AI UGC-generation software to produce variations → drops finished files into a shared drive → pings the media buyer to review and launch. `[e5199936326a]` `[verify UI: current ad-library access terms and the specific tool interfaces]`
6. Keep a human review step before anything ships; do not let AI-generated UGC fabricate customer testimonials, endorsements or results, and check platform-policy compliance. `[e5199936326a]`
7. Use AI for nearly all image ads, thumbnails and most UGC-style video. Eddie estimates ~90% of the effect at a fraction of the effort, but still uses real production when the ad depends on a specific known individual (e.g., a celebrity cameo) or on organic reach that only a real account can generate. `[a984fdf4f581]`

### Paid media

1. Before increasing spend, compute revenue per 100 visitors: conversion rate (%) × AOV. Eddie's threshold is roughly 300 (e.g., 3% × $100); he treats results well under 300 as unscalable at his assumed ~$3 CPC floor. `[9e6eb4c8669a]` `[e5cd6f1465bb]`
2. If the number is under 300, decide which lever is cheaper to move for your store — conversion rate or AOV — rather than trying to force cost-per-click down further. Eddie ranks micro-CRO tests (popups, +0.1% lifts) below fixing this core number. `[9e6eb4c8669a]`
3. Model 12-month lifetime gross profit per customer before setting an allowable Day-1 CAC. Method: pull customers who purchased at least 12 months ago (recent buyers have incomplete data) and measure their profit over the following 12 months as an estimate. `[8914117448ec]` `[0ac75b0ea6cb]`
4. Use that LTV estimate to size how much first-order loss is acceptable. Eddie's example: a $60 CAC against a $40 first-order profit (a $20 Day-1 loss) is sound if the customer is worth roughly $380 more in the following year. `[8914117448ec]` `[0ac75b0ea6cb]`
5. If CAC-cutting has plateaued (Eddie cites "Jordan Manard" on how hard it is to move CAC from ~$70 to ~$60 once an account is already well run), stop optimizing CAC directly and instead raise the lifetime value behind each customer through retention. `[5baa55c8c61a]`
6. For channel prioritization, weigh TikTok Shop/creator-economy virality (commission-only, no upfront click cost) as a current arbitrage opportunity — flagged as something that changes "every single month," so re-check current conditions before committing budget. `[a984fdf4f581]` `[web check: current TikTok Shop / creator-commission economics]`

### Organic

1. Keep using real people, not AI, specifically for TikTok, because the goal there is a creator posting to their own organic audience — something an AI persona cannot do. `[a984fdf4f581]`
2. Build the founder's personal brand (content, podcast appearances) as a long-horizon distribution asset before you think you "need" it. Eddie's stated event takeaway: founders with the largest personal brands had the most inbound opportunity — an observed association, not a proven causal driver. `[a901d93b0685]`
3. Pair publishing with ordinary relationship-building — goodwill, staying in touch with former colleagues and employers, and (per Eddie) investing personal capital in other operators' businesses — since he treats these relationships as compounding assets on top of the content itself. `[a901d93b0685]`

### Email

1. Stand up welcome and abandoned-cart flows before scaling paid traffic at all. Eddie states he will not take on a brand as a client without them. `[36f24e817a8e]`
2. If using one, budget for both the acquisition lift and the cost of a third-party identity-resolution/email-append tool. Eddie names a tool that captures a visitor's email after roughly 30 seconds on-site, without an on-site opt-in, at roughly $0.20/email, and reports 20-40x short-term ROI on some accounts and an overall ~15% acquisition lift from email generally. Treat all of these figures as Eddie's own account, not benchmarks. `[36f24e817a8e]`
3. Before adopting such a tool, have your own privacy owner confirm its legal basis and jurisdiction coverage — do not rely on Eddie's explanation alone. His own account: the visitor opted in on another site and that permission trail transfers via a shared "certificate"; he says the tool currently operates in the US and (he believes) Canada, not Europe. `[36f24e817a8e]` `[e17963c7293b]` `[web check: current legality and vendor terms of identity-resolution/email-append tools in your operating jurisdictions]`
4. Design a daily-touch habit-formation email sequence for the first 90 days after purchase on consumable products, tied to actual product use. Eddie's example: a hair-loss brand asks for a daily progress photo. Expect to taper frequency once use becomes habitual, around day 60-90. `[bf5f23012c88]`
5. Run incentivized, open-ended surveys to buyers and non-buyers. Eddie's structure: an "everyone wins" incentive (entry into a $1,000 gift-card draw plus a guaranteed $30 store credit) and 4-5 open-ended written questions (why did you buy, what held you back, what would you want next). `[414f3d559f4e]` `[2a60e95ff096]`
6. Do not use an owned email/SMS list to test whether a new offer will work on cold paid traffic. Eddie is explicit that an owned list is already brand-aware and is not a fair test bed — use it for insight (surveys, soft signal) only. `[2a60e95ff096]`
7. Feed survey answers into your own customer base to find next-product demand once the list is large enough to treat as a signal. Eddie's example: repeated requests for creatine gummies on a supplement brand's list led him to launch it. `[2a60e95ff096]`

### Retention

1. Treat the daily 90-day habit-formation sequence (see Email) as retention infrastructure, not a one-off onboarding email — the goal is durable reorder behavior. `[bf5f23012c88]`
2. Reframe the CAC conversation around retention instead of grinding CAC down further: Eddie's framing is "it's cool to get a customer, it's way cooler to get the same customer 15 times over with no acquisition cost." `[5baa55c8c61a]`
3. Size bundle/multi-unit offers around actual consumption rate, not just AOV impact, to protect the reorder cycle (mechanic detailed under Offers). `[9bf05e0dda65]`

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- Check: can this store scale paid traffic profitably? How: compute conversion rate % × AOV. Good: result is at or above ~300, or whatever your own CPC/margin math implies as break-even. Bad: result is well under 300. Then: fix conversion rate or AOV — whichever is cheaper to move — before adding paid spend; don't chase micro-CRO wins first. `[9e6eb4c8669a]` `[e5cd6f1465bb]`
- Check: is a "profitable" front-end loss on Day 1 actually sound? How: pull cohorts of customers who bought 12+ months ago and total their gross profit over the following 12 months, then compare to Day-1 CAC. Good: the modeled 12-month LTV comfortably covers the Day-1 loss. Bad: you cannot produce this number at all — Eddie's claim is that most brands, even $6-7M/month ones, can't — that gap is the first thing to fix before setting CAC targets. `[8914117448ec]` `[0ac75b0ea6cb]`
- Check: does a hero-SKU gift-gating play make sense here? How: confirm from actual sales data (not a survey) that one SKU drives a clearly disproportionate share of revenue, and re-check that removing it from direct sale won't break unit economics once given away. Good: the item is genuinely coveted and incremental cart value outweighs the giveaway's cost. Bad: it's a low-desire item — Eddie's counter-example is a generic tote/bag — it will not move the threshold behavior. `[fd5777d14d59]` `[7555f8b740ac]`
- Check: is your ad angle the actual reason a page underperforms? How: run 4-5 distinct angles against one existing/generic PDP and compare CTR within 1-2 days. Good: one angle clearly separates from the rest. Bad: no angle stands out — the problem may be product-market fit, not messaging. `[6b9ad04ab9c3]` `[a335ae58c3ad]` `[8bbceb08f413]`
- Check: should we pivot the product or the audience? How: confirm real effort has already gone in (ads, UGC, email backend, site work) over a meaningful window, then test multiple distinct demographics with multiple angles. Good: at least one demographic/angle combination clears friction. Bad: friction persists across all of them — treat this as a product-market-fit signal and do more customer research before another product launch. `[8bbceb08f413]`
- Check: is a bulk/multi-unit offer actually helping retention? How: watch reorder rate and consumption signal (not just AOV) after the offer ships. Good: customers reorder on cadence. Bad: AOV rose but reorder rate fell — the stockpile failure mode. `[9bf05e0dda65]`
- Check: is your identity-resolution/email-append vendor's use of your list actually permitted? How: have your own privacy owner (not the vendor) confirm brand, channel and jurisdiction coverage before sending anything, and hold off until confirmed. Good: confirmed, documented, suppression and opt-out preserved. Bad: coverage is unclear or the jurisdiction is unsupported — do not treat it as a substitute for an owned opt-in audience. `[e17963c7293b]` `[36f24e817a8e]`
- Check: is your internal AI/automation effort producing anything, or is it scattered tinkering? How: see whether requests flow through one intake form → one prioritized board → one accountable builder, or whether individual employees each build their own one-off workflows. Good: centralized, prioritized, with weekly visible progress. Bad: sprawled, duplicated, and going stale as tools change weekly. `[725bc1a75caf]` `[cc06d2a56717]` `[9b674f4dc81a]`
- Check: will a new company-wide process actually get followed? How: ask the managers who'll enforce it whether it helps or duplicates something they already do, and state the explicit personal "why." Good: managers can restate the why in terms of their own benefit. Bad: it reads as busywork with no clear reason — expect it to be quietly skipped, especially by your strongest people. `[4fe972618132]`

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Giving away a low-desire, generic gift (a tote bag, "shitty" branded bags) and expecting it to lift AOV the way a genuinely coveted item does. `[fd5777d14d59]` `[7555f8b740ac]`
- Removing a hero SKU from sale without first re-checking whether incremental cart value actually covers the giveaway cost. `[fd5777d14d59]`
- Sizing a bulk/multi-unit consumable offer purely to raise AOV without checking whether customers will actually use — and reorder — that much product. `[9bf05e0dda65]`
- Rebuilding a landing page or site infrastructure before testing which ad angle actually wins — Eddie calls this "premature site surgery." `[6b9ad04ab9c3]` `[a335ae58c3ad]`
- Running broad-audience ads and generic PDP imagery when your own purchase and review data already shows a dominant buyer segment. `[a335ae58c3ad]`
- Giving up on a product or audience before real effort (ads, UGC, email, site work) has actually been tried. `[8bbceb08f413]`
- Treating an owned email/SMS list as a fair test bed for whether a new offer will convert on cold paid traffic. `[2a60e95ff096]`
- Scaling paid traffic without a welcome flow and abandoned-cart flow already in place. `[36f24e817a8e]`
- Assuming an identity-resolution/email-append vendor's "network opt-in" claim alone is sufficient permission, without your own privacy owner confirming brand, channel and jurisdiction. `[e17963c7293b]`
- Letting employees each build their own one-off AI/automation workflows instead of centralizing requests through one prioritized board and one accountable builder — Eddie says this produced stale work as tools changed weekly. `[725bc1a75caf]` `[cc06d2a56717]`
- Mandating a new company-wide process without checking with the managers who'll run it or stating a clear personal "why" — Eddie's claim is that this reads as busywork and your best people quietly skip it. `[4fe972618132]`
- Hiring or discussing an obviously entrepreneurial candidate without setting expectations (commitment length, exit norm) up front, then being surprised or resentful when they leave. `[ba53cc416810]`
- Answering every request personally instead of asking "can I hand this off?" first — Eddie frames this as the main way big, founder-only plays never get attention. `[90ab58908635]`
- Piling more tasks onto the same founder-dependent operating structure at each revenue plateau instead of rebuilding the foundation. `[a56c19b082b4]`
- Waiting to build a personal brand until "you've made it" — Eddie frames this as a regret ("you wish you'd started a podcast two years ago"), not a nice-to-have. `[a901d93b0685]`
