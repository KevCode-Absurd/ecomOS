## 1. What this source is for

This is the foundational text on compliance psychology: why people say yes, and which six repeatable "weapons of influence" compliance professionals use to get that yes almost automatically. It is not written for ecommerce. It is written from sales floors, charities, cults, political campaigns, courtrooms and laboratories. Its job in this library is to supply the underlying mechanism (and the evidence, or lack of it, behind each mechanism) that ecomOS's offer, landing-page, email and ad-copy playbooks borrow from when they talk about scarcity, social proof, reciprocity or authority. Use it to check whether a proposed tactic has real psychological grounding, what its true operating conditions are, where it backfires, and what a customer-side defense against it looks like, since a defense the source describes is also a description of when the tactic stops working on a sophisticated audience.

## 2. Principles

Numbered 0 to 6. Principle 0 is the foundational mechanism the other six run on. Principles 1 to 6 are the book's six named "weapons of influence," each a full chapter.

0. **Fixed-action patterns and trigger features** (the mechanism underneath all six principles). Condition: applies whenever a decision-maker is rushed, distracted, uncertain, indifferent, or fatigued, and therefore reaches for a single reliable cue instead of analyzing a full situation. Evidence: animal ethology (a mother turkey mothers a stuffed polecat that plays a recorded "cheep-cheep" chick sound; a male robin attacks a clump of robin red-breast feathers but ignores an accurate stuffed robin without red breast) `[web check]`, and the human parallel, Ellen Langer's Harvard copy-machine study, where adding the single word "because" to a request (even with no real reason attached) raised compliance from 60% to 93 to 94% `[web check]`. Cialdini's own frame: the mechanism is efficient and usually correct; it becomes a vulnerability only when a "trigger feature" is faked by someone who knows how to mimic it (the way a predatory firefly mimics a prey species' mating flash code, or a con artist mimics a uniform). 1. **Reciprocation.** People feel obligated to repay, in kind, what another person has given them, including an unrequested, unwanted, or trivial "gift," and this obligation is strong enough to override even active dislike of the giver. Condition: fires whether or not the recipient asked for the initial favor; the obligation to receive (you cannot easily refuse an offered gift without looking rude) is what makes it exploitable, since it lets the giver, not the recipient, choose who becomes indebted. Evidence: Dennis Regan's Cornell "art appreciation" experiment, where a confederate who bought a subject an unsolicited 10-cent Coke got roughly double the raffle-ticket sales of a confederate who gave nothing, and where liking for the confederate predicted ticket sales only in the no-gift condition, not the gift condition (the obligation overrode liking entirely) `[web check]`. Also the Hare Krishna Society's flower-giving fundraising shift and the Disabled American Veterans mail study (an 18% baseline response rate nearly doubled to 35% with an unsolicited gift of address labels attached) `[web check]`. Card ids: [eba045f9fe03] [d690f71a75b1] [d1fe88bdcd7d] [8d852dc8c90f] [09edbcf0efa2] [38c98207d034] [5d3184b62ffe] [ecc4097514cf] [a2d0ba8b9063].

1a. **Reciprocal concessions (a second face of the same rule).** A retreat from a larger request to a smaller one is read as a concession that must be reciprocated with a concession of one's own (compliance), independent of interest in the smaller item itself. This is the mechanism behind the named tactic "rejection-then-retreat," covered fully under Procedures. Condition: only works if the first request is large enough to look like a real ask, and small enough (relative to the audience) to still look like good-faith bargaining; an opening request perceived as unreasonable is not reciprocated when retreated from, per research at Bar-Ilan University `[web check]`. Card ids: [3866a7fb25f7] [e020fb4b6275] [ee37d97509c0] [e9fddef96d88] [4c4ed8151f87].

2. **Commitment and consistency.** Once a person takes a stand, especially an active, public, effortful, and freely chosen (not heavily bribed or threatened) stand, they feel strong internal and social pressure to behave consistently with it, even after the original reasons for the stand disappear. Condition: the pressure is strongest when the commitment is written rather than spoken, made public rather than private, cost real effort, and was not obviously coerced by a large external reward or threat (a large external cause lets a person attribute the behavior to the cause instead of to genuine belief, so the commitment fails to "stick"). Evidence: racetrack bettors were more confident in their horse right after betting than right before `[web check]`; Freedman and Fraser's foot-in-the-door study, where signing an unrelated "keep California beautiful" petition roughly doubled later compliance with a large, unrelated request to post a "DRIVE CAREFULLY" billboard `[web check]`; Deutsch and Gerard's public-vs-private commitment study, where publicly signed judgments were the most resistant to later correction `[web check]`; and Aronson and Mills's severe-vs-mild initiation study, where more effortful, embarrassing initiations produced more favorable ratings of an objectively worthless group `[web check]`. 3. **Social proof.** People decide what is correct behavior partly by observing what other people, especially similar other people, are doing, and this shortcut fires even in response to social evidence they consciously know is fake (a laugh track) or ambiguous (a crowd of onlookers, none of whom is acting). Condition: works best under two combined circumstances, uncertainty (an unfamiliar or ambiguous situation) and similarity (the observed "others" resemble the observer). It fails, or actively misleads, when the evidence has been deliberately falsified, or when it is a genuine but accidental cascade in which everyone is copying everyone else and nobody actually has independent information ("pluralistic ignorance," the bystander effect, or the freeway lane-change pileup). Evidence: canned TV laugh tracks make audiences laugh longer and rate poor jokes as funnier `[web check]`; Latane and Darley's staged-seizure study, where a lone bystander helped 85% of the time versus 31% with 5 bystanders present `[web check]`; the Columbia University dropped-wallet study, where a wallet with a note from a "similar" finder was returned 70% of the time versus 33% for a "dissimilar" finder `[web check]`; and Festinger, Riecken and Schachter's covert study of a failed doomsday cult, which shifted from total secrecy to aggressive proselytizing within hours of its prophecy failing, because manufacturing social proof was the only route left to restore a belief that physical evidence had just destroyed `[web check, this is the real "When Prophecy Fails" study]`. 4. **Liking.** People say yes more readily to people they know and like, and total strangers can manufacture that liking through at least five documented levers: physical attractiveness (a "halo effect" that unconsciously assigns unrelated positive traits like honesty and intelligence to attractive people), similarity (in dress, background, opinions, or lifestyle, even trivial or manufactured similarity), compliments (flattery works even when it is known to be self-interested and even when it is false), familiarity (repeated positive contact, working unconsciously), and cooperation (framing the requester and the target as teammates against a common obstacle, real or manufactured). Condition: liking is powerful but fragile; it can be overridden entirely by a prior reciprocity obligation (the Regan study again) and it depends on the contact being pleasant, not competitive (contact under rivalry, per Sherif's summer-camp and school-desegregation research, breeds hostility, not liking). Evidence: the Tupperware party's hostess-as-requester structure, where the buyer-hostess social bond was reportedly twice as predictive of purchase as product preference `[web check]`; Joe Girard's "I like you" mass mailing to 13,000+ former customers `[web check]`; and the Canadian federal election study, where attractive candidates got more than 2.5 times the votes of unattractive candidates, while 73% of voters denied any influence from appearance `[web check]`. 5. **Authority.** People defer to perceived legitimate authority far more, and far more automatically, than they predict they will, and this deference responds to the mere symbols of authority (a title, a uniform, an expensive suit or car) almost as strongly as to genuine expertise. Condition: strongest when the authority appears in a role we associate with superior knowledge or the power to reward/punish, and it fails to protect us precisely because we consistently underestimate its own strength before we experience it. Evidence: Stanley Milgram's Yale obedience experiments, where roughly two-thirds of subjects delivered a fictional 450-volt maximum shock to a screaming "learner" on a lab-coated researcher's instruction, against pre-study expert predictions of 1 to 2%, and against a psychiatrist panel's prediction of about 1 in 1,000 `[web check, this is the real, famous, and since-debated Milgram study]`; a midwestern hospital study in which 95% of nurses began to administer a dangerous, unauthorized drug dose on the say-so of an unverified caller merely claiming to be "a doctor" `[web check]`; and Leonard Bickman's guard-uniform study, where compliance with an odd street request rose from 42% in street clothes to 92% in a security-guard uniform `[web check]`. Card ids: [6ef2415cede8] [ae53dc263561] [eff569999325] [72d23ea7ddf2] [e9400cdb0199] [e1020c15977b] [efb0633227f5] [e6fb10561aef] [57c69c609f38].

6. **Scarcity.** Opportunities, goods, and information seem more valuable specifically because they are, or are becoming, less available, and this operates through two separate mechanisms: an efficient (usually correct) heuristic that scarce things tend to be better, and psychological reactance, the drive to want a freedom more precisely because it is being taken away. Condition: the effect is strongest for newly-reduced scarcity (abundance that just became scarce) rather than constant scarcity, and strongest of all when the scarcity is caused by competing demand from other people rather than by accident; the effect increases desire to possess an item, not its actual functional quality, so it applies only to the "want to own it" motive, not to the "want it to work well" motive. Evidence: Stephen Worchel's chocolate-chip-cookie study, where cookies from a jar of 2 were rated more desirable (though not better-tasting) than identical cookies from a jar of 10, with the largest jump for cookies that had gone from abundant to scarce due to other raters' demand `[web check]`; a beef-importer's real sales-staff test, where warning of scarce future supply roughly doubled orders, and framing that scarcity news as "exclusive" information raised orders to about 6 times the standard pitch `[web check, Cialdini's own applied field test with a student's company]`; and Jack Brehm's psychological reactance theory, evidenced by a 2-year-old toddler study (toddlers reached for a toy roughly 3 times faster when a tall barrier blocked it) and the Dade County, Florida phosphate-detergent ban, after which residents rated the (unchanged) banned product as cleaning better than residents in an unaffected county did `[web check]`. Card ids: [743b6f53913c] [7bd422020873] [c8c9e0285d1c] [40c55e8ed7b8] [7ea17cf991af] [fdc00c6aa447] [11bcf453bac4] [3128fdab039e] [80d1a965dcd8] [dfde8d3fd5c0] [ac38952103cc] [d60a4510b85e] [ec46e5da3485] [28f3d6876a8c] [99016d30d66e] [cb31b8de451f] [184387263bb4] [d77e538965d6] [b06504fa4dd7] [1ba0b515409e].

Underlying, non-chaptered principle used throughout: **the contrast principle**, a perceptual effect (not one of the six named "weapons," but foundational to several of them, especially reciprocal concessions). Presenting item B right after item A changes the perceived value of B in the direction opposite A, disproportionate to the true difference. Condition: order matters directionally; presenting the expensive item first makes a cheaper item look cheaper, but presenting a cheap item first makes an expensive item look more expensive. Evidence: the hot/cold/lukewarm three-bucket demonstration; the retail "sell the suit before the sweater" rule; and the Brunswick billiard-table field case, where leading customers to a $3,000 table before letting them shop down produced an average sale over $1,000, versus $550 average when customers were shown the cheap end first and traded up `[web check]`. ## 3. Procedures

Step-by-step sequences the source documents. These are the book's named manipulative and defensive scripts, given so an agent can recognize, and where legitimately adapted, replicate the mechanism honestly. `[verify UI]` does not apply (this source has no platform steps); tag instead where a step depends on live customer or brand data.

### A. Rejection-then-retreat ("door-in-the-face")

1. Decide the real target request (the one you actually want accepted).
2. Open with a larger, plausible request you expect to be refused. It must be big enough to create room for a "concession," but not so extreme it reads as bad-faith bargaining (the Bar-Ilan finding: an unreasonable opener is not reciprocated when retreated from) `[web check]`.
3. Let the first request be refused.
4. Retreat immediately to the real, smaller target request, framed implicitly or explicitly as a concession.
5. Expect three combined effects reported in the source: (a) reciprocity, the target feels obliged to concede in return; (b) contrast, the smaller request looks smaller next to the large one than it would look alone; (c) felt responsibility and satisfaction, the target believes they "made" the requester concede, which increases both follow-through and willingness to agree to further requests later. Evidence for the follow-through claim: a Canadian field study in which the retreat sequence produced 76% verbal agreement to volunteer versus 29% for a direct ask, and an 85% show-up rate versus 50% for the direct-ask group `[web check]`.
6. Do not repeat this on the same counterparty without a real cooling-off period; a detected pattern (see Procedures, section G, the redefinition defense) collapses the reciprocity effect entirely, since a spotted trick is not a favor and creates no obligation. Card ids: [e020fb4b6275] [ee37d97509c0] [e9fddef96d88] [4c4ed8151f87] [3866a7fb25f7].

### B. Foot-in-the-door / "start small and build" (layered commitment escalation)

1. Choose a first request so small and so easy to agree to that refusal would feel unreasonable.
2. Get the target to act, not merely agree verbally in the abstract; a behavior (signing, writing, doing) shapes self-image far more than a spoken assent.
3. Wait; let the small act settle into the target's self-concept ("I am the kind of person who does this").
4. Present a second, related, and larger request, timed while the new self-image is fresh.
5. If durable change (not just one-time compliance) is the goal, keep any external reward or threat attached to step 1 small; the source's evidence (a children's toy study) shows a threat just barely strong enough to produce compliance produces lasting internal change, while an obviously large threat or reward produces compliance only as long as the threat or reward is present, because the person attributes their own behavior to the external cause, not to genuine preference `[web check]`.
6. Escalate again if needed, each new step justified by the prior one ("after all, it's what you really believe, isn't it?" was the literal Korean-War-camp phrasing the source documents for this move) `[web check]`. Card ids: [1dc5c87f6b56] [47ad577a71af] [7d9e21c715c1] [7372e8f77f4d] [39d3229ff1f7].

### C. The lowball (commitment first, advantage removed second)

1. Offer an unusually good deal or advantage to produce a purchase decision.
2. Once the decision is made, run the target through commitment-building steps (paperwork, financing arranged, a trial period using the item) so they begin generating their own new reasons to want it, independent of the original advantage.
3. Remove the original advantage (an "error" is found, a manager "disallows" the deal, a trade-in estimate is "corrected" down) before the contract is finalized.
4. Most people proceed anyway, because the decision now stands on self-generated reasons, not on the withdrawn advantage. Evidence, including the source's own more defensible use of the same mechanism for a beneficial end: Michael Pallak's Iowa energy-conservation field study, where residents promised newspaper publicity for conserving fuel saved 12.2% in the first month; after the publicity promise was withdrawn, they increased savings to 15.5% for the rest of the winter, and a repeat summer study on air-conditioning use showed 27.8% savings during the promised-publicity period rising to 41.6% after the promise was withdrawn `[web check]`.
5. ecomOS use: card [e0ab1f148d59] explicitly repurposes this procedure as a checkout audit (find and fix your own lowball-shaped pricing, do not run it on customers), and card [3f1108aa246a] repurposes the Pallak finding as a retention test (does value hold after a disclosed introductory incentive ends), not as a manufactured bait-and-switch. Card ids: [e0ab1f148d59] [3f1108aa246a] [b3eea5415ee8].

### D. Manufactured or genuine limited-number sale close

1. Identify a customer showing interest cues (close examination, reading the manual, discussing the item) without yet seeking a salesperson.
2. Approach and state the item appears to be sold out or nearly so ("I sold it to another couple twenty minutes ago... the last one we had").
3. Let interest spike as availability appears to drop.
4. Ask for a firm purchase commitment contingent on finding another unit ("if I can get it for you at this price, you'll take it?").
5. "Find" the unit and complete the sale, now backed by a commitment made at the moment of maximum perceived scarcity.
6. This exact sequence, as documented, depends on a fabricated stockout and is the kind of tactic ecomOS rules forbid outright (see section 9). The lawful, honest analog kept by the ecommerce cards is to run the identical psychological structure on a real, verifiable stockout and back-in-stock flow: state true availability, let the customer opt into a wait, and never claim urgency you cannot support with real inventory data. Card ids: [743b6f53913c] [c8c9e0285d1c] [fdc00c6aa447] [11bcf453bac4] [ac38952103cc].

### E. The "bank examiner" con (authority-symbol stacking)

1. Identify an isolated target (classically, an elderly person, followed home from an actual bank).
2. Approach dressed in unmistakably conservative, expensive business attire (an authority "trapping").
3. Claim a relevant, specific, professional identity ("bank examiner") investigating a specific, plausible wrongdoing (an employee doctoring records).
4. Ask the target to withdraw their own savings to "help" the investigation, exploiting the fact that a real bank examiner would never need this and the target has no way to verify the claim once they have left the bank.
5. Introduce a second confederate ("bank guard," another authority uniform) to receive the money "for safekeeping" after hours, when the real bank is closed and cannot be called to check.
6. This procedure is included only as a diagnostic template: any funnel that (a) claims an unverifiable authority title, (b) asks the customer to act before they can independently confirm the claim, and (c) times the ask for when verification is unavailable, should fail an ecomOS trust review regardless of category. Card ids: [72d23ea7ddf2] [e6fb10561aef].

### F. "Good Cop / Bad Cop" (manufactured cooperation plus contrast plus reciprocity, stacked)

1. Present two authority figures to the target, one hostile and threatening (Bad Cop), one moderate and eventually protective (Good Cop).
2. Let Bad Cop escalate threats until the target is anxious.
3. Have Good Cop intervene on the target's behalf, visibly and repeatedly (even paying for something small, like coffee, which triggers reciprocity).
4. Remove Bad Cop from the room.
5. Good Cop then frames the remaining ask as teamwork against the shared "problem" (Bad Cop, or the situation), offering a better outcome in exchange for immediate compliance, before Bad Cop returns.
6. This stacks contrast (Good Cop looks unusually reasonable next to Bad Cop), reciprocity (Good Cop did favors), and manufactured cooperation (the "we're on the same side" frame) into one sequence. Direct ecommerce analog is the honest version only: a support agent or salesperson who genuinely advocates for the customer against a real constraint (a shipping carrier, a supplier), not a staged adversary. Card ids: [0ac6098b64d7] [313c00948117].

### G. Vincent-the-waiter's authority-plus-reciprocity upsell

1. Read the party size and composition; tailor tone accordingly (playful for families, formal for young couples, warm for solo diners).
2. For large parties: as the first order is placed, visibly hesitate, glance around, and recommend one or two alternatives that are slightly cheaper than what was ordered, framed as insider, disinterested advice ("that isn't as good tonight... might I recommend...").
3. This single move produces three effects at once: reciprocity (the table feels done a favor), manufactured authority (the recommender now appears to be the resident expert on quality), and credibility (arguing against one's own short-term interest, by suggesting something cheaper, makes the later, more expensive recommendations far more persuasive).
4. Immediately after, offer the higher-margin add-ons (wine pairing, dessert) while credibility is at its peak.
5. Legitimate ecommerce adaptation: resolve a genuine, verifiable customer concern first (an honest sizing or fit note, a real "this one runs small" flag) before presenting a complementary paid add-on; the sequencing, not the deception, is what the source shows working. Card ids: [e1020c15977b] [e6fb10561aef].

### H. Two-question authority defense (customer-side or buyer-side)

1. When facing an authority-based influence attempt (a title, a uniform, a credential, an endorsement), ask: "Is this authority truly an expert, on this specific topic?" This filters out irrelevant authority (a business suit has no bearing on jaywalking safety; a former TV-doctor actor has no bearing on caffeine's health effects).
2. If yes, ask: "How truthful can we expect this expert to be here?" Check whether the authority benefits from your compliance. Trust rises when an authority concedes a real, minor point against their own interest before making their main claim (the "Avis: we're number two, but we try harder" pattern) `[web check]`.
3. Apply this to your own store's authority claims as an audit, not only to incoming pitches: replace any credential or endorsement that is not both relevant and honestly disclosed. Card ids: [eff569999325] [efb0633227f5] [ae53dc263561].

### I. Consistency-trap defenses ("stomach signal" and the "time-travel question")

1. Stomach signal: when a request feels wrong but you feel cornered by something you already said or did, notice the physical discomfort itself as the trigger to stop. Name the tactic out loud to the requester (Cialdini's own personal countermove); a genuine compliance professional using a real tactic will typically disengage once named, since the trick depends on going unnoticed.
2. Time-travel question, for slower-building, self-justified commitments with no sharp "gotcha" moment: ask "Knowing what I now know, if I could go back in time, would I make the same choice again?" and trust the first flash of feeling, before rationalizations arrive; Cialdini cites unnamed psychological evidence that felt reaction precedes conscious justification by a fraction of a second `[web check, treat as a personal heuristic, not a validated instrument]`.
3. ecomOS application: use question 2 on your own launch and campaign post-mortems (would we greenlight this again, knowing what we now know), not only on individual customers, to separate genuine product-market fit from sunk-cost defense of a bad call. Card ids: [8292b0464f45] [239a4c7c7019] [66ae43096af3] [0e946e408fb2] [deab84e5f347] [2f71ca0f90fc] [95e9000831b0].

### J. Redefinition defense against manufactured reciprocity

1. Accept the "gift," sample, or first favor being offered (blanket refusal of all favors is impractical and causes real social friction, per the source's own example of a girl handing out flowers who was rudely rebuffed).
2. Ask: was this an authentic, no-strings favor, or a device engineered specifically to produce a larger return favor?
3. If authentic, treat the resulting mild obligation as fair and reciprocate normally.
4. If it was a device (a "free" audit or sample explicitly built to justify a pitch, a lowball, a manufactured "smaller ask" after a refusal), mentally redefine it as a sales tactic, not a favor. The reciprocity rule obligates favors to be met with favors; it does not obligate tricks to be met with favors. You may accept the free item and still decline the paid offer entirely, and the source frames this explicitly as fair play, not rudeness. Card ids: [a2d0ba8b9063] [ecc4097514cf].

### K. "Devictimizing yourself": directing a request to one person, not a crowd

1. In an ambiguous situation where a crowd's inaction might read as "nothing is wrong" (pluralistic ignorance), do not issue a general request.
2. State clearly and specifically that an emergency or need exists; do not soften the language out of fear of overstating it.
3. Isolate and address one specific individual by a visible, unmistakable identifier ("you, sir, in the blue jacket").
4. Give that one person a specific, actionable instruction ("call an ambulance").
5. This removes both blockers documented in the bystander research: diffused responsibility (nobody feels personally assigned) and pluralistic ignorance (nobody can tell if it's real). Cialdini reports using this on himself after a real car accident, with immediate compliance, and a secondary effect: once the first driver acted, others copied the action without being separately asked. ecommerce adaptation: a specific, single, named CTA per page or step (not three competing buttons and a chat bubble) does the same job for a hesitant, uncertain visitor. Card ids: [ab6cbc054bf3] [e23f6027a769] [6b8dbcf365be].

### L. Simultaneous-appointment scarcity (private-party rivalry manufacture)

1. Advertise an item for sale.
2. Schedule every interested buyer for the identical appointment time.
3. When a second buyer arrives while the first is still deciding, establish a visible "first come" order and ask the newcomer to wait nearby, in view.
4. Let manufactured rivalry (not the item's real merits) drive urgency in both parties; either buyer's hesitation now risks losing the item to a visible rival.
5. This is documented in the source as effective and is explicitly the kind of manufactured competitive urgency ecomOS rules forbid (fake "other buyer" pressure); the honest analog is showing real, verifiable concurrent-interest signals only (a real "X people have this in their cart" count from your own system), never a staged rival. ## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Is this social proof verifiable or seller-produced?** What to check: can the number (bought, viewing, reviewed) be traced to real platform or order data. Good: aggregated, auditable, matches actual sell-through. Bad: a static or seller-typed count with no backing data. Next step: remove or substantiate before publishing. Card ids: [2610a89b3503] [b00c278eaa05] [d147d0be41fb].
- **Is the audience already certain, or genuinely uncertain?** What to check: is this cold/unfamiliar-category traffic (uncertainty is high, social proof and authority cues do the most work) or high-intent branded search (uncertainty is low, proof adds little). Next step: concentrate proof and authority cues on the ambiguous steps of the funnel, not uniformly everywhere. Card ids: [c0a38656da11] [e23f6027a769].
- **Is the claimed authority actually relevant to this specific claim?** What to check: does the credential, title, or endorsement bear directly on the product's actual risk area (safety, technical performance, medical claim), or only on general prestige. Good: a named, checkable, relevant expert. Bad: an unrelated celebrity or an empty title used only for its symbolic weight. Card ids: [eff569999325] [72d23ea7ddf2].
- **Is a scarcity or deadline claim tied to a real, checkable constraint?** What to check: actual remaining units, a real end-date already fixed in the system, or a genuine one-time batch. Good: a number that will visibly and permanently change once true stock is gone. Bad: a countdown that quietly resets, or "limited" language on an unlimited SKU. Card ids: [743b6f53913c] [fdc00c6aa447] [cb31b8de451f].
- **Does the customer want to own this, or to use it?** What to check: is the purchase motive status/collection (scarcity can honestly justify premium pricing) or function (scarcity should not be used to imply the item performs better; per the source, scarce cookies did not taste better and banned detergent did not clean better). Card ids: [28f3d6876a8c] [1ba0b515409e].
- **Would the "gift" or discount survive being named out loud to the customer as what it is?** What to check: read your own free-sample, gift-with-purchase, or "we'll take less profit for you" copy and ask if it is a genuine no-strings favor or a device built to trigger a bigger ask. Good: it is what it says it is. Bad: it is a lowball or manufactured concession dressed as generosity. Card ids: [a2d0ba8b9063] [e0ab1f148d59] [e6fb10561aef].
- **Are you (or a stakeholder) defending a decision because of the evidence, or because you already committed to it?** What to check: run the time-travel question ("knowing what we now know, would we make this call again?") on the team, not only on customers, before continuing to fund a launch, channel, or influencer bet. Card ids: [239a4c7c7019] [95e9000831b0] [deab84e5f347] [913bb06d2be3] [e2d412db7e87].
- **Is a "trending" or "bestseller" signal genuine independent demand, or a self-reinforcing cascade?** What to check: repeat-purchase rate, direct search volume, and customer-support-request volume, not just rank or velocity, which can be produced by shoppers copying other shoppers rather than by real preference (the freeway lane-change and racetrack-odds mechanisms). Card ids: [83427f562f2c] [a5df3105dd91].
- **Does your team's own arousal (urgency, FOMO, auction fever) match the underlying facts, when buying inventory, ad space, or a competitive bid?** What to check: pause when you feel "logic going out the window" (Robert Wood's own phrase after ABC/CBS's Poseidon-rights bidding war, where CBS's president described losing the auction as a relief `[web check]`) and set a pre-committed ceiling before entering any competitive or auction-style purchase decision. Card ids: [80d1a965dcd8] [3128fdab039e].

## 6. Tools, settings, named features

None of these are software tools; they are named tactics, theories, and case studies the source uses as reference points. Listed here so an agent recognizes the term when it appears in a brief or another playbook.

- **Fixed-action pattern / trigger feature**: the ethology term for an automatic behavior sequence set off by one narrow cue. Root metaphor for all six principles.
- **Rejection-then-retreat / "door-in-the-face"**: named tactic, Procedures section A.
- **Foot-in-the-door**: named tactic, Procedures section B. Coined by Freedman and Fraser `[web check]`.
- **Lowball**: named car-sales tactic, Procedures section C.
- **The BUG**: Amway's leave-behind sampling tray/bag of household products, left 24 to 48 to 72 hours at no cost, used as the concrete historical example of leave-behind sampling `[web check]`.
- **Talking the top of the line**: retail sequencing practice, show the deluxe model first.
- **"Setup" properties**: real-estate practice of showing intentionally unattractive comparison listings first (contrast principle applied to comparison sets).
- **Psychological reactance theory**: Jack Brehm's theory that restricting a freedom increases desire for it; foundational to the scarcity chapter's second mechanism `[web check]`.
- **Commodity theory**: Timothy Brock and Howard Fromkin's theory that scarce or exclusive information is itself more persuasive `[web check]`.
- **Pluralistic ignorance**: the social-proof failure mode where each observer reads others' outward calm as evidence nothing is wrong, compounding uncertainty across a group. Central to the bystander-effect research (Latane and Darley).
- **Bystander effect / diffusion of responsibility**: the finding that more bystanders present can mean less help given, not more.
- **Basking in reflected glory (BIRG)**: Cialdini's own named finding (with Avril Thorne) that people display connections to winners and hide connections to losers, especially when their own self-image needs repair.
- **Claquing**: historical (1820s Paris onward) practice of hiring paid audience members (a claque, led by a chef de claque) to fake enthusiastic opera-house response; cited as the direct ancestor of the modern canned laugh track `[web check]`.
- **The luncheon technique**: Gregory Razran's 1930s finding that opinions rated while eating gain approval unconsciously, tracing back to Pavlov's classical-conditioning bell-and-food pairing `[web check]`.
- **Good Cop / Bad Cop**: named police-interrogation tactic, Procedures section F.
- **The bank examiner scheme**: named confidence-fraud tactic, Procedures section E.
- **The jigsaw classroom**: Elliot Aronson's cooperative-learning structure (each student holds one piece of needed information, forcing mutual teaching) used to convert classroom rivalry into liking `[web check]`.
- **Robbers Cave / Eagles vs Rattlers summer camp research**: Muzafer Sherif's studies showing imposed shared goals, not mere pleasant contact, dissolve group rivalry `[web check]`.
- **"When Prophecy Fails" doomsday-cult study**: Festinger, Riecken and Schachter's covert study of a failed end-of-world prediction, source of the social-proof-under-disconfirmation mechanism `[web check]`.
- **The Werther effect**: David Phillips's finding that publicized suicides precede a measurable, matching rise in vehicle-crash and homicide fatalities, named for Goethe's 1774 novel `[web check, a contested area of social science]`.

## 7. Examples and case results

All entries below are the source's own illustrations, examples, or self-reported field notes, not controlled ecommerce benchmarks. Treat every number as belonging to its original, non-ecommerce context.

- The Arizona turquoise-jewelry story: a slow-selling batch sold out completely after a staff misreading turned a intended half-price markdown into a doubled price, illustrating the "expensive = good" heuristic. Personal anecdote, not a study.
- The Hare Krishna Society's shift from off-putting street solicitation to a flower-gift-then-ask model, reportedly funding property and temples in 321 US and overseas centers `[web check]`.
- Lyndon Johnson's early legislative success attributed to years of accumulated Congressional favors, contrasted with Jimmy Carter's outsider "indebted to no one" identity and early legislative difficulty. Cialdini's own political analysis, not an experiment.
- The Boy Scout candy-bar anecdote: Cialdini himself bought unwanted $1 chocolate bars after declining $5 circus tickets, the seed example for the rejection-then-retreat chapter.
- The Watergate break-in decision, read by Cialdini as an instance of rejection-then-retreat (Liddy's escalating-then-retreating proposals from about $1,000,000 down to $250,000), his own interpretation of a real historical event, not an established causal finding `[web check]`.
- The Chinese Communist Korean War POW "lenient policy," escalating small pro-Communist statements into larger acts of collaboration, the historical seed for the foot-in-the-door chapter `[web check]`.
- Fraternity and tribal (Thonga) initiation rites, matched trial-by-trial to illustrate that costly, effortful commitments produce stronger group loyalty; includes real, named hazing-death news cases (Michael Kalogris, Frederick Bronner, Richard Swanson, a Zeta Beta Tau pledge) `[web check names and details]`.
- The "forbidden toy" study (Jonathan Freedman): a threat just strong enough, without being obviously coercive, produced durable internalized avoidance; a strong threat produced only compliance while enforced.
- The "lowball" new-car and energy-conservation examples (Procedures section C).
- The Mesa, Arizona Mormon temple anecdote: Cialdini's own sudden, short-lived interest in touring a normally restricted area purely because it would soon become unavailable, the seed example for the scarcity chapter.
- The Kennesaw, Georgia mandatory-gun-ownership law: reactance theory predicted, and local reporting appeared to confirm, that gun sales boomed mainly among out-of-town visitors drawn by the publicity, not the residents the law targeted `[web check]`.
- The Dade County, Florida phosphate-detergent ban: residents smuggled and hoarded the banned product and rated it as functionally superior to an unaffected county's residents, despite no product difference `[web check]`.
- The 1991 Soviet coup against Gorbachev, read by Cialdini as a reactance case: newly granted freedoms (glasnost, perestroika) produced faster, more massive popular resistance than decades of prior total repression had ever produced `[web check]`.
- The Barry Diller / ABC 1973 "Poseidon Adventure" broadcast-rights auction: a $3.3 million winning bid (versus a prior record of $2 million) in a first-of-its-kind open competitive auction, an expected $1 million loss for ABC, and a direct quote from the losing CBS bidder describing losing as a relief `[web check]`.
- Cialdini's brother Richard's simultaneous-appointment used-car scarcity scheme (Procedures section L), a family anecdote used to fund his college education.
- The Jonestown, Guyana mass suicide (November 1978), read by Cialdini as an engineered maximum-social-proof condition (isolation removed all "similar others" except fellow cult members) `[web check, real historical event]`.
- Reader's Report, a Portland, Oregon woman: agreed to kiss a stranger for a fake "contest," then, purely to stay consistent with that small act, bought a SKI magazine subscription she did not want. Self-report, not a controlled test.
- Reader's Report, a former racetrack employee: describes a real betting-odds manipulation, placing a large bet on a genuine long shot to create a false "public favorite" on the tote board, then betting the real pick once its odds improve as other bettors copy the manufactured favorite. Self-report of an observed real-world tactic, not the source's own study.
- Reader's Report, a former TV/stereo salesperson: self-devised rejection-then-retreat on service-contract tiers (pitch the 3-year plan first, retreat to the 1-year plan), self-reported 70% attach rate versus roughly 40% for peers. Self-report, not controlled data.
- Reader's Report, a Blacksburg, Virginia woman: fell harder for an older boyfriend specifically after her parents objected, a first-person "Romeo and Juliet effect" account matching the Colorado-couples study finding.

- Frank Layden, then head coach of the NBA's Utah Jazz, quit while the team was leading its division after repeated abusive treatment from fans following losses, illustrating how fan identification with a team's outcome (basking in reflected glory's negative mirror image) can turn vicious `[web check]`.

Langford): named US TV weather forecasters (Bob Gregory, Tom Bonner, Mike Ambrose, Chuck Whitaker, Tom Jolls, Sonny Eliot) harassed, threatened, or assaulted by viewers for weather they only reported, illustrating the association principle's negative "bad news infects the teller" side `[web check]`.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Assuming an authority figure, symbol, or a big brand name will be resisted or discounted by a rushed or uncertain audience; the source's repeated finding is the opposite, people consistently underestimate authority's actual pull on their own behavior (Milgram predictions, the uniform study, the prestige-car study).
- Treating any single influence tactic as durable; the source itself notes the Hare Krishna gift tactic lost effectiveness once the public learned to recognize and avoid it, and warns generally that exploited shortcuts erode with repeated exposure.
- Stacking contradictory or conflicting cues on one page or step (an aggressive countdown next to heavy, unrelated social proof) can crowd a decision and reduce credibility rather than adding force; the source's principles interact and are not simply additive.
- Using a cheap item to precede an expensive one in the same comparison view; the contrast principle works against you in that order, making the expensive item look more expensive than it would look alone.
- Opening a rejection-then-retreat sequence with a request so large it looks like bad-faith bargaining; the Bar-Ilan finding is that the retreat is then not read as a genuine concession and the compliance boost disappears.
- Confusing scarcity's effect on desire to possess with an effect on actual functional quality; the source is explicit that scarce cookies did not taste better and banned detergent did not clean better, so using scarcity language to imply better performance (not just rarity) is a category error, separate from being a truth problem.
- Believing that mere repeated contact always builds liking; contact under frustration, conflict, or competition reliably breeds more hostility, not more familiarity-driven liking (the school-desegregation and Robbers Cave findings).
- Relying on customer self-report to judge whether an attractiveness, similarity, familiarity, or association effect worked; the source repeatedly shows these effects operate unconsciously and people deny being influenced by them even when the data show large effects (the Canadian election study, the car-ad-with-model study).
- Letting a promotional withdrawal (of a promised toy, a discount, a perk) land as a broken promise instead of managing supply and expectations honestly in the first place; the "you promised" toy-industry pattern shows the resulting parental resentment and forced compliance is a fragile, adversarial position to put a brand in.
- Assuming low conversion on unfamiliar or cold traffic means low interest; the bystander research's core surprise is that ambiguity, not indifference, usually explains inaction, so the fix is removing uncertainty (who should act, is this a real need), not assuming apathy.
- Believing that knowing about a bias protects you from it; the source is explicit, especially in the scarcity chapter, that the arousal a tactic produces suppresses the very cognitive faculties that "knowing better" depends on, so intellectual awareness alone is not a defense (Robert Wood's own "logic goes right out the window" quote after losing a bidding war).
- Treating a public, costly commitment's survivors as a genuine signal of product quality; the doomsday-cult case shows that the more a person has publicly sacrificed for a belief, the harder they will work to manufacture confirming evidence for it after it fails, which is a warning against reading vocal post-launch community enthusiasm as proof a product delivered, when that enthusiasm may be self-protective rationalization by people who already spent money or reputation on it.
- Assuming a large reward or a severe punishment produces genuine, lasting compliance; both the forbidden-toy and Korean-POW-camp material show a large external inducement lets a person credit their own behavior to the inducement, not to real belief, so the behavior collapses the moment the inducement is removed, while a small, barely sufficient one produces durable, self-owned belief.

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Where the source hedges or is thin on method.** Several of the book's most vivid claims (the Werther-effect suicide-crash statistics, the "95 percent are imitators" sales-trainer quote, some Reader's Reports) are anecdotal, attributed opinions, or single-source figures the book does not independently verify; the source itself treats Reader's Reports as illustrative, not evidentiary. `[web check: several of these classic studies, including Milgram's replication rate and some details of the Genovese/bystander case reporting, have been questioned or revised by later scholarship since this edition was written]`.

**Where the source conflicts with common ecommerce advice.** Common CRO advice often treats "more social proof is always better" and "always create urgency" as near-universal wins. This source complicates both: social proof only helps under uncertainty and similarity, and can crowd out credibility or read as desperate when overused on a confident buyer; scarcity's own author warns it clouds judgment and should trigger a pause, not a purchase, meaning a store that engineers maximum urgency is deliberately degrading its customers' decision quality, which cuts against ecomOS's plain-language, honest-benefit writing rule. The source also complicates a common assumption that competitive bidding/auction formats are efficient price discovery; its own extended example (the Poseidon auction) shows competitive formats can drive buyers well past rational value, a warning that applies to ecomOS teams bidding in ad auctions or on wholesale inventory, not only to customers.

**What is missing for ecommerce use.** The source has no digital-commerce content at all: no PDP, cart, checkout, email, or paid-ad examples: every ecommerce application in the cards below is an adaptation by the card author, not a result the book itself demonstrates, and each card correctly flags this. The source also gives no guidance on measurement thresholds (how much lift justifies a tactic), no guidance on regulatory limits (advertising law varies badly by market and the book predates most of it), and, being a general compliance-psychology text, gives no brand-specific, category-specific, or platform-specific guidance at all.

**Where sources in this library will disagree.** A landing-page or ad-copy playbook that recommends "always show urgency" or "add a bestseller badge to every PDP" should be read as in tension with this source's own two failure-mode warning (falsified proof, ambiguous/accidental cascades) and its own defense chapters, which exist specifically to describe how a sophisticated buyer neutralizes exactly those tactics. Do not average the two into a single rule; flag the disagreement in the folder playbook's section 7.

**ecomOS adaptation (own addition, not in the source).** The mapping below is the playbook author's own work, applying the six principles to concrete ecommerce surfaces, and explicitly marking where ecomOS's own rules forbid the manipulative version the source documents.

- *PDP.* Reciprocity: a genuine, unconditional free sample or guide, delivered before any ask, can lift conversion the way the Regan Coke study suggests, but ecomOS rules forbid a "free" item engineered as a lowball bait-and-switch (Procedures C). Contrast: sequence a premium anchor before cheaper accessories on a bundle or comparison table; never invent a fake "was" price to manufacture contrast, since ecomOS forbids invented claims and false reference pricing is a legal risk in most markets. Authority: use only named, verifiable, relevant credentials or certifications; ecomOS rules forbid an actor, an unrelated celebrity, or an empty title implying expertise the product does not have. Scarcity: state true remaining stock or a real, fixed deadline only; ecomOS rules forbid a countdown that resets or a "3 left" claim not backed by actual inventory. Liking: attractive, similar, or familiar models and creators are a legitimate lever, but ecomOS rules forbid fabricated personas or astroturfed community content standing in for real customers.
- *Offers.* Rejection-then-retreat is a legitimate pricing-ladder structure (open with the premium tier, land on the mid-tier you actually wanted) as long as both tiers are real and honored; it becomes a dark pattern the moment the "premium" anchor is fictional or never actually sellable. Commitment and consistency support a small, real first purchase or trial designed to earn a customer's own follow-on interest, not a trick foot-in-the-door that hides the true cost or contradicts the initial framing (a real ecomOS-forbidden manipulation).
- *Email.* Reciprocity and the luncheon technique both argue for attaching a genuine value-add (a useful guide, a real discount, content people enjoy) before or alongside a purchase ask, and for pairing brand mentions with a customer's actual moment of pleasure (delivery, unboxing) rather than with bad news; per the source's own "bad news infects the teller" finding, route delayed-shipment or price-increase notices through a distinct identity from your best-news marketing sends, without hiding the brand's real responsibility when the brand caused the problem, since ecomOS forbids invented claims and misattributed blame.
- *Ads.* Social proof and liking (similarity, familiarity) are the strongest, most transferable levers for cold, uncertain traffic; use real customer-similarity matching (a plus-size shopper's own photo, a same-use-case testimonial) rather than an aggregated "everyone loves this" claim with no real numbers behind it, since ecomOS forbids fake social proof outright, and the source's own claque and canned-laughter material shows that fabricated proof both works in the short term and destroys trust once discovered, which is exactly the tradeoff ecomOS's quality-first priority tells this team to refuse.
