## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a 6-part checklist, CLOSER (Credibility, Lure, Objection handling, Social proof, Ease of use, Result), to audit any page that asks a visitor to act. It explains the persuasion work around a call to action (CTA), not button colour. It covers the math of why conversion rate changes cost per lead or order, how to write a CTA that offers something the visitor wants, how to answer fears about the first step, where to put proof, how many form fields to ask for, and 4 tools (call tracking, callback widget, live chat, alert bars and landing pages). For an ecommerce operator it is best used as a qualitative audit frame for PDPs, landing pages, email capture, quizzes, cart and checkout.

## 2. Principles

1. **CRO is the fastest lever and should often come before traffic.** Source 1 says there is "no faster way" to improve results than raising conversion rate, and that CRO is best done before driving traffic, because traffic costs money or time. Condition: the presenter frames this as a recommendation; the card notes a new store may need some traffic first to set a baseline. Evidence: the £1 per click math (see section 4). [b22709fcf770] [ab4878e2910b]
2. **The form or button is not the conversion. The persuasion around it is.** Putting a form or button on a page is easy. People fail because they change button colours or move forms without knowing what to write around them. Condition: applies to any CTA. Evidence: presenter opinion from agency work. [9d6f772a1213]
3. **If you do not give visitors a reason to believe you are good, they cannot believe it.** A first-time visitor has no context on your business. Condition: every page, but most of all around the CTA. [409693679dd7] [0ae2ac29bac6]
4. **Before-and-after proof works when the "before" matches where the reader is now and the "after" matches where they want to be.** It does not have to be a photo; it can be a testimonial, a case study or a video. Evidence: Bill Phillips fitness camp page (Body for Life author; built EAS, sold for $160M, resold for $320M). [4428427c21f5] [f95f16ca7611]
5. **Meaningful specifics build belief.** Precise details that do not cause the result still make it believable: "at age 50", "a fireman in California", "21% to 9% body fat", "dropped 30 lbs", "25-minute workouts". Show varied people (ages, genders). Close the loop by naming what caused the result. Condition: details must be true and permissioned (card caveat). [f95f16ca7611] [4428427c21f5] [409693679dd7]
6. **Visitors judge the whole business on first impression of the site.** In the website A versus website B seminar exercise, the same company's old and new site made people rate product, service, staff happiness and wait times all higher for the new site (a site that cost about £4,000). Condition: a seminar demo, not a controlled study. Owners who built their own site cannot judge it; get impartial feedback. [9398191fe1b4]
7. **Put proof where people decide, not only on a proof page.** Case study pages are "one of the least visited pages". Put case studies and logos on each service page. Visitors do not need to click to get the credibility; seeing the logo is enough. "You really can't overuse case studies." Condition: the "least visited" claim has no data; check your own analytics (card caveat). Ecommerce translation (cards): put product-matched reviews on each PDP, collection and bundle page. [4be25816f37b] [ba8350548af5]
8. **Alternative credibility when you have no proof yet.** 3 forms: (a) nail the customer's problem by hyper-niching, like a palm reader who describes your life; (b) tell the parts of your own story that resonate with the audience; (c) "celebrity" endorsement by anyone the audience already trusts, famous or not (example: Alan Titchmarsh selling life insurance, shown in a gilet with a mug so he does not look like a salesperson). Condition: new business with few testimonials. Card caveat: a very narrow audience can cap demand. [6247dfe184a2] [502dbeee4943]
9. **Every CTA needs an attractive "lure".** "Contact us", "make an inquiry", "subscribe to our newsletter" offer no value to the visitor. The lure must move the visitor to the next step on *their* journey, not yours. For ecommerce, Source 1 says the lure is "obviously" the product itself, and the same logic applies to email signups: a buyer guide for those who do not know what to buy, or 10% off or free postage on the first order for those who do. [ea8b1b83867d] [d043167a017f] [6026a7303e2a]
10. **Lure rule 1: if the audience does not know the scale of their problem, your offer helps them see it.** Examples: free consultation plus mockup for cosmetic surgery or architecture; free 3D visual of an exhibition stand. **Lure rule 2: if they know the problem, your offer gives them a road map.** Example: funding consultation plus a plan. Test for a lure: can you say exactly how it moves them closer to their goal? If not, it is not strong enough. [ea8b1b83867d] [d043167a017f]
11. **Reframe steps you already do; do not add new ones.** If the sales process already has a 30-minute qualifying call, sell that call as a "free consultation". The stand company already made free 3D visuals but did not say so on the site. Condition: operations must be able to deliver the promised step (card). [ea8b1b83867d]
12. **Match the CTA to what the visitor has already decided.** In ADT's test, "Get a free quote" beat "Book a free survey" by 60%. Buyers already wanted ADT and only wanted a price. "Survey" implied a home visit and a sales pitch. Condition: the 60% has no sample size or duration (card caveat). Keep it simple when the offer is simple. [8121c53ef9d8] [a01ac2d91e2d]
13. **Handle objections to the first step, not only to the product.** Visitors have "inertia to act". The blockers are often fear, doubt and uncertainty about what happens next (Will you serve my area? Will a salesperson call? Will someone come to my house? Will I be embarrassed if I do not qualify?). Condition: optimise for undecided visitors; people already sure do not need it. [182f13121915] [6e5644b9f415] [f62861431f31]
14. **The "cocktail stick" rule.** Free samples on a counter with cocktail sticks convert because the sticks signal "take one, it's free". Remove the sticks and fear of embarrassment kills conversion. CTAs need the same explicit permission cues. [a4d2ce6b188a]
15. **If you do not tell people what they will get, they assume the worst.** State the exact next event after the click. [16c2667cb67c]
16. **Social proof: nobody wants to be the first to decide.** Show that many people like them already took this step. Use it for the CTA itself, not just the business ("42,000 other people get our tips"). [3ef8c77c4464] [2d56e9a1802e]
17. **Review volume often beats average rating.** Seminar exercise: people pick the book with more reviews even when its average rating is lower. Build volume over time by sending customers review links (Google My Business, Trustpilot, Amazon, own site). If you have only 2 customers, use the 2 reviews you have. Condition: directional, not a rule to optimise volume alone (card). [affc22a52d1b] [3ef8c77c4464]
18. **Make the conversion goal the most visible element on the page.** Copy the Amazon PDP pattern: a bordered box top right on desktop, big contrasting buttons, white space. Do not put the CTA only at the bottom. [a360a5796cb7]
19. **The "Homer Simpson" rule.** Every visitor is busy and distracted and acts like a novice on an unfamiliar site, even scientists. User-testing videos show this. Give them what they expect where they expect it. [d4484bf96e29]
20. **Do not be a pioneer in critical UI.** Stand out in look and feel, but keep navigation and conversion areas familiar. An award-winning law-firm site with a quirky hamburger menu had a "massive" bounce rate because people could not find the menu. "The only metric that matters though is conversion rate." [d4484bf96e29]
21. **Many repeated CTAs are fine if they all do the same thing.** The exhibition stand site had a CTA box, header button, 2 banner buttons, a top CTA, a phone number, a Live Call widget and a sticky bottom bar, all "get in touch". Card caveat: competing or conflicting CTAs add friction. [a360a5796cb7]
22. **Form length: fewer fields convert better, but too few look like a sales trap.** HubSpot data showed conversion falling from 3 to 10 fields, and also lower conversion at 1 to 2 fields. Diagnostic-style questions raise perceived value. [4ae56138baa5] [8accb09355e6]
23. **Text boxes cut conversion more than choices.** More free-text areas = lower conversion. Use multiple choice and dropdowns. [4ae56138baa5]
24. **Ask only for information you will use.** Do not ask for a phone number if nobody will call; or make it optional. Ask enough to qualify and, if leads exceed sales capacity, to disqualify (for example, budget). [4ae56138baa5]
25. **Sell the free thing hard, then filter in the form.** Deliberately suppressing response with more fields is valid when the free step is costly to deliver, but only after doing everything to sell it. "The worst thing" is a perfect prospect leaving because the offer looked low value. [4ae56138baa5]
26. **Ask for needs before contact details.** A multi-step flow that asks about the problem first feels like a diagnosis. Asking name, email, phone first feels like lead capture. Once invested, people give details more easily. [9ed6a0a32b9f]
27. **Be honest about what the result step is.** The claims checker did not show live results; step 3 said the team would call with results, which made the follow-up call expected. [9ed6a0a32b9f]
28. **Result: design the first step so it moves a lead toward a sale.** The sales process should be a "slippery slide". Ask what the lead is most scared of and do the opposite right away. Seed your paid service early, in the middle (case studies) and at the end of the free deliverable. [9d6f772a1213]
29. **A 100% sales close rate is a red flag.** It means the funnel is too narrow. A more attractive lure brings more unqualified leads and more qualified leads. Have a plan B to monetise leads you cannot serve: a cheaper product or course, referral to partners for commission, and a long email "indoctrination" sequence (one client joined after about 5 years of weekly emails; some after 6 years). Condition: stated for service businesses. No card covers this rule directly.
30. **Every product and category page must be ready for cold traffic.** Search and ads send people to inner pages. Do not assume they came through the homepage. [3127398d2368]
31. **A menu is a guide, not a directory.** Long option lists make people forget earlier options (like phone menus past option 6). Do not sort alphabetically. Use customer words, not jargon. A click should open a useful page, not an intermediate "home" page. [f5942adec72b]
32. **Paid-ad landing pages are worth studying.** Advertisers pay per click so they are more likely to track and measure. A £60-a-month Leadpages page beat a site that "probably cost £20,000" on CLOSER. Condition: a paid ad does not prove the page is profitable (card). [a8ff5e236f0f]
33. **Even familiar products must be sold.** "Build it and they will come. Not on the internet." Source 1 says this applies even to batteries. [e24c4415d85e]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 CLOSER page audit (the teardown method)
1. Pick the page a visitor really lands on (often a subpage from search or ads, not the homepage). [3127398d2368]
2. Credibility: count the credibility triggers. Is it clear the business has reason to talk about the topic? [bdded17187e0] [095620484975]
3. Lure: how attractive is what they offer for acting? Is there one at all?
4. Objection handling: are fears about requesting the thing removed?
5. Social proof: is it obvious that others chose this business?
6. Ease of use: how easy is it to become a lead or sale? Any blockers?
7. Result: is it clear what the visitor gets when they act?
8. Score each of the 6 as present or missing. Source 1 notes that even a page that gets only ease of use right ("1 out of 6") can beat many.
9. Card additions (not in source): record gaps as concrete hypotheses, rank by likely effect and cost, test or build the top ones, re-audit after changes because fixing 1 element can expose another. [bdded17187e0] [9d6f772a1213]

### 3.2 Credibility asset inventory (exercise)
1. List every credibility asset: testimonials, before-and-after pictures, case-study statistics, client logos, awards, Google reviews, your story, a trusted endorser, and wording that nails the audience's problem. [0ae2ac29bac6]
2. Put some form of credibility on every page, and especially next to the CTA.
3. Put client names in the above-the-fold copy too, not only as logos (example: "For over 11 years... Deloitte, BBC, NHS, the Professional Cricketers' Association").
4. Add job title and company to each testimonial so readers in the same role see themselves.
5. Card additions for ecommerce: verified reviews, customer photos, product test results, certifications, material or ingredient facts, retailer or press mentions, warranty terms, customer counts, years trading; match each asset to the doubt it answers; update as new proof arrives. [0ae2ac29bac6] [409693679dd7]

### 3.3 Build the lure (exercise)
1. Write the clear, attractive next step you offer. Not "request a free consultation" but "request a free consultation and we will help you X". [ea8b1b83867d]
2. Decide which rule applies: does the audience know the scale of the problem (give a road map) or not (help them see the scale)?
3. Describe exactly how the lure moves them closer to their goal. If you cannot, rewrite it.
4. Walk through your current sales process. Reframe an existing step (qualifying call, mockup, audit) as the lure.
5. Keep it as simple as the buyer's stage requires (ADT: a quote, not a survey). [8121c53ef9d8]
6. Ecommerce email capture: buyer guide for the unsure; first-order discount or free postage for the ready but delaying. Card adds: check discount and shipping cost against margin first; measure signup-to-first-purchase, not signup volume. [d043167a017f] [6026a7303e2a]

### 3.4 Objection handling for the first step (exercise)
1. Put yourself in the buyer's shoes with a scenario (the source's example: broken boiler, newborn, mid-winter, search "boiler repair"). List what you would need to know before contacting anyone: rough price, how fast, who comes, qualified, works on my boiler type, covers my area. [f62861431f31]
2. Write the top 3 fears about requesting *your* CTA. Exposure Ninja's own: (a) it will be low value, (b) nothing new, (c) someone will pitch me.
3. Answer each fear head on in the CTA copy. Exposure Ninja's answers: "over 400 five-star reviews on Google and Facebook" (answers low value); "one of the team will record you a 15-minute video... and a prioritised step-by-step plan" (answers nothing new); "emailed to your inbox" (answers the pitch fear).
4. Use permission phrases: "no obligation", "to see if you might be eligible", "to find out if we might be a good fit", "apply for a discovery call". [a4d2ce6b188a]
5. For an ecommerce newsletter with a 10% discount, answer "you'll spam me" by saying what they get: "occasional tips on how to do X". [a4d2ce6b188a]
6. Write out the exact process after the click. Tested wording the agency says raises conversion: "For a free, no obligation consultation, fill in the details below and one of our team will be in touch to arrange a consultation at a time of your convenience via phone, Skype or in person." [16c2667cb67c]
7. If the process is long, in person, feels risky, or objections are serious, explain it on the page itself.
8. Good benchmark copy from the source: a kitchen company's "If you'd like to arrange a free home survey, please don't forget to include your home address", which makes the address optional and removes the home-visit fear.

### 3.5 Social proof for the CTA (exercise)
1. Place reviews above the fold and again next to the button. National Accident Helpline showed Trustpilot in the header, again in the sidebar, then recent testimonials. [3ef8c77c4464]
2. Collect reviews of the lead step itself: in the email that delivers the free thing, ask for a review if it was useful; ask consultation clients "what would you say to someone thinking of getting a free consultation?". [2d56e9a1802e]
3. For a newsletter, run an NPS or rating (source names Delighted, delighted.com) and show the aggregate rating where you ask for signups. `[verify UI]`
4. Show those reviews on the pages where you ask for the action.
5. Build a page that sells the free thing, not the company (Exposure Ninja's review page: what is included, results, many testimonials about the review itself).

### 3.6 Ease of use and form design (exercise)
1. Make the conversion goal the most visible element; use a right-hand box on desktop plus clickable phone numbers in header and headline. [a360a5796cb7]
2. Add repeated access points that all lead to the same action, including a sticky bar. [a360a5796cb7]
3. Watch user-testing videos to check people find it. [d4484bf96e29]
4. List the minimum information you need to move the lead forward. [4ae56138baa5]
5. Newsletter: name plus email (name lets you personalise, which Source 1 says raises open and read rates); birthday if you will send a birthday message. Email only may convert more but lowers later engagement.
6. Service lead: contact details you will use; qualifying questions; if leads exceed capacity, a disqualifier such as budget.
7. Convert free-text questions to multiple choice or dropdowns.

### 3.7 Result: map the sales process after the lead (exercise, service businesses)
1. Use the generic 4 steps: assessment (size the problem, show the solution), check-in (feedback, questions, spot later objections), proposal (plan and price), check-in and close.
2. Combine assessment and check-in into 1 call when the lead already knows what they need (3 steps).
3. Choose the delivery format: phone, Skype or Zoom call (best when starting, gives live feedback, lets you qualify); in-person meeting (least scalable, most effective for high ticket; discovery days for 6 to 10 people; Source 1 notes clients at £10,000 a month who never met the agency); emailed presentation, proposal, mockup or video (lowest risk for the buyer: no awkward "no"); full product trial (software; watch onboarding load).
4. Script every sales call. A script can be "5 questions that must be answered on the call" plus the next step. Do the process yourself first, then hand the script to the team.
5. Open the deliverable by defusing the lead's biggest fear ("this is not a pitch"; "choose how often we email you").
6. Seed the paid offer at the start, through case studies in the middle, and at the end.
7. Plan B for unqualified leads: cheaper product or training, referral commission, long email sequence.
8. Ecommerce note: Source 1 says this step is "only relevant for service companies". For ecommerce the Result check becomes "what happens after the action: what they receive, when it ships, the product outcome". [095620484975]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **CLOSER scorecard.** For each of the 6 elements: present, weak or missing. Good: all 6 visible near the CTA. Bad: CTA only at page bottom, no lure, no proof near button. Next: fix the missing element with the largest likely effect first (card: rank by effect and cost). [bdded17187e0] [095620484975]
- **"Why would I bother?" test for the CTA.** Read the CTA as a stranger. If the only outcome is "someone will contact me to sell", the lure is missing. Next: rewrite as a benefit the visitor gets. [ea8b1b83867d]
- **"How does this move them closer to their goal?" test.** If you cannot state it specifically, the lure is too weak.
- **Commitment check.** Does the CTA ask for more than the visitor is ready for ("Start my claim" when they do not know if they have a claim)? Next: lower it ("Find out if you could claim", "Find out how much you could claim", "Try our 30-second claim checker"). [a01ac2d91e2d] [8121c53ef9d8]
- **Unknowns check.** Does the page say what happens after the click, who contacts them, when, how (phone, email, in person), and whether it costs anything? Missing answers = objections. [16c2667cb67c]
- **Service-area and eligibility check.** Can a visitor tell if you serve them? If not, they will not risk embarrassment.
- **Proof placement check.** Is proof above the fold and next to the button? Or only on an About or case-studies page? [3ef8c77c4464] [ba8350548af5]
- **Direct-landing check.** Open the subpage from search, not the homepage. Are the homepage reviews and awards still visible? Is the page title specific ("commercial contract solicitors", not "commercial contracts") so a visitor with no context understands? [3127398d2368]
- **Menu check.** Too many options? Alphabetical? Jargon labels? Menu icon that does not look like a menu? Clicks that land on an intermediate "home" page? [f5942adec72b] [d4484bf96e29]
- **Tracking check.** Is the main CTA an email link (mailto)? That gives no data on leads and fails when the visitor's email app is not set up. Next: use a form, and track calls. Irwin Mitchell example. [af7a5ac33e5f]
- **First-impression check.** Ask impartial people (Source 1 names usertesting.com, written "useresting.com" in the transcript, or a free review) what they expect about quality, service and legitimacy from the page alone. Owner-built sites cannot be self-judged. [9398191fe1b4]
- **Ecommerce objection check (Shark Proof teardown).** Returns window, dispatch speed, shipping cost, and consistency of currency and shipping-origin cues. A free-shipping note in dollars on a pounds price made a UK buyer worry about US shipping time. Delivery times shown inconsistently. [e24c4415d85e]
- **Review authenticity check.** Reviews bunched around the same dates raise doubt. [e24c4415d85e]
- **Result clarity check.** Does the PDP show the product doing the job under the conditions buyers care about (Shark Proof could show phones attacked with knives)? [e24c4415d85e]
- **Sales close-rate check.** If nearly 100% of leads buy, the lure is too narrow. Widen it and add a plan B.

## 7. Examples and case results

All are examples, not promises. None report sample size, test length or significance.

- PPI claims client, "We find out if you had PPI": 10% to 15% conversion, up to 30% from organic; peak 400 leads a day; 200 leads in 1 hour after a TV mention. Back end was fully automated: an info pack, then a notification of PPI with an order form, no human contact, because of volume.
- PPI test site, "Find out how much you could claim": 33.7% site-wide.
- ADT: "Get a free quote" beat "Book a free survey" by 60%. [8121c53ef9d8]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Thinking CRO is button colours or form placement. [9d6f772a1213]
- Focusing only on traffic and ignoring what happens after the click. [ab4878e2910b]
- Offering no lure: "Contact us", "Make an inquiry", "Subscribe to our newsletter". [ea8b1b83867d]
- Putting the CTA or form only at the bottom of the page, or making people hunt for the contact page. [a360a5796cb7]
- Asking for too big a first step ("Start my claim", "Book a free survey"). [a01ac2d91e2d]
- Leaving the process after the click unexplained. [16c2667cb67c]
- Hiding strong proof on About or case-study pages. [ba8350548af5]
- Using vague praise instead of meaningful specifics. [f95f16ca7611]
- Judging your own site's first impression when you built it. [9398191fe1b4]
- Assuming a sophisticated audience will find the CTA anyway. [d4484bf96e29]
- Hiring creative agencies to invent novel navigation or menus; award-winning design that hurts conversion. [d4484bf96e29]
- Menus built like directories: too long, alphabetical, jargon, landing on "home" subpages. [f5942adec72b]
- Using a mailto link as the main CTA (no tracking, fails without a set-up email client).
- Asking for fields you will not use, especially phone numbers nobody will call. [4ae56138baa5]
- Too few fields (1 to 2) so it looks like pure lead capture. [4ae56138baa5]
- Asking for contact details before the diagnostic questions. [9ed6a0a32b9f]
- Worrying about "giving too much away" when the free step already exists in the sales process.
- Handing an unscripted free-consultation process to a team.
- Never mentioning in the free deliverable that you sell the service.
- Panicking when more leads lower the close rate.
- Assuming a product with known demand sells itself. [e24c4415d85e]
- Hiring an influencer the audience does not trust (the "Kylie Jenner for a tax service" example).

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Built for lead generation.** Most rules are shown on service sites (law, claims, construction, agencies). The presenter says ecommerce lure is "obviously" the product and that the Result/sales-process lesson is "only relevant for service companies". Ecommerce uses in the cards (PDP proof placement, quiz design, cart reassurance, sticky add-to-cart) are the card writer's translations. Treat them as hypotheses. [8accb09355e6] [9ed6a0a32b9f] [e24c4415d85e]
- **No test evidence standards.** No numbers carry sample size, duration or significance. The 60% ADT uplift, 9.2% vs 2.48%, 33.7% and 10% to 15% are anecdotes. [8121c53ef9d8] [a360a5796cb7]
- **Conversion rate is treated as "the only metric that matters".** Sources 2 and 3 in this folder say revenue per visitor matters more and conversion rate can be gamed. Source 1 never discusses AOV, margin or RPV. The cards add purchase, revenue and margin checks that Source 1 does not state. [b22709fcf770]
- **Many CTAs versus focus.** Source 1 praises 7 or more repeated CTAs on 1 page. It does not address competing offers; the card adds that caveat. Source 3 (in this folder) uses "one clear call to action" on a sales-page hero. [a360a5796cb7]
- **Trust seals and logos.** Source 1 uses secure-checkout logos, awards and Trustpilot badges as credibility. Source 2 in this folder calls generic trust badges useless.
- **Deliberate friction.** Source 1 allows adding fields to filter serious leads. This fits costly free services; the source gives no rule for when it applies to ecommerce.
- **Form-field data.** HubSpot study conditions, dates and sample are not given. [4ae56138baa5]
- **The CRO-first order is a recommendation.** New stores may need traffic to measure anything (card view). [ab4878e2910b]
- **Prices and tools are 2020.** Live Call and Delighted may have changed or closed. `[web check: current status of ResponseTap, Live Call, Tawk.to, Leadpages, Delighted]`
- **Compliance is not covered.** Before-and-after pictures, weight-loss numbers, celebrity endorsement and review solicitation can be regulated. The cards add consent and substantiation caveats. `[web check: review and endorsement rules for the brand's market]` [f95f16ca7611] [4428427c21f5]
- **Missing how.** No method to find objections other than imagining a scenario (cards add support logs, reviews, recordings). No proof-scoring model. No review-volume threshold. No field-count optimum. [0ae2ac29bac6] [3ef8c77c4464]
- **Needs brand data:** `[needs brand data: current conversion rate, CPC, AOV and margin by channel]`, `[needs brand data: top 3 first-step fears from support tickets and reviews]`, `[needs brand data: credibility asset inventory]`, `[needs brand data: which pages get direct entrances]`.
