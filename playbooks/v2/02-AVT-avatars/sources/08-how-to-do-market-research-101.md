## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a way to build a customer avatar and validate market demand before writing marketing copy, launching an offer, or setting up an ad campaign. It replaces traditional research (focus groups, surveys, white papers) with a 3-part method: define who the avatar is (5W framework), find what they say they want (Google), and find what they actually buy (Amazon), then check what competitors are already spending money to say to them (Meta Ad Library). The output is an avatar profile plus a "miracles and miseries" list and a competitor angle list, meant to feed copywriting, creative and media, not to replace them.

## 2. Principles

1. **Do research before writing copy or launching an offer, not instead of a marketing strategy.** The source frames market research as step 1: "market research doesn't mean a thing if you don't have a solid marketing strategy to actually go out and apply it." Condition: applies to any new business, new offer or entry into a new niche. [16074c80a9e5]
2. **Traditional academic/qualitative methods are the source's stated low-priority option, not banned.** Focus groups: expensive, hard to get in-depth data from, prone to moderator bias (the person running the group can sway direction by how they ask questions). Surveys: fragile to wording — rephrasing a question from positive to negative changes results, leading questions skew answers before the respondent thinks, and even question order and the survey's own description can prime answers. Academic white papers and research reports: often too theoretical for real-world use and can be outdated the moment they publish, especially in fast-moving markets. Card caveat: this is the presenter's opinion about method trade-offs, not a universal rule, and applies specifically to poorly constructed versions of these methods. [42ca0e985030]
3. **Stated wants and actual purchases are 2 different datasets; both are needed.** "Talk is cheap" — asking if someone would buy something differs from asking them to hand over their money. Search behavior (Google) shows what people say they want; retail behavior (Amazon) shows what they actually buy. Weight behavioral (Amazon) evidence more heavily for offer/product decisions, and stated language (Google) for messaging and copy. [f33468b23555]
4. **Identify who you're selling to before anything else.** You cannot make the "what they say" vs. "what they buy" research useful without first defining who "they" are. This is why the 5W framework's "Who" step comes first. [16074c80a9e5]
5. **For an existing market, base the avatar on your top 20% of customers, not assumptions.** Look for shared patterns across demographics, geographics and psychographics in that top segment rather than guessing. [cef3f6a44c44]
6. **For a brand-new offer or a new market, base the avatar on competitors' ads and targeting instead.** Look at who competitors target and what their messaging and offers look like. [16074c80a9e5]
7. **Split "What" into miracles (wants, dreams, goals, desires) and miseries (fears, frustrations, pains, problems) as 2 distinct lists**, because they drive different marketing angles: miracles feed aspirational messaging and offer promises, miseries feed problem-agitation and objection handling. [32ce1018d3e2]
8. **"When" covers both life-event triggers and daily-schedule differences.** Life events (marriage, divorce, new child, new job, moving) change buying decisions. Daily schedule (a traditional 9-to-5 worker vs. a stay-at-home parent vs. a home entrepreneur) changes when and where the avatar consumes content or buys. [826d0e12dcda]
9. **"Where" means online and offline presence, checked against platform demographic data, not assumed.** A quick search for "social media demographics" shows which kinds of people use which platforms, and that should decide where content and ads are placed. [e6c10689be00]
10. **"Why" converts benefits into personal motivation** and decides which benefit leads in copy versus which one supports. Ask, for each benefit, why it matters to this avatar personally, and check the avatar actually benefits from your specific offer, not just the generic category. [e35f8fec52f0]
11. **Ad duration in the Meta Ad Library is used as a profitability signal, with an explicit caveat that it is not proof.** Ads that recur and ads near the bottom of the library feed (longest-running) are treated as more likely profitable, because an advertiser is assumed to keep paying only for what works. The source explicitly says "that's not always the case," so treat it as a screening signal to corroborate, not a verdict. [146bbf72ccfa] [0ea951a84570]
12. **Research produces direction for downstream teams; it does not produce finished deliverables.** Research hands off an avatar profile, miracles/miseries lists, a keyword map and a competitor angle list. Copywriting and creative production, paid media launch/management, and landing-page implementation are explicitly out of scope for this phase. [0926a3def9d8]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Build the avatar with the 5W framework

Source basis: the full "who what when where why" walkthrough. [16074c80a9e5] [229a47af9d51]

1. **Who.** If launching a new offer to an existing market, pull your top 20% of customers and compare them across 3 categories: demographics (age, gender, income, occupation), geographics (city, state/province, country), psychographics (values, attitudes, beliefs, lifestyles). `[needs brand data: customer list ranked by value, demographic/geographic/psychographic fields]` If it's a brand-new offer, look at competitors and their ads to see who they target and what their messaging and offers look like instead. [16074c80a9e5] [229a47af9d51] [cef3f6a44c44]
2. **What.** Write 2 explicit lists, not summarized categories: miracles (wants, dreams, goals, desires) and miseries (fears, frustrations, pains, problems). Keep items specific because the specific items become marketing angles. [32ce1018d3e2]
3. **When.** List life events that could shift buying decisions for this avatar (marriage, divorce, new child, new job, moving). Separately, map the avatar's daily schedule type (9-to-5, stay-at-home parent, home entrepreneur) and how it affects when/where they consume content or buy. [826d0e12dcda]
4. **Where.** Note where the avatar is present and active, online and offline (what they read, watch, socialize on). Then run a search for "social media demographics" to confirm which platforms actually skew to this avatar's profile before committing content or ad spend there. `[web check: current social media demographics data]` [e6c10689be00]
5. **Why.** For each benefit the offer provides, write why that benefit matters to this avatar personally, and check the chain from feature to benefit to motivation lines up with the miracles list from step 2. Use this to decide which benefit leads in the copy. [e35f8fec52f0]

### 3.2 Search intent mining on Google (what they say they want)

Source basis: sock-knitting walkthrough, used here only as an illustration of the method, not the niche. [8ef3a86a5137] [771e65508b21]

1. Go to Google and type your main niche keyword, but do not press enter yet.
2. Read every autocomplete suggestion that appears after the keyword. Record the ones relevant to your business; relevance depends on whether your business is product- or service-based, so filter accordingly.
3. Type a promising suggestion as a new stem and read the next layer of autocomplete suggestions it produces, to surface deeper sub-topics. Repeat as needed.
4. To size the terms you found: log into Google Ads, open Keyword Planner, choose "discover new keywords" `[verify UI: current label and location in Google Ads]`, and enter your seed term.
5. Select the geographic pool to research (the source used US and Canada as its example; pick your own market).
6. Review the returned keyword ideas (the source's example returned "over a thousand" ideas for one seed term) along with search volume and competition level, to check overall niche competition and spot trends/seasonality.
7. Prioritize with judgment — volume and competition are inputs, not a mandate to target every high-volume term.

### 3.3 Purchase intent mining on Amazon (what they actually buy)

Source basis: the Amazon walkthrough. [bb02448d1007] [8a7237ef9555]

1. Search your main keyword on Amazon and read the autocomplete suggestions (the source notes these often resemble Google's, but reflect shopping behavior rather than information seeking).
2. Press enter and study book titles and subtitles in the niche specifically. Because a book takes far longer to produce than a blog or social post, authors put significant effort into optimizing title/subtitle/description copy for the exact target demographic — treat that copy as pre-tested headline and offer language you can mine.
3. If the niche has no meaningful book presence, apply the same logic to the most heavily optimized listings in the category instead.
4. Open individual listings and read the reviews specifically for what buyers liked and did not like about what they bought. This is the primary voice-of-customer data source; feed it into copy themes and objection handling.
5. Estimate relative sales volume across competing listings by comparing review counts — the source frames this explicitly as a rough guess, since exact unit sales are not visible.
6. Cross-check the extracted miracles/miseries language (section 3.1 step 2) against what actually showed up in reviews, so angles are grounded in real buying behavior.

### 3.4 Competitor ad spy in the Meta Ad Library

Source basis: the Facebook Ad Library walkthrough. [146bbf72ccfa] [0ea951a84570]

1. Go to Facebook and search your industry or a specific competitor's page name. `[verify UI]`
2. Open that page, scroll to the "Page Transparency" section, and click "see all." `[verify UI]`
3. If the page runs ads, click through to "go to ad library" so all of the page's ads appear. `[verify UI: current path from Page Transparency to Ad Library]`
4. Review each ad. Pay special attention to (a) ads that recur repeatedly across your scan, and (b) ads closest to the bottom of the feed, which indicates the longest running time.
5. Treat long-running, repeating ads as the most likely to be profitable relative to the competitor's other ads — but do "a little bit of due diligence" rather than assuming duration alone proves profitability, since the source explicitly says this is not always true.
6. Adapt the underlying angle you observe; do not copy the ad's text, images or offer wholesale (this playbook's parent rules also prohibit copying competitor creative directly).
7. Repeat across as many advertising competitors as you can find. Note: the source says this step is harder in some industries (its example: a knitting niche) than others (its example: health and fitness), because fewer competitors may run ads at all.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Skipping market research entirely or doing it with outdated/ineffective methods, leading to ineffective campaigns, bad results, wasted time, money and energy. [16074c80a9e5]
2. Running focus groups: expensive, hard to get in-depth data from, and prone to moderator bias. [42ca0e985030]
3. Using badly worded surveys: rephrasing questions from positive to negative, leading questions, question order, and even the survey's own description can all skew results before the respondent has genuinely answered. [42ca0e985030]
4. Relying on academic white papers/research reports that are overly theoretical and can be outdated the moment they publish, especially in fast-moving markets. [42ca0e985030]
5. Treating what people say they want (search behavior) as the whole picture, without confirming what they actually buy (retail behavior). [f33468b23555]
6. Building an avatar from assumptions instead of your actual top 20% of customers (existing market) or actual competitor targeting (new market). [cef3f6a44c44] [16074c80a9e5]
7. Treating a long-running competitor ad as proof of profitability without doing further due diligence. [0ea951a84570]
8. Writing generic miracles/miseries instead of specific, usable items. [32ce1018d3e2]
9. Skipping the platform-demographics check and guessing where the avatar spends time online. [e6c10689be00]
10. Doing research and having no marketing strategy to apply it to. [16074c80a9e5]
