## 1. What this source is for

A manual, qualitative research method to run before writing copy or building a product: work an idea through a quick profitability filter, then read competitor pages, Amazon reviews, Reddit and Quora by hand to build a picture of who the real sub-avatars are, what they already believe, what language they use, and what has already failed them. The presenter frames this as the highest-leverage step in new product development and says it should happen before quantitative validation, paid traffic tests, or building. It does not cover keyword/search-volume validation, running ads to test demand, or sourcing/production — this source stops at the research document.

## 2. Principles

1. **Research should take 60-80% of new-product development time.** Condition: applies to the presenter's own new-product process, stated as a personal practice, not a rule proven across other operators. Evidence: his own claim, no data. "Get this step right and everything else is gonna come easily." [46a98b023ad3]
2. **Classify every idea in a demand matrix (price x number of customers) before investing further.** Condition: as soon as you have a rough idea, before deep research. Four quadrants: high-end (high price, few customers — his examples: Rolls-Royce, Bentley, Prada, Leica), golden goose (high price, many customers — his example: iPhone), mass market (low price, many customers — his examples: McDonald's, Starbucks), labor of love (low price, few customers — discard immediately). Evidence: presenter's own mental model, illustrated with his own "teach white people to eat habanero salsa" example, which he says has zero real demand despite his personal passion and credibility. [d113ec3fa69f]
3. **The same core idea can sit in different quadrants depending on how you refine it.** Condition: when placing an idea in the matrix, don't assume one fixed quadrant. Example: his fat-loss-for-men idea could be high-end (personal coaching for wealthy, time-poor clients), golden goose, or mass market (a book), depending on execution. Comparison example: personal finance has many potential customers but typically a low price per book/product. [d113ec3fa69f]
4. **Treat the matrix as an estimate, not a measurement.** Condition: whenever placing an idea. There is no "definitively right" quadrant at this stage; the presenter calls it a "back of the napkin exercise." Do this before quantitative demand validation, which the source treats as a separate, later step. [d113ec3fa69f]
5. **Competitor sites reveal sub-markets through imagery, tone and headline, not just product features.** Condition: early in research, once you have problem-keyword search terms. Example: one fitness site used aggressive, testosterone-focused imagery (a narrow, aggressive-male sub-market); another spoke to "normal," out-of-shape guys wanting a regular life; a third addressed "nerds, misfits and mutants" in a fun, self-deprecating tone; a fourth called out "skinny fat guys" explicitly, distinguishing them from "regular guys." The presenter treats a course on handstands for video-game nerds as proof of how narrow a sub-niche can go. [c97ccae24606]
6. **Read headlines literally for the constraint and reward they encode.** Condition: analyzing any competitor headline or promise. Example: "get an hour's worth of results in just 25 minutes" implies a buyer who lacks time (or believes they do); "short, intense, results ridiculous" implies buyers who enjoy being challenged and will accept difficulty if it's fast. Contrast: a mass-market promise ("feel better look better fast") named no specific person, while the skinny-fat page named its buyer explicitly — these are two different framing strategies to note side by side. [3e4fe752116b]
7. **A competitor's polish and apparent profitability is a market-health signal.** Condition: scanning competitor pages during positioning research. If a page looks like "a serious business" making real money, that supports the category; if competitors all look broke or amateur, that is a warning sign for the category too, not just for that one competitor. Sub-market diversity (aggressive branding, self-deprecating branding, identity branding, urgency/time-compression branding, all in one niche) is read as evidence the market is deep enough to support several angles. Example given: a $60, 14-week DVD program (videos, nutrition plan, bonuses) sits in the mass-market quadrant. [2ce06b2439fa]
8. **Filter Amazon reviews by star rating for different signal types.** Condition: once you've found the top 5-10 books/products in the category on Amazon. Read 5-star reviews first for what people genuinely like; read 3-star reviews (the presenter frames these as the most representative of the 2-4 star band) for structural, recurring criticisms rather than one-off taste complaints. Example: multiple 3-star reviews of one fitness book independently said "good for beginners, but oversimplified" — a level/pacing signal, not noise. Another example: a review complaining a diet plan needs more than $50/week for food is read as an opening for budget-friendly meal planning content. [46eec728bb7e]
9. **Buyers who already spent money on a failed solution are the best prospects.** Condition: mining 1-star and 5-star reviews, or listening in live customer conversations. Signal phrase: variations of "I spent thousands of dollars and it didn't work" (his example: a reviewer who hired multiple personal trainers and was never satisfied). Two reasons given: (a) they already believe the problem is real, so you don't have to convince them of that; (b) they haven't gotten results, so they are still actively hunting for a better answer. In live interviews, when someone says "I already tried X and it didn't do Y," the presenter says to lean in and ask more — that person could be your best customer. [b08301158fb6]
10. **Reddit and Quora serve different research jobs; the presenter explicitly avoids Quora for emotional pain points.** Condition: hunting for psychological barriers versus tactical how-to content. Reddit, in his read, surfaces raw emotional answers (fear, shame, identity) because it functions more like anonymous confession; Quora, in his experience, skews to long-form tactical tutorials that don't reveal the underlying emotional block. He states this is his own experience with these two platforms, not a platform rule, and says to verify it per category. [6e03505404f7]
11. **Search Google for platform-hosted threads using natural-language problem phrases plus platform name.** Condition: finding relevant Reddit/Quora threads. Example queries used in the video: "reddit fitness confidence men," "reddit why don't you go to the gym," "quora no time to work out." `[verify UI: whether Google still surfaces Reddit/Quora thread results this way — Reddit search indexing, robots.txt policy and the 2024 Google-Reddit data deal have changed since 2018, so this exact search pattern should be re-tested]`. [6e03505404f7]
12. **Online answers mix honesty and performance; read for repeated patterns, not single comments.** Condition: reading any forum or review thread. The presenter's warning: people give answers "they think other people want to hear," even anonymously, so a single dramatic quote is not proof — but a pattern repeated across many independent answers is a real signal. [6e03505404f7] [e2f0e8697fe7]
13. **Word frequency across threads points to under-examined benefits.** Condition: after reading a batch of forum answers. If one word (his example: "motivation") keeps recurring, treat that as a cue to search further and specifically on that word (his follow-up searches: "how do you find the motivation to exercise," "how do you find motivation to work out after long hours at work"). [0017b1b018a4]
14. **Look for counterintuitive benefit connections, not just the obvious outcome.** Condition: compiling the benefits list from forum reading. Example: people connected losing weight to becoming more focused and goal-oriented — an unexpected link worth writing down and later testing as a headline angle. Other benefits surfaced this way: feeling more at ease talking to people, recovering faster from embarrassment, being treated with more respect, more romantic attention. Map each benefit to the sub-avatar most likely to feel it. [0017b1b018a4]
15. **Primary interviews are necessary because you cannot verify anonymous online answers.** Condition: after desk research (Google, Amazon, Reddit, Quora), before building. The presenter's stated reason for talking to real people: online you don't know who is honest; he says direct conversation with real customers is what "has set our products apart from many other competitors." He backs this with his own claimed practice: reading every email he receives, replying to hundreds, taking customer calls himself and with his team. [e2f0e8697fe7]
16. **Ask lateral questions in interviews instead of pitching or asking head-on.** Condition: running a customer-research call, email exchange or in-person conversation. Example questions given: "Have you thought about XYZ? Is this something that's on your mind? How do you think about it? Who else do you know that's done this? Why did they do it?" The goal is to surface context and history, not to sell or get a yes/no on your idea. [e2f0e8697fe7]
17. **Do the reading yourself; do not script, scrape or AI-summarize reviews or forum threads at this stage.** Condition: any time you are tempted to automate review/forum mining for speed. Reasoning given: automation is efficient but not effective here — it strips the emotional nuance and exact vocabulary of the avatar, which is the actual output of this exercise. Manually copy and paste exact quotes into a research document so you remember precisely what was said. [46a98b023ad3]
18. **Expect the process to look messy, not like a dashboard.** Condition: while doing this research. The presenter explicitly denies any "Minority Report"-style clean interface: in practice it is several open documents, draft headlines, and moving pieces around by hand. Each session's goal is simply to collect and absorb, not to produce a finished artifact. [46a98b023ad3]
19. **Do not react to every piece of critical feedback; look for contradictions and repeated patterns before changing anything.** Condition: reading critical (especially 3-star) reviews. Example of a genuine contradiction found in the same book's reviews: one reviewer wanted more scientific, study-based evidence; another said the material was already too technical. Resolution offered: sometimes the right call is "that reviewer isn't my market" (his imagined response as the author, since the book's appeal rests on being one man's own experiment); other times, file the note away for later rather than acting immediately. Where possible, look for a synthesis that satisfies both sides — e.g., keep the main text simple and put technical/scientific detail in an appendix for the minority who want it. Only change direction on a pattern repeated across many reviewers, not a single voice. [fcf1bd930a6c]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Full research sequence as demonstrated in the video

1. State the idea in one sentence with a specific avatar (his worked example: "I help single men between 25 and 35 look better and feel more confident by showing them how to go to the gym and burn fat," specifically beginners who work a 9-to-5 office job).
2. Run the idea through the demand matrix (see Principle 2) as a fast sanity check. Do this before deep research, and revisit it as refinement narrows the idea.
3. Google natural-language problem phrases related to the idea (his examples: "how to burn fat and gain confidence for men," "fat burning advice for men in their 20s," "men gain confidence by going to the gym"). Open every notable result and write the site down in your research doc.
4. For each competitor site found, read its imagery, tone and headline and name the sub-market it is speaking to (aggressive/testosterone, normal/regular-guy, self-deprecating/nerd, skinny-fat identity, time-starved/challenge-seeking, etc.). Note distinctions the site itself draws (e.g., "us" [skinny-fat guys] versus "regular guys").
5. For each competitor, note the offer format and price (coaching, PDF packages, DVD/video program, forum/call access) and place it in the demand matrix quadrant you'd expect.
6. Go to Amazon. Identify the top 5 to 10 books or products in the category.
7. For each, read the 5-star reviews first for what people like.
8. Then read the 2- to 4-star reviews (3-star treated as most representative) for structural complaints: what's missing, what's oversimplified, what's too expensive, what's too technical.
9. Separately scan 1-star and 5-star reviews for the failed-solution signal ("I spent thousands of dollars and it didn't work" and variants). Capture these buyers' exact wording.
10. When you find a contradiction between reviews (e.g., "too technical" versus "not enough science"), write both down; do not resolve it by acting on the first one you saw. Decide later whether it's a real pattern or a single reviewer who isn't your market.
11. Go to Google and search for Reddit threads using natural-language queries plus "reddit" (his examples: "reddit fitness confidence men," "reddit why don't you go to the gym"). Read answer after answer for emotional mechanics: fears, shame, identity statements, unexpected side benefits.
12. Search for barrier-specific phrasing: [niche] + [fear/barrier] (his examples surfaced quotes like "I feel like people are judging me," "I feel watched," "too far and too expensive").
13. Separately search Quora for the same or adjacent topics, but treat it as a check on tactical framing, not emotional pain points, per Principle 10.
14. As you read, watch for words that keep recurring (his example: "motivation") and run a follow-up, word-specific search to go deeper on that theme.
15. Write down counterintuitive benefit connections you notice (his example: weight loss linked to increased focus), and tag which sub-avatar is likely to feel each benefit.
16. Throughout steps 3-15, manually copy and paste exact quotes into a single research document. Do not summarize or paraphrase at this stage, and do not use scripts or AI to parse the source material.
17. Schedule and run at least one real conversation this week: an email reply, a phone call, or an in-person conversation with someone who fits the avatar. Use lateral questions (Principle 16), not a direct pitch.
18. During interviews, listen for the failed-solution signal live and follow up on it the same way you would in a review.
19. Compile the notes into a working list of sub-avatars, each with its pain points, hopes/benefits, obstacles, and the language it uses for all three — ready to feed avatar definitions and copy/headline drafts.

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Check: does the idea clear the demand matrix?** How: place it in one of the 4 quadrants using rough price and audience-size estimates. Good: golden goose or mass market (or a deliberate, resourced high-end play). Bad: labor of love (low price, few customers) — the source says discard immediately regardless of personal passion. Next step: if unsure, look at comparable existing products/competitors and see where they sit, then refine the idea's format or price to move quadrants. [d113ec3fa69f]
- **Check: do competitor pages look like real, profitable businesses?** How: assess site polish, professionalism, and apparent production value. Good: professional, well-produced pages — read as evidence the category supports paid demand. Bad: amateur or clearly struggling competitor sites across the board — read as a caution on the category, not just that competitor. Next step: if all competitors look weak, dig further before committing budget to the niche. [2ce06b2439fa]
- **Check: are 3-star (and 2-4 star) reviews repeating the same complaint?** How: read across multiple products/books in the category, not just one. Good: a repeated, specific complaint (e.g., "oversimplified," "too expensive") — usable as a design constraint. Bad: scattered, one-off complaints with no pattern — treat as noise, not a mandate to change direction. Next step: build the repeated complaint into your product's positioning or content depth. [46eec728bb7e] [fcf1bd930a6c]
- **Check: does a review or interview answer contain the failed-solution phrase?** How: scan for "I spent [time/money] and it didn't work" language. Good: presence of this language, especially paired with a specific number ("thousands of dollars," multiple trainers) — a high-intent prospect signal. Bad: absence doesn't mean the segment isn't viable, but the source treats this presence as its strongest single buyer signal. Next step: capture the exact wording for later copy use; in a live interview, ask follow-up questions instead of moving on. [b08301158fb6]
- **Check: is a forum thread giving emotional or tactical content?** How: read a sample of answers. Good (for pain-point mining): raw feelings, fears, identity statements — treat as Reddit-type content per this source. Bad (for pain-point mining): long, structured how-to answers — treat as Quora-type content per this source and set aside for a different research purpose. Next step: if a Reddit thread is unexpectedly tactical or a Quora thread is unexpectedly emotional, follow the content, not the platform label — the source's own split is a personal pattern, not a rule. [6e03505404f7]
- **Check: is a piece of critical feedback a pattern or a single voice?** How: see if the same complaint shows up across multiple, independent reviewers or interviewees. Good: repeated across many — act on it. Bad: appears once, or contradicts another reviewer's complaint — file it away or conclude that reviewer isn't your target market, rather than changing direction. [fcf1bd930a6c]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Pursuing a "labor of love": an idea you're passionate about but that has no real customer base or pricing power. Discard these regardless of personal enthusiasm. [d113ec3fa69f]
- Automating review or forum research with scripts, algorithms or AI parsing. The source says this destroys the emotional nuance and exact vocabulary that make the research useful. [46a98b023ad3]
- Treating any single review or interview comment as proof, rather than looking for a repeated pattern across many independent sources. People give answers "they think other people want to hear," even anonymously. [6e03505404f7] [e2f0e8697fe7]
- Reacting to every piece of critical feedback and getting pulled in "a million different directions." Contradictory feedback (e.g., "too technical" vs. "not enough science") is common and must be weighed, not obeyed literally. [fcf1bd930a6c]
- Being a "keyboard warrior" reader (his own aside) — consuming programs/content without applying them, then leaving reviews like "nothing new here." The source flags this as a pattern to recognize when discounting certain critical reviews, distinct from genuine structural gaps. [46eec728bb7e]
- Skipping primary interviews because online research feels sufficient. The source treats desk research as necessarily incomplete since you can't verify who's being honest online. [e2f0e8697fe7]
- Using Quora when you actually need emotional/psychological pain points, per this source's experience with the two platforms. [6e03505404f7]
