## 1. What this source is for

It gives a repeatable method for building buyer personas from real research instead of invented demographic fluff, aimed at B2B SaaS and tech companies with some B2C client work. It covers: why demographic personas fail, a four-part research method (conversations, surveys, digital intelligence, social listening), how to run and recruit for discovery interviews, how to segment by jobs-to-be-done (JTBD), and how to make the output actually get used across a company instead of filed away. It does not cover how to turn findings into ad copy, page copy or an offer; those live in 07-LPG, 03-OFF and 04-BRD per this folder's scope note.

## 2. Principles

1. **Demographic personas fail because they do not answer usable questions.** Condition: any persona meant to guide copy, product or campaign decisions. Adrienne's frustration as a hired copywriter: clients handed her personas like "Mary marketer is 32, loves the color red, is a superwoman, goes to Trader Joe's on the weekends" with no information on the buyer's concerns, what she uses the product to do, or her objections. A persona built this way cannot tell you what to write. [6f03704d60e9]
2. **Segment by jobs-to-be-done (the job and the trigger), not age, gender or job title.** Condition: deciding how to group buyers into personas. JTBD groups people by why they behave, which the source says is "a much stronger way to segment your audience" than demographic or firmographic traits. Every persona should contain evidence, trigger moments, and a job statement. [83486686c828]
3. **Use all four research prongs together; no single one is sufficient.** Condition: building or refreshing personas. Conversations give the why and the trigger moment but people can misreport their own behavior. Digital intelligence and social listening show actual behavior and topics at scale but never explain motivation. Surveys verify trends and gather structured facts. The source's practice is to compile all four before segmenting. [6d0a453de28f] [2fac02da19a5]
4. **Frame research interviews as exploratory, never as sales or support calls.** Condition: any customer discovery interview. State up front, in the scheduling email and again at the start of the call, that you are not a product expert, cannot solve their problem, and are not there to demo or upsell. This removes the hidden-motive concern and is why her calendar "fills up ... pretty quickly." [2534ec17bc50]
5. **A full calendar (or an empty one) is itself a signal.** Condition: recruiting interview participants. Customers who love or hate a product want to talk about it; if very few will book, that itself suggests they lack strong feeling toward the product, which is diagnostic information, not just a recruiting failure. [2534ec17bc50]
6. **Fear of bothering or losing customers by asking to talk is largely unfounded, in this source's experience.** Condition: B2B SaaS teams hesitant to run customer interviews for fear of prompting churn. Adrienne says she can count on her fingers the times an interview led to a cancellation, out of talking to "probably thousands of people" -- her recollection, not an audited number. [2534ec17bc50] Caveat: this is one practitioner's experience in her client base, not a controlled result, and interviews should still be handled with care per the source's own framing.
7. **Match the interview incentive to who you're asking, not a flat rate.** Condition: recruiting participants, especially senior, busy or high-earning ones. Low-cost brand perks (a t-shirt, beta access) work for regular customers; a charitable donation in the person's name works better for busy executives because it avoids turning the ask into a formal paid transaction with rate implications, and it is a favor exchange rather than something they have to "quantify." [70460b582ffd]
8. **A persona is only as good as its use, not its existence.** Condition: any team that already runs persona work. The source's own survey (via the tool Audience) found roughly 90%+ of marketers create personas but about 77% never use them for a campaign, product launch or piece of copy -- her description is "check the box marketing practice." Building one is not the goal; being referenced during real work is. [6f03704d60e9]
9. **One persona output should serve marketing, product, sales and customer success, not just campaigns.** Condition: deciding scope and distribution of the finished persona document. The source argues for cross-functional use as the payoff for the research investment. [83486686c828] [6f03704d60e9]
10. **Naming conventions are a distraction from the content.** Condition: internal pushback on calling the output a "persona," "buyer insights," or "buyer documentation." Some marketers strongly defend a traditional named/acronym persona format ("you will take my acronymed name over my dead body," her paraphrase of pushback she's received); the source's position is that the label does not matter if the content is evidence-based and used. [6f03704d60e9] [83486686c828]
11. **Talking to first customers, and continuing to talk to them, should be permanent company practice, not a one-off phase.** Condition: any business type. The source frames this as universal across "a plumber," "a farm," "B2B SaaS," and "DTC" -- her own general life advice for anyone starting or pivoting a business, restated as a research/culture rule: figure out why customers actually purchase, and keep asking. [5d0d40c930ec] Note: this is the source's broadest, least detailed claim; it names no method for consumer ecommerce specifically beyond "talk to buyers."
12. **Push multiple company functions into customer conversations, not just researchers or marketers.** Condition: building a customer-informed culture. Example given: at Triple Whale, "everyone's been on sales calls essentially," and the practice was infused into support and sales as well as marketing, framed as a feedback loop. [5d0d40c930ec]
13. **When paid acquisition gets harder or more restricted, going direct to your own buyers is a data source you own.** Condition: rising churn, or platform-side signal loss (the source names Facebook/iOS changes specifically). Rather than only reacting to platform data, ask the customers themselves. [2dfaff611f9a]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Run the four-pronged research method end to end
1. Recruit and run about 20 customer conversations, 30 minutes each. [2534ec17bc50] [6d0a453de28f]
   - In the scheduling email and at the start of the call, state plainly: you are not a product expert, you cannot solve their problem, and this is not a demo or upsell. State exactly how much time you need. [2534ec17bc50]
   - Ask about their full journey: how they found the product, why they use it, their experience, and their emotions around it. [2534ec17bc50]
   - Offer an incentive suited to the participant (see 3.3). [70460b582ffd]
2. After analyzing the interview themes, design a quantitative survey. [d7d12982e3e6]
   - Use the survey to verify the interview trends at a larger scale, not to discover new themes.
   - Put structured, easy-to-check facts in the survey rather than the interview: role, tenure in the role/company, company size (employee count), buying authority. [d7d12982e3e6] -- **B2B-specific fields; for a consumer ecommerce brand these do not map directly (there is no "role" or "company size" for a household buyer). The source does not give a consumer equivalent, so do not invent one.** [needs brand data: what data-centric, form-friendly facts matter for this ecommerce brand's buyers, e.g. household situation, purchase occasion, repeat vs first-time]
3. Run digital intelligence analysis on the wider audience, separate from the interviewed sample. [5a83b6bd8cad]
   - Identify which platforms the audience spends time on. Source tools: SparkToro, "Audience." [verify UI: current SparkToro/Audience feature set and whether "Audience" is still the same product]
   - Identify influencers who shape the audience's decisions, on social media and in buying contexts.
   - Assess buying mindset: fast decision-maker vs. needs a sales team, risk-taker vs. conservative, self-serve vs. wants support. Source cites IBM Watson-based data for this socio-emotional layer. [verify UI: current availability of this kind of Watson-based personality/behavior data] -- **"Needs a sales team" vs. self-serve is a B2B buying-mindset axis; for consumer ecommerce this needs adapting (e.g. impulse vs. considered purchase, single-item vs. cart-building behavior), but the source does not supply that adaptation, so treat it as an open gap, not a rule.**
4. Run social listening on the category, not the brand. [bf1e76633e2e]
   - Source tool: Brand24. [verify UI: current Brand24 features/pricing, or an equivalent tool]
   - Track topics, pain points and frustrations people discuss in their own words, not mentions of your own brand handle.
   - Use this to fact-check whether interview themes match broader public conversation.
5. Compile all four data sets. Use digital intelligence and social listening for how the audience behaves; use the conversations for why. Cross-check mismatches between stated and observed behavior as signal, not noise. [2fac02da19a5] [6d0a453de28f]
6. Segment the compiled findings into personas by job-to-be-done and trigger moment, not by age, gender, job title or other demographic/firmographic trait. [83486686c828]
7. Write each persona so it includes evidence, the trigger moment, and a job statement, in language usable by marketing, product, sales and customer success. [83486686c828] [6f03704d60e9]

### 3.2 Frame and recruit for a discovery interview
1. Draft the scheduling email to state clearly: the purpose (learning about their experience, not solving a problem or selling), the time needed (source uses 30 minutes), and that there is no demo or pitch. [2534ec17bc50]
2. Repeat the same framing verbally at the start of the call before asking questions. [2534ec17bc50]
3. Ask journey questions: how they found the product, why they use it, their experience and emotions, rather than feature or satisfaction-survey questions. [2534ec17bc50]
4. If bookings are slow, treat that as a signal about how strongly people feel about the product rather than only a recruiting problem; consider whether the audience segment or incentive needs to change. [2534ec17bc50]

### 3.3 Choose an interview incentive
1. For a normal customer base, offer a low-cost, brand-relevant perk: a company t-shirt for signing up, or early/beta access to a new feature (source example: three months of beta access). [70460b582ffd]
2. For hard-to-reach, senior or high-earning participants (source example: CEOs, busy founders), do not offer cash. Offer a charitable donation in their name instead (source example: $30 donated for a 30-minute call). [70460b582ffd]
3. Reasoning to apply when picking between the two: cash is a rate negotiation for someone whose time is worth much more than the amount offered, which creates an awkward, semi-professional transaction; a charitable donation reframes the ask as a favor exchange instead. [70460b582ffd] -- **Applies as stated to both B2B and consumer contexts in the source's own examples (a company t-shirt example is not restricted to B2B); no adaptation needed for this specific procedure.**

### 3.4 Introduce jobs-to-be-done to a client or team that does not know it
1. Tell the milkshake story: intuition says people buy milkshakes for a sweet tooth; the actual finding was that the job was a clean, easy snack to eat on the go. [c8109839796f]
2. Use the story to make the point that the stated/obvious reason for purchase is often not the real one.
3. Then translate the same question to the client's own product: what job is the buyer hiring this product to do, and in what situation? [c8109839796f]
4. Expect that many people already know the milkshake story but still need help applying the reasoning to their own product; the story is a bridge, not the answer itself. [c8109839796f]

### 3.5 Investigate churn or retention drop directly
1. Identify the customers who are leaving or have recently cancelled. [2dfaff611f9a]
2. Ask them directly why they left, rather than only inferring from usage or platform-attribution data. [2dfaff611f9a]
3. Act on the specific, fixable reasons given (source example: a B2C subscription business added live chat after learning customers left partly for lack of support access). [2dfaff611f9a]
4. Treat this as an ongoing practice tied to retention monitoring, not a one-time audit. [2dfaff611f9a]

### 3.6 Audit whether an existing persona is actually used
1. Check whether the current persona document is referenced in campaign launches, product launches, copy briefs, or strategy planning. [6f03704d60e9]
2. If it is not referenced, treat it as a check-the-box artifact regardless of how much effort went into creating it.
3. Replace fictional/fluff attributes (invented names, stock photos, lifestyle details) with buyer insights: concerns, product-related jobs, objections, trigger moments. [6f03704d60e9]
4. Keep whatever label the team will actually adopt (persona, buyer insights, buyer documentation); do not fight over the name if it blocks adoption. [6f03704d60e9]
5. Distribute the revised document to marketing, product, sales and customer success. [6f03704d60e9]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Persona usefulness check.** What to check: is the persona referenced in an actual campaign brief, product decision, or piece of copy in the last cycle. How: ask the teams who would use it. Good: yes, with examples. Bad: nobody can point to a use. Fix: run procedure 3.6. [6f03704d60e9]
- **Interview booking-rate check.** What to check: how quickly the interview calendar fills once invites go out. How: track response/booking rate per invite batch. Good: fills quickly, in either direction (love or hate the product). Bad: very low response with no evidence of strong feeling either way. Fix: the source treats a low fill rate itself as a signal the audience segment lacks strong feeling toward the product; reconsider the segment or the incentive. [2534ec17bc50]
- **Say-do mismatch check.** What to check: compare stated behavior in interviews against observed behavior in digital intelligence/social listening data. How: run both prongs on the same audience and compare. Good: alignment, or an explainable gap. Bad: unexplained, large mismatch. Fix: do not discard either source; treat conversations as the source of "why" and digital data as the source of "how," and keep both in the persona with the mismatch noted. [2fac02da19a5]
- **Segmentation check.** What to check: does each persona contain evidence, a trigger moment and a job statement, or does it lean on demographic labels. How: review the persona doc against this checklist. Good: job + trigger + evidence present. Bad: age/gender/title-led with no job or trigger. Fix: rerun procedure 3.1, steps 5-7. [83486686c828]
- **Churn-cause check.** What to check: whether cancellation reasons are inferred from data alone or confirmed by asking the customer. How: reach out directly to a sample of recent cancellations. Good: direct answers collected and actioned. Bad: only dashboard attribution used. Fix: run procedure 3.5. [2dfaff611f9a]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Building personas from invented demographic fluff (a name, an age, a favorite color, a lifestyle detail) instead of real buyer evidence. [6f03704d60e9]
- Treating persona creation as a one-time, check-the-box deliverable that nobody consults afterward (77% figure). [6f03704d60e9]
- Letting a customer interview drift into a disguised sales call, demo, or upsell attempt, which breaks the trust that fills the interview calendar. [2534ec17bc50] [5d0d40c930ec]
- Avoiding customer outreach out of fear of bothering people or triggering cancellations, without testing whether that fear is founded in your own data. [2534ec17bc50]
- Paying cash to interview subjects whose time is worth far more than the offered amount, turning a research ask into an awkward, quasi-commercial negotiation. [70460b582ffd]
- Assuming you already know your customer well enough to skip research; the source describes clients whose confident answers were "orthogonal" (her word) to what the data actually showed. [83486686c828] (context only, not a distinct card)
- Relying only on platform/usage data to explain churn instead of asking the customer directly. [2dfaff611f9a]
- Fighting over what to call the deliverable (persona vs. insights vs. documentation) instead of focusing on whether the content is evidence-based and used. [6f03704d60e9]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **B2B lean, stated by the source itself.** Adrienne describes her client base as "B2B SaaS and tech companies, and some B2C even." - **Possible conflict with common ecommerce practice.** This source treats demographic and firmographic segmentation (age, gender, job title, company size) as weak and something to move away from in favor of JTBD segmentation. Common ecommerce practice often relies on demographic and interest-based targeting (Meta/Google audience builders) and RFM-style demographic/behavioral customer segmentation for email and lifecycle work. The source does not address ad-platform targeting or RFM segmentation at all; it is a persona-research method, not an ad-targeting or lifecycle-segmentation method, so this is a difference in purpose more than a direct contradiction. - **No method given for choosing how many personas to build.** The source repeats that persona count and specific data points vary by business but gives no rule, threshold, or worked example for deciding the number.
