## 1. What this source is for

Explains how to find and choose a buyer avatar (a specific sub-slice of a market) before building ads, landing pages or product pages for it, and gives a reason (market sophistication) for why niching down usually beats staying generic. It stops at discovery and the logic for how deep to niche; it explicitly does not cover writing the tailored copy, building the tailored landing page, or setting up ad account targeting structure - those live in other modules.

## 2. Principles

1. **Market sophistication forces broad markets to fracture into sub-avatars.** As a market matures, buyers move from "you run Facebook ads? run mine" to "have you done this before?" to "I want someone who only works with my type of business." The source places this shift in the ad-agency market around 2017-2018, when specialist agencies for chiropractors, dentists, roofers and lawyers emerged after those buyers had already tried and failed with generalist agencies. Applies when a market has already had multiple generic attempts fail on its buyers; does not apply to markets still in the "just run my ads" stage. [baea6f8c2d2c]
2. **Narrow and deep beats broad and shallow.** Going all-in on one avatar lets creative, marketing and systems get fine-tuned to that one audience; spreading across many avatars (roofers, lawyers, dentists, e-com, personal brands, etc. at once) keeps everything generic. This is the source's own stated preference ("this is what I want and this is what I do not want"), not a proven universal rule. [baea6f8c2d2c]
3. **A niche still has to be big enough to be worth serving.** Check the size of the parent market before committing to a sub-niche: the source estimates gym-goers worldwide at over 100 million, so many sub-niches still have room, but a parent market of about 20,000 people could leave a sub-niche of only 100-1,000 people, which the source calls possibly too small to promote into. Applies before locking in a very narrow avatar; the population figures are the source's own estimates, not measured data. [58497983a714]
4. **Define an avatar by growth, not by a fixed definition.** The source says explicitly there is no one-word definition of an avatar. Instead, treat a segment as a real avatar when it shows up repeatedly and is growing: recurring review phrases ("as a nurse" appearing again and again), or a recurring self-identification among customers or community members (most people saying they're ecom owners). Once a pattern holds, you can go one level deeper (not just "ecom owners" but "ecom owners doing $100k/month+"). Applies as an ongoing check, not a one-time test, since sub-avatars keep splitting further. [14590ac68739]
5. **Whole markets keep producing new sub-avatars over time.** The source frames this as a nesting-doll process: a mass market (athletic apparel) splits into a niche (gym clothing, via Gym Shark), which splits again into sub-avatars (Alphalite for entrepreneurial lifters, Buff Bunny for women only, Anaka Power for trend-resistant "misfits," Young LA for a specific gym-bench crowd). The source says this is currently happening in weight loss, where perimenopausal and postmenopausal women are becoming their own avatar, which is itself starting to split further. Applies as a way of scanning any category for the next unserved slice; the specific brand characterizations are the source's own reading and should be checked before reuse. [7369370000034] [14590ac68739]
6. **Being first to serve a newly forming avatar is an advantage.** When a recreational trend or sport is emerging (the source's example: cornhole becoming a mainstream US pastime), the source's advice is not to compete on the crowded core product (cornhole boards) but to be the first dedicated brand in an adjacent category for that audience (clothing, supplements, an energy drink for cornhole players), using products that may already exist elsewhere just rebranded and marketed only to that group. Applies to visibly growing recreational or lifestyle trends; the source gives this as a hypothetical strategy, not a documented launch. [1fa73b0ea73b] [2bcb37305e50]
7. **Avatar-fit has to run through the whole funnel, not just the ad hook.** The source calls it the most common mistake to put the avatar name only in the headline ("how to scale Facebook ads for ecom brands") while leaving the rest generic. Every layer needs to change: creative setting/visuals, landing or product page proof (only that avatar's case studies or results), and the sales conversation. The intended effect: someone outside the avatar who lands in the funnel should feel like they're in the wrong place and leave, rather than everyone feeling vaguely addressed. This is the source's stated opinion about trade-offs (deliberately repelling non-fit traffic), not a tested conversion rule. [746f5e6fba38]
8. **Avatar specificity replaces narrow targeting, it doesn't require it.** The source says he still uses broad targeting on Meta even after niching an avatar down, and relies on the ad platform's algorithm to find the matching audience based on which creative performs. Because the algorithm is doing the audience-matching, the creative itself has to carry all of the avatar signal - weak or generic creative under broad targeting gives the algorithm nothing to key off of. This is presented as the source's current practice; platform targeting behavior can change. `[verify UI]` [af1c969a5868]
9. **The same avatar logic applies to ecommerce/dropshipping, not just agencies.** The source generalizes from his agency experience: take a mass-market product/ad angle already proven by competitor ecom brands, and rework every layer (primary text, creative, landing/product page, business name/framing) for one specific avatar instead of the whole market, showing only that avatar's results as proof. [4af92fa26772]

## 3. Procedures

**A. Find avatar candidates from reviews (review mining)** [a724805d3534]
1. Identify comparable products already selling in your category. Example given: selling generic running shoes -> look at Nike, Reebok, Under Armour and On Cloud, since they sell the same product category.
2. Pull reviews from three places: your own product's reviews, competitor reviews, and generic marketplace reviews (source names AliExpress for a new/low-review product).
3. Scan review text for identity-marker phrases: "I am a...", "As a...", or any phrasing where the reviewer states who they are. Given examples: "I am a nurse, I'm on my feet all day and these On Clouds are amazing"; "As a registered dietitian for the last 20 years I'm on my feet all day talking to patients."
4. Tally which identity phrases repeat. A repeated pattern (e.g., multiple "as a nurse" reviews) signals a real, buying avatar.
5. Carry the discovered identity forward as raw material for creative, copy and product-page work - done in other modules, not this one.

**B. Survey existing customers to name an avatar** [d287ab9044c4]
1. Build a free survey in Google Forms. `[verify UI: current free tier limits and question types]`
2. Send the form link to existing customers via whatever email software you already use.
3. Ask how they'd describe themselves: role, job title, family role (husband, father, wife, etc.).
4. Ask what specifically about the product works for them, and what their unique situation is.
5. Look for answers that pair an identity with a mechanism, e.g., "I'm a nurse, I'm on my feet all day, so these shoes work really well."
6. Aggregate responses; treat recurring identity + situation pairs as avatar candidates. Prefer patterns that repeat across many respondents over single, possibly aspirational, answers.

**C. Detect your dominant avatar by talking to customers directly** [bccb9c4b4dab]
1. Hold one-on-one and group calls with customers (source does this inside his own mentorship community).
2. Note the identity language customers use unprompted (e.g., "I'm an ecom brand owner").
3. If a large majority uses the same self-description (source's own figure: about 95% of his mentorship said "ecom brand owner"), treat that as your dominant avatar.
4. Point all outward content (social posts, landing page VSL, group call recordings) at that dominant avatar.
5. Re-run this check periodically - if the customer mix shifts, retarget content to the new dominant group.
6. Cross-check against review mining and trend research: a dominant avatar in your existing base is not proof it's the largest available market.

**D. Map sub-avatars through social content** [7369370000034]
1. Search broad category terms on social platforms (source names TikTok; e.g., "bodybuilding," "CrossFit," "gym clothing").
2. Study the distinct groups of people posting under that term and note what visibly separates them.
3. Trace the nesting pattern: identify the current mass-market layer, the niche layer it split into, and any further sub-avatars claimed by specific brands.
4. Note the signals each sub-avatar's brand uses to mark identity: creative style, product design, brand language/values, and which people the brand "uplifts" (features, sponsors, sends to events).
5. Pick a sub-avatar you can signal consistently across all of those channels at once.

**E. Rework a mass-market ecom play for one avatar** [4af92fa26772]
1. Start from a mass-market product already proven by other ecom brands in the category.
2. Study competitors' working ads: what they claim, what they show.
3. Rewrite the same underlying material - claim, creative concept, page structure - for one specific avatar instead of the general market.
4. Retarget every layer: primary text, the creative itself, the landing/product page, and even how the business is named or framed.
5. Replace all proof with that avatar's results only; do not mix in generic or other-avatar case studies.
6. Example cited: a dropshipping student launched with a 5x ROAS on day one using this approach, then found his niche's main buyers, copied competitors' mass-market ads, and showed results for only that avatar - stated as one example, not a guaranteed result.

**F. Tailor the full funnel to a chosen avatar (avoid hook-only tailoring)** [746f5e6fba38]
1. Rewrite the core message for the avatar, not just the headline: "how to scale Facebook ads" becomes "how to scale Facebook ads for roofers," applied throughout, not only in the hook.
2. Change the creative's visual setting to the avatar's world (source's example: from a generic office to standing on a roof for a roofing avatar).
3. Replace the landing page's case studies with only that avatar's case studies (e.g., only roofing results, not mixed industries).
4. Adjust the sales conversation to speak specifically to how the offer fits that avatar's business.
5. Extend the same tailoring to product photos and every claim on the product page.
6. Check the result with the "wrong Chick-fil-A" test: someone outside the avatar who lands in the funnel should feel out of place and leave, rather than everyone feeling generically addressed.

**G. Enter an emerging trend as the first dedicated brand** [1fa73b0ea73b] [2bcb37305e50]
1. Identify a fast-growing recreational trend or sport (source's example: cornhole becoming mainstream in the US).
2. Avoid the crowded core product category (don't compete with existing cornhole board manufacturers).
3. Pick an adjacent category to enter as the first dedicated brand for that audience: clothing, supplements, an energy drink, etc.
4. Source or reuse products that already exist in other niches rather than building new ones from scratch.
5. Rebrand and market them exclusively for the new avatar - naming, branding, all advertising built only for that group.
6. Move early; the described advantage is being first to serve the newly forming group, not product novelty.

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Is there a real avatar here, or just noise?** Check: are the same identity phrases recurring across multiple reviews or customer answers (e.g., repeated "as a nurse")? Good: a clearly repeating pattern across many independent reviews/respondents. Bad: a single one-off phrase. Next step: if repeating, treat it as an avatar candidate; if not, keep collecting data or try another discovery method (survey, direct conversation, social mapping). [14590ac68739] [a724805d3534]
- **Is the sub-niche big enough to build on?** Check: rough size of the parent market and the estimated size of the sub-niche within it. Good: parent market is large (source's example: 100M+) leaving room for a meaningful sub-niche. Bad: parent market is already small (source's example: ~20,000), leaving a sub-niche in the low hundreds or low thousands. Next step: if too small, either target a shallower cut of the market or pick a different parent category. [58497983a714]
- **Is your avatar-specific creative actually differentiated, or just hook-deep?** Check: compare the creative's visual setting, landing page proof, and sales conversation against the mass-market version, not just the headline. Good: setting, proof and conversation are all changed to the avatar's world. Bad: only the headline/hook mentions the avatar while everything else stays generic - flagged as the source's named common mistake. Next step: rebuild the non-hook layers (procedure F). [746f5e6fba38]
- **Does your dominant customer identity match your outward messaging?** Check: what identity do customers state unprompted on calls, and does most of your content (social, landing page VSL) target that same identity? Good: alignment, e.g., ~95% self-identify one way and content targets that way. Bad: mismatch, or the mix has shifted since you last checked. Next step: re-point content to the current dominant identity and re-check periodically. [bccb9c4b4dab]
- **Is broad targeting still workable with a niched avatar?** Check: is the creative carrying clear, specific avatar signal (visuals, language, proof) rather than generic messaging? Good: creative is dialed to the avatar, so the algorithm has a clear signal to match against under broad targeting. Bad: creative is generic or the avatar only appears in the hook, giving the algorithm nothing to key off. Next step: strengthen the creative's avatar signal before assuming broad targeting is the problem. `[verify UI]` [af1c969a5868]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Putting the avatar only in the ad hook/headline (e.g., "...for ecom brands") while leaving the creative setting, landing page proof and sales conversation generic. Named as where "a lot of people mess up." [746f5e6fba38]
- Showing generic or mixed case studies on the landing page instead of only the case studies belonging to the chosen avatar - undermines the fit the avatar tailoring is meant to create. [746f5e6fba38] [4af92fa26772]
- Trying to serve too many avatars at once (roofers, lawyers, dentists, chiros, landscapers, ecom stores, personal brands, etc. simultaneously) instead of going deep on one - the source's stated reason most large "general" agencies stay rare compared to niche-specific ones. [baea6f8c2d2c]
- Picking a sub-niche without checking whether the parent market is large enough to support it, risking a sub-niche too small to promote into (source's 20,000 -> 100-1,000 example). [58497983a714]
- Competing head-on in an emerging trend's crowded core category (e.g., cornhole boards themselves) instead of entering as the first dedicated brand in an adjacent category. [1fa73b0ea73b]
- Treating a single self-reported customer or review statement as proof of an avatar instead of waiting for a repeating, growing pattern. [14590ac68739]
