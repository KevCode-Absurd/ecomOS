## 1. What this source is for

This source gives an operator a rigorous, non-demographic way to define who buys and why: not a persona, but a "Job to Be Done" — the functional, emotional and social progress someone is trying to make in a specific circumstance. Use it to find real avatar candidates (via nonconsumption, workarounds, negative jobs, unusual uses and your own life), to interview and storyboard customers so the avatar rests on quoted evidence rather than invented traits, to write a "job spec" that turns the job into a design and messaging blueprint, and to check that a business's positioning, metrics and organization still serve that job rather than the product category. It is strong on the theory, the diagnostic questions and named cases; it is explicitly thin on statistical validation and offers only one full worked interview transcript as a method example.

## 2. Principles

### A. What a job is, and what it is not

1. **A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance**, not a demographic segment, a product category or a stated need. This is the book's core definition, repeated throughout. Condition: applies to any purchase decision with real functional, emotional or social complexity. Evidence: argument plus dozens of named cases, no controlled data. [882242897d3b] [f2b99dd64bbd]
2. **A job has three inseparable dimensions: functional, emotional and social.** Most companies over-index on the functional and miss the rest; the book argues social/emotional weight is often larger (example: hiring childcare is functionally about safety, but "who will I trust with my children" often dominates). [782e3f599df4] [74ac1061a1c9]
3. **The circumstance, not the person, drives the choice.** The same person hires different solutions for the same product in different circumstances (the milkshake buyer at 7am commuting vs. the same chain at 5pm with a child). Demographics and psychographics do not predict this; the circumstance does. [f2b99dd64bbd] [c8f28fdc6c65]
4. **Needs are not jobs.** Needs ("I need to eat," "I need to feel healthy") are generic, ever-present and too vague to guide innovation or messaging. A job is specific to a circumstance and yields a testable hypothesis about what will get hired. [98d43a545897]
5. **A "guiding principle of life" (be a good father, be a good employee) is not a job either** — too abstract for a company to design against directly, though the sum of many resolved jobs can serve those themes. Card caveat and book both make this point.
6. **Two rules validate whether a "job" is real, stated late in the book but foundational for QA:** (a) it must be phrased as a verb + noun describing progress ("I need to write books verbally"), not an adjective/attribute like "convenience" — an attribute is an experience that might cause a hire, not the job itself; (b) it must sit at a level of abstraction where the candidate solutions span *different* product categories, not just competing SKUs in the same category. "A 12-oz chocolate milkshake in a disposable container" is a preference, not a job; "keep me occupied and full until my 10am meeting" is a job, because a banana, bagel, doughnut, Snickers bar or coffee could all be hired for it. [b2121bd6e7ce]
7. **Jobs are discovered, not created, and endure while solutions change.** The book's example: the job of sharing information across distance persisted from Pony Express to telegraph to email; the teen job of communicating without parental oversight went from passed notes to Snapchat. [06f039bfa4ba]
8. **Correctly framing the job redraws the competitive set.** Once you know the real job, competitors are often outside the category you assumed (Netflix vs. "everything you do to relax," a cigarette break vs. Facebook, V8 vs. peeling carrots, not other juices). [554e0cd99c6c] [2a7279282866]
9. **Nonconsumption is a first-class competitor and a first-class opportunity.** People who buy nothing rather than an imperfect solution are not "no market" — the job still exists, unserved, and can be a larger opportunity than the visible category (SNHU's online adult learners; Airbnb's ~40% of guests who would not have traveled at all; Kimberly-Clark's incontinence sufferers who go without a product for ~2 years on average). [35de9821d183] [113a77a88966]
10. **Data itself is not neutral evidence of a job.** "Active data" (sales, CRM fields, competitive benchmarks) is easy to get and describes outcomes; "passive data" (context of struggle: what people tried, what frustrated them, what they rigged up) is hard to get and describes causes. Growing companies systematically let active data crowd out passive data. [da14b9693dec] [241ce7605df0]

### B. Hiring and firing forces

11. **Four forces determine whether a new solution gets hired: the push of the problem, the pull of the new solution, the habit of the present, and the anxiety of the new.** For a switch to happen, push + pull must outweigh habit + anxiety combined — "good enough" alone does not win. Citing Kahneman and Tversky, loss aversion is roughly twice as psychologically powerful as the appeal of a gain. [d60553248611] [188ed2166ca7] [69702437d033]
12. **To hire something new, the customer must fire something old** — a competing product, a workaround, or the choice of doing nothing at all. Companies rarely design for what has to be fired (wristwatches fired for phones; Sports Illustrated fired for ESPN). [4171f8999a97] [e8e9e127d973]
13. **Anxiety about switching, not just product appeal, blocks purchases**, and it can be designed against directly: ING Direct's non-transacting "cafés" existed purely to reassure customers it was a "real" bank; SNHU's not-for-profit, real-campus status reduced fear it was a diploma mill; health clubs eventually dropped annual-contract lock-ins once they recognized the anxiety was blocking signups. [e9fd545cfc45] [2174f296edbe]
14. **Even B2B and procurement decisions carry emotional/social weight** — reputational risk, fear of looking unprepared to a boss or board — that a purely rational "features and price" framing misses (Mercer's pension-plan buyers; a plant manager's fear of stockouts). [488785e75dab]

### C. Discovery and research method

15. **Five under-used sources reveal jobs a features survey will not: your own life, nonconsumption, workarounds/compensating behaviors, negative jobs (what people don't want to do), and unusual uses.** Each is a distinct research lens, not a single technique. [9662bde03a61] [a08d84e7c35c] [99ef75b0fbb2] [d9081272b7dd] [817a338fa0e1] [7da672f7f3e9]
16. **Feature-request surveys and focus groups produce wishlists, not jobs**, because they ask people to invent solutions rather than describe their struggle. Pleasant Rowland's one focus group rejected the American Girl doll concept outright; she built a $700M exit anyway by trusting the job, not the panel. [d125c1d8df51] [b5a6783a503a]
17. **Interviews should reconstruct the purchase timeline and the struggle narrative (storyboarding), not ask about product attributes.** The mattress interview asked nothing about coils, firmness or price; it surfaced a year of back pain, a spouse conflict, and germaphobia about mattress stores. [da4191b7964a] [63c8da8b3648] [ab9f2f70e75c]
18. **Depth beats breadth: a small number of rich interviews outperforms a large number of shallow data points** for finding the causal job, though quantitative follow-up is still needed to confirm prevalence once a job is found. [c4d6dd183e5b] [f13714393d43]
19. **Distinguish the "Big Hire" (the purchase) from the "Little Hire" (the repeated moment of use).** Most company data only tracks the Big Hire; a truly solved job is confirmed by repeated Little Hires, not by the sale alone (unused phone apps as the negative example). [ec89bc0904f5] [16f58e3ab952] [e5b43e1bd5d6]
20. **A genuine insight "feels true on conception"** (quoting HBS's Gerald Zaltman) — you should not need to argue yourself into believing a real job insight.

### D. From job to product: job spec, experiences, purpose brand

21. **The job spec translates the customer's job into the innovator's design brief**: functional/emotional/social dimensions, the tradeoffs the customer will accept, the full competitive set to be beaten, and the obstacles/anxieties to remove. [e30632499b68] [fddf69615826] [88dec6a091ff]
22. **Products that nail a job function as services**, and the experiences of purchase and use — not the core product — are what create durable competitive advantage, because experiences and the internal processes behind them are what's hardest to copy (American Girl's packaging ritual; IKEA's childcare + single-tool flat-pack design; Uber's push-button ETA and driver ID). [f45664fa39fe] [dc943dfef776] [a197e843c9cc]
23. **Customers gladly pay a premium when a solution nails the job (IKEA, American Girl), but resent a premium created by lock-in/interdependency (printer ink, phone chargers)** — the book explicitly separates these as different phenomena; only the first is described as a "jobs win."
24. **Purpose brands own a specific job in the customer's mind, precluding consideration of alternatives**, and are built by being hired successfully and repeatedly, rarely by advertising alone (FedEx, OnStar, IKEA, Sawzall/HOLE HAWG). Purpose brands erode when a company chases new features/segments instead of protecting the job (Volvo after the Ford acquisition; V8's proliferation into flavor variants after its "quadrupled sales" jobs-based relaunch). [2b63dd9f725f] [1da34b764041]
25. **Online reviews function as job-matching evidence, not just quality signals** — buyers look for reviewers who share their job (does this toaster oven reheat a frozen pizza, not just brown toast evenly), and a business now has to signal who should *not* hire the product, since a wrong-fit buyer can produce a lasting bad review. [541818498033] [741dd2ac873a]

### E. Organizing and measuring around the job

26. **Processes, not resources or products, are the real source of hard-to-copy competitive advantage** — because processes are largely invisible and built from hundreds of small decisions (Toyota let competitors tour its plants because the real advantage was in unspoken process, not visible steps). [59d7dc3a768a] [ecb46611592c]
27. **Organize a team's actual coordination and metrics around the job, not the org chart.** Standard reorgs move boxes and reporting lines and mostly fail (a cited 2010 Bain study found fewer than one-third of 57 reorgs reviewed produced meaningful improvement); a shared job gives cross-functional teams (like CFPB's policy committee) a reason to stop "arguing about the scars" and instead solve the problem together. [b28cb75bb455] [41516283a4bc]
28. **Measure customer-benefit metrics, not internal-activity metrics.** SNHU switched from "packages mailed" to "minutes to callback"; Amazon tracks delivery date, not ship date; Intuit nearly broke a good product by forcing phone registration to drive upsell revenue instead of measuring ease of first use. [c79827d20a3f] [9b38f4060bd2]
29. **A clear job statement functions as a "commander's intent"**, letting employees make good autonomous decisions without micromanagement (OnStar waiving upgrade fees during Hurricane Rita; SNHU staff buying a struggling student groceries; unprompted, because each act was obviously consistent with the job). [51230f4f32c9] [2c9eb2206eeb]
30. **Watch for the Three Fallacies of Innovation Data as a company scales**: (1) active data crowding out passive data, (2) "surface growth" — selling more products/jobs to the same customers instead of nailing the core job better, which invites focused disruptors, and (3) conforming data — every function brings self-serving data to a decision and none of it reflects the customer's job. [e95a35c04ebc] [4f376b72845a] [39e7eece8ab9]
31. **Organizations drift away from the job as they scale**, because growth forces added management layers, and managers get further from customer context and closer to internally benchmarked "competencies." This is a persistent structural risk, not a one-time failure. [336e01894bc3]

## 3. Procedures

**Procedure: mine for job candidates (chapter 4/9662bde03a61 method).**
1. Look in your own life and the lives of people close to you for an unresolved struggle outside the product category (Sony's Morita, Sal Khan, Sheila Marcelo/Care.com all started here).
2. Study nonconsumers: people who buy nothing in the category, and find out what they do instead (or nothing).
3. Watch for workarounds and compensating behaviors: duct-tape fixes, spreadsheets, manual routines, symbolic substitutes (the author's "Bank of Daddy").
4. Identify negative jobs: tasks people dread or actively avoid, and what they currently do to escape them (CVS MinuteClinic from "I don't want to see the doctor").
5. Examine unusual/off-label uses of an existing product (Arm & Hammer baking soda in the fridge/laundry; NyQuil taken just to sleep).
6. For each candidate, record a verbatim quote and the trigger circumstance, not just an inferred need. [needs brand data: interview transcripts, support tickets, return reasons, and review text for the brand's own products]

**Procedure: run a Jobs-to-Be-Done interview (chapter 5, "The Mattress Agenda").**
1. Reconstruct the full purchase TIMELINE, starting as far back as the first thought of buying, not the day of purchase. Ask when, where, with whom, what else was happening.
2. Do not ask about product attributes (coils, color, firmness, price) — ask about the struggle: what wasn't working, what was tried first, who else was affected.
3. Probe for the moment the "little hire" (ongoing use) started failing, since repeated failed little hires often precede and explain the eventual big hire.
4. Ask what was fired — the old product, a compensating behavior, or the choice to do nothing — and what finally made that untenable.
5. Identify the four forces at play: what pushed (the growing problem), what pulled (the specific new option seen), what habit/anxiety held them back, and what removed that anxiety at the final moment (e.g. spouse touching the product in-store; a lenient return policy).
6. Treat the result as ONE data point supporting hypotheses, not a proven conclusion; cluster with further interviews before acting. [c4d6dd183e5b] [f13714393d43]

**Procedure: storyboard the customer's struggle (chapter 5, Airbnb pre-launch example).**
1. Use a simple narrative structure: "Once upon a time... Every day... One day... Because of that, we did this... Because of this, we did that... Finally I did..."
2. Map discrete emotional moments across the full journey (Airbnb mapped 45 such moments for hosts and guests before launch), including the first "little hire" moment (e.g. arrival at the home).
3. Design specific experiential details for each identified moment (soft music or a scented candle at arrival) rather than leaving the moment generic. [df9261a8fe1b] [63c8da8b3648]

**Procedure: validate a job statement before using it (chapter 10, [b2121bd6e7ce]).**
1. Write the candidate job as a verb + noun phrase of progress. Reject any statement made only of adjectives/adverbs (e.g. "convenience," "premium," "trustworthy") — these describe an experience, not the job.
2. List the products/services that could conceivably be "hired" to solve it. If they all come from the same product category, go up one level of abstraction and repeat.
3. Stop when the candidate solutions span genuinely different categories (a job solved by a banana, a bagel, a Snickers bar AND a milkshake is at the right level; a job solved only by different milkshake brands is not).
4. Treat this as iterative, "more art than science" per the source; expect several passes.

**Procedure: write the job spec (chapter 6).**
1. State the functional, emotional and social dimensions of the desired progress.
2. List the tradeoffs the customer is willing to make (what quality means to them, what they'll sacrifice).
3. List the full competitive set the solution must beat, including nonconsumption and out-of-category alternatives.
4. List the obstacles and anxieties (at both purchase and use) that must be removed.
5. Use the spec as the blueprint for product, packaging, and messaging decisions — not a one-time document, but an ongoing filter. [e30632499b68] [88dec6a091ff]

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Is this actually a job, or a persona?** Check: does the statement include a circumstance, and functional + emotional + social dimensions? If it only lists age/gender/income, it fails. [3f6f208703f1]
- **Is this actually a job, or a preference/spec?** Check: do the candidate solutions span different product categories? If not, go up an abstraction level. [b2121bd6e7ce]
- **Is the avatar record backed by evidence?** Check: does every key claim (the job, the trigger, the objections) carry a verbatim quote and a source? Remove or flag claims that don't. [294308de8309]
- **Are you seduced by active data?** Check: can you point to passive-data evidence (a described struggle, a workaround, a firing decision) behind this segment, or only sales/CRM fields? [da14b9693dec]
- **Is the org drifting from the job?** Check: in a resource or roadmap dispute, is anyone debating "what job are we solving" versus arguing spreadsheets/campaigns only? The source's rule of thumb: internal disagreement about tactics is fine; nobody should be debating the job itself. [336e01894bc3]
- **Is this "surface growth" or real growth?** Check: is the next initiative solving the *same* job better, or a *different* job for the same existing customers? The latter risks confusing customers and inviting a focused, single-job disruptor. [4f376b72845a]
- **Is your review/return strategy actually a job-fit filter?** Check: do product pages set expectations precisely enough that a mismatched buyer self-selects out before purchase, rather than after? [741dd2ac873a]

## 6. Tools, settings and named features

- **Job spec** — a structured brief (functional/emotional/social dimensions, tradeoffs, competitive set, obstacles) used as the design blueprint; a documentation practice, not software. [verify UI: n/a, conceptual tool]
- **Storyboarding** — narrative interview/synthesis technique borrowed from Pixar-style story structure; used by Airbnb pre-launch for 45 emotional moments.
- **"Emotional score"** — Intermountain Healthcare's term (Todd Dunn) for the emotional dimension of a physical environment/interaction that a purely functional design misses (crinkly exam-table paper; a doctor choosing pen-and-paper over the EHR to keep eye contact).
- **"Design for People"** — Intermountain's internal jobs-based innovation framework requiring functional + emotional + social observation before writing an innovation brief.
- **Amazon's mock press release** — an internal practice of writing the customer-facing press release for a new initiative at kickoff, stating the job it solves, before development starts, so every function aligns to it from day one.
- **Amazon shopping-price robot** — automated system scanning benchmark competitor prices twice daily and auto-adjusting price down (with a gross-margin floor that triggers human review) to protect the "low prices" job metric.
- **Mercer's Pension Risk Exchange** — a real-time marketplace plus a scenario-modeling/monitoring tool, built specifically to reduce the anxiety and opacity of a slow B2B procurement process.
- **"Stack fallacy"** — a named cognitive trap (credited to Anshu Sharma, Storm Ventures): engineers overvalue their own technology layer and undervalue the customer application one level up; cited via the Ford/Qualcomm Wingcast failure against OnStar.

## 7. Examples and case results

All results below are as reported in the source (often self-reported by the company or executive named); they are examples of a jobs-based decision, not proof the method causes the outcome.

- **Milkshake / fast-food chain (unnamed):** 18-hour in-store observation found many shakes bought before 9am by solo commuters, revealing an AM job ("keep me occupied and full until 10am") distinct from a PM parent-child "bonding/yes moment" job — two different jobs, two different competitor sets, requiring two different fixes, not one averaged product change.
- **Unilever margarine (~1997):** JTBD pitch presented to Unilever executives at HBS was rejected ("tepid reception"); the source notes margarine/spreads later lost share to butter and olive oil and was spun off by Unilever in 2014 — explicitly framed as "I can't help but wonder," not proven causation.
- **SNHU (Southern New Hampshire University), president Paul LeBlanc from 2003:** discovered two distinct jobs (on-campus "coming of age" vs. online "credential fast, with dignity, competing against nonconsumption"); cut financial-aid response time from 24hrs+weeks to a callback in <10 minutes; by fiscal 2016, ~$535M revenue, 34% CAGR over 6 years, 75,000+ online students, 95% of surveyed graduates said they'd do it again (early 2016).
- **Detroit condo builder (Bob Moesta, unnamed company):** dining-room-table anxiety revealed the real job as "moving lives," not selling features; fixes (smaller 2nd bedroom to fit a table, 2 years free storage, 3 fixed unit variants instead of 30-page customization) cut cancellations from 5-6/month to 1/month and grew the business 25% in 2007 while the industry fell 49%.
- **Kimberly-Clark Depend Silhouette:** redesigned adult incontinence product to not look/feel like a diaper; $60M sales year 1, 30% growth year 2, without cannibalizing the existing line.
- **ING Direct:** built around "no fee, no minimum, feel-good savings" job; became fastest-growing US bank; sold to Capital One for $9B in 2012.
- **P&G Pampers, China:** abandoned the "10-cent functional diaper" framing after focus-group research revealed a sleep/marital-intimacy job; 2-year study with Beijing Children's Hospital found babies slept 30% faster/30 min longer; by 2013, ~$1.6B in sales, ~30% market share.
- **Medtronic Healthy Heart for All, India:** reframed from "better pacemaker" to "navigate the care pathway" (screening clinics, 2-day bridge loans, hospital patient-navigator "Sherpas"); by Dec 2015, 100+ hospital partnerships in 30 cities, ~167,000 screened, ~15,000 treated.
- **GM OnStar, CEO Chet Huber:** reframed from "cool brochure-ware" to "peace of mind while driving" after the team spent an hour each listening to real crash/emergency calls; built an out-of-cycle validation process to out-iterate the standard auto dev cycle; by 2009 OnStar was a stated purchase driver for GM cars; contrast case Ford/Qualcomm's "Wingcast" (announced 2000) never shipped and was scrapped in ~2002.
- **Intuit / TurboTax:** reframed the job from "improve the tax interview" to "get my taxes done, period" under GM Sasan Goodarzi; a 2015 test prefilling just one questionnaire "chapter" raised completion rates.
- **Deseret News, CEO Clark Gilbert:** after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing exposed print's total loss on breaking news, reframed around a "like-minded believers" job segment (self-reported ~56% of US news consumers, invisible to demographics); social following grew from "low millions" to 100M+ across its FamilyShare Network.
- **V8 (Campbell's Soup):** reframed from "juice vs. juice" to "V8 vs. peeling carrots"; sales reportedly quadrupled within a year; later drifted back into product-line proliferation (Spicy Hot V8, V8 Splash!, etc.), diluting the purpose brand — offered as a cautionary bookend to its own win.
- **American Girl (Pleasant Rowland):** built with zero market research validation (one hostile focus group ignored); 29M dolls sold, $500M+ annual sales, avg. buyer spend $600+; sold to Mattel after 13 years for $700M. Internal shorthand: "American Girl is story, not stuff." The "belly band" packaging wrap added 2 cents and 27 seconds per unit purely to make unwrapping feel special — Rowland rejected a cheaper/faster print-on-box alternative.
- **BMW (2008 recession era):** reframed from "high performance cars" / "a man's car" to the job of "mobility, get me from A to B painlessly," after California anti-combustion legislation, urbanization and falling teen licensing rates. Quote (Steven Althaus, global director of brand management and marketing services): "We realized we were competing with companies whose names we couldn't pronounce eighteen months ago." Led to the BMWi electric/hybrid line and the DriveNow car-sharing pilot (Berlin, Vienna, San Francisco, London). Ford's Mark Fields (late 2015) and GM's investment in Lyft plus its own Maven ride-share (early 2016) are cited as parallel industry moves, without result figures.
- **FranklinCovey (CEO Bob Whitman):** after revenue fell from a $589M peak (2000) to $130M (2009), Whitman personally visited ~400 customers over 3 years and found the real B2B job was helping HR/L&D leaders be seen as mission-critical to the business, not "buy training courses." Shifted from selling individual courses to unlimited-access subscriptions plus outcome-owned engagements (e.g. a full-time embedded coach for deals above $500M). Revenue reached $220M by 2015 (9.2% CAGR over 6 years); over the following 6 years FranklinCovey doubled revenue and increased profit tenfold while shifting ~2/3 of its R&D budget to process innovation rather than new content.
- **Intuit / QuickBooks (cofounder Scott Cook):** small-business owners were mis-using the personal-finance app Quicken as a workaround because they didn't want real accounting complexity, only confidence that "money moves in and out." QuickBooks hid the accounting mechanics; priced at roughly 2x cost for about half the functionality of full accounting software, it still became category leader because its real competitors were a hired bookkeeper, a spreadsheet, or a shoebox of receipts (nonconsumption), not other accounting software. By the mid-2010s, Intuit's revenue was ~$4B with a ~$25B market cap.
- **Church & Dwight (Arm & Hammer baking soda), late 1960s:** management noticed customers already using baking soda in laundry, toothpaste, on carpets and in the fridge, and expanded deliberately into laundry detergent, cat litter, carpet cleaner, air freshener and deodorant around each distinct job. The original orange-box baking soda is now under 7% of Arm & Hammer's consumer revenue. NyQuil → ZzzQuil is cited as a parallel unusual-use discovery (people dosing NyQuil just to sleep, not for cold symptoms).
- **CVS MinuteClinic / QuickMedx:** founded by HBS alum Rick Krieger after a frustrating ER wait for his son's strep test, built around the negative job "I don't want to see the doctor" for routine ailments; grew to 1,000+ walk-in locations inside CVS stores across 33 states.
- **OpenTable:** born from the common workaround of calling a restaurant, then calling friends back to renegotiate a time, then repeating for a second restaurant — real-time online reservations solved the coordination job directly.
- **Airbnb (cofounder Brian Chesky; head of hospitality Chip Conley):** started from Chesky's own job (rent air mattresses to afford conference fees/rent as a broke new grad); Conley reports ~40% of guests say they would not have traveled at all without Airbnb (pure nonconsumption), and virtually all hosts would never otherwise have rented a room. Pre-launch, the team storyboarded 45 emotional moments across the host/guest journey.
- **Uber:** competitive advantage framed as experience, not the car — push-button hailing with a 3-7 minute ETA, known driver identity/rating, and cashless payment remove the safety and payment anxieties of hailing a cab; named as competing with taxis, car services, the subway, and calling a friend for a ride. Car-rental industry named by contrast as failing to fix its own poor experience (cites #hertzsucks/#avissucks) despite obvious workaround behavior (business travelers imposing on junior staff, or paying for a cross-city Uber rather than rent a car after a flight diversion).
- **Amazon:** reviews (not price or selection) are named as the hardest thing for a competitor to copy, because they let buyers find people who "hired for the same job." Cites a 2015 algorithm update weighting newer reviews, verified purchases and helpfulness more heavily, and a cited stat (PowerReviews-sourced, dated) that up to 95% of consumers use reviews and 86% call them essential to purchase decisions.
- **Volvo:** founded 1927 on an explicit safety purpose ("the guiding principle behind everything we make... is and must remain, safety"); after Ford's 1999 acquisition it drifted into flashier/luxury-style cars, lost its purpose-brand position, was unprofitable by 2005, and was sold to China's Geely in 2010 at a substantial loss. Quote (Tony Nicolosi, Volvo North America CEO, to Autoweek 2013): "We lost our way... We gotta go back to our roots." Under Geely's ownership Volvo began growing again in 2015, but the book states it "may have lost its purpose brand status forever."
- **Mercer (Jacques Goulet, global president of the retirement business from 2013):** facing the shift from defined-benefit to defined-contribution pensions eroding its advisory revenue from ~30,000 corporate clients, reframed the client job from "find a pension plan" to "manage this high-stakes, reputation-bearing decision with confidence" (old process could take up to 6 months, with market risk and no transparency). Built the "Pension Risk Exchange," a real-time marketplace plus a scenario-modeling tool, launched in the US, UK and Canada.
- **Intermountain Healthcare (director of innovation Todd Dunn):** a personal patient visit revealed the "emotional score" missed by purely functional design (crinkly exam-table paper; a doctor choosing pen-and-paper sketches over the hospital's own advanced EHR software specifically to maintain eye contact and calm the patient). Led to an internal "Design for People" framework requiring functional + emotional + social observation before writing any innovation brief.
- **Sargento ultrathin sliced cheese:** solved the job "enjoy the cheese experience on my sandwich without the calories/fat/guilt"; $50M in year-1 sales, $150M+ in year 2, against ~3,400 other CPG launches that year that mostly failed within 12 months (quote, VP Rod Hogan: Jobs Theory "forces you to define the offering in the context of a very specific consumer struggle").
- **Sony Walkman (founder Akio Morita):** launched against market-research advice (research said no one would buy a non-recording tape player with earphones) on Morita's own instinct to "carefully watch how people live"; sold over 330 million units.
- **Khan Academy (Sal Khan) and Care.com (Sheila Marcelo):** both cited as founders who built from an unresolved job in their own life (a 12-year-old cousin's math frustration; the founder's own childcare struggle); Care.com reported, by the time of writing, ~10M members across 16 countries and ~$60M revenue in under 10 years.
- **Drybar:** a single-service hair-blowout salon chain, cited briefly in chapter 10 as a fast-growing multi-city example of a narrowly and precisely defined job (a "going-out ritual" experience), with no revenue figures given.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Treating demographics or "average customer" profiles as the causal explanation for a purchase (the Air Force "average pilot" cockpit story: redesigned for the average of 4,000+ pilots, and literally no pilot fit it).
- Asking customers what features to add (Intuit's "feature chase": customers listed up to ~150 wanted features with no way to prioritize among them).
- Averaging conflicting jobs into one "average" solution that satisfies no one (the milkshake chain's original approach; SNHU's original one-size-fits-all recruiting).
- Confusing the Big Hire with proof the job is solved, when only repeated Little Hires prove it.
- Chasing "surface growth" (more products/jobs to the same customer base) instead of nailing the core job better, leaving the company exposed to a single-job-focused disrupter (the *New York Times* losing distinct jobs one-by-one to The Economist, TV and Metro).
- Letting active, easy-to-collect data (sales, CRM, benchmarks) replace the harder-to-gather passive data of customer struggle.
- Believing quantitative data is inherently more "objective" than qualitative/case data — both are human-curated abstractions (cites the Google Flu Trends overestimation failure as a caution against trusting a large, computable dataset uncritically).
- Letting a brand's feature/line proliferation erode a hard-won purpose brand (Volvo after Ford's ownership; V8's own later drift).
- Assuming "it's trivial to build the layer above yours" — the "stack fallacy" — mistaking product/technical expertise for customer-job understanding (Ford/Qualcomm's Wingcast vs. OnStar).
- Not signaling who a product is *not* for, risking alienated wrong-fit buyers and their negative reviews.

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Evidence standard is narrative, not statistical.** The book is explicit that it was built inductively from cases and interviews, not from a testable, falsifiable dataset; it invites readers to report anomalies rather than claiming completeness. Treat every quantitative outcome in section 7 as a single-case example, never a benchmark.
- **Most case numbers are self-reported by the profiled company or executive**, sometimes identified only anonymously ("a gentleman... from Campbell's," "the fast-food chain"), and are not independently audited in the text.
- **The book explicitly bounds its own theory (chapter 10):** Jobs Theory does not apply where there is no real struggle, where the decision is purely rational/mathematical (e.g. commodities trading), or where a "job" statement is really an attribute (convenience) or a same-category preference. Applying it outside these bounds, the authors say, "dilutes its clarity and predictability."
- **No digital-native or paid-acquisition ecommerce case is used.** Every case is either a legacy brand/retailer/B2B firm or a platform business (Airbnb, Uber, Amazon, OpenTable) reasoned about at the strategy level, not at the level of an ad, landing page or checkout flow. `[needs brand data: does not map to CAC/LTV, ad creative testing or funnel-stage behavior without translation]`
- **The book is explicitly compatible with, not a replacement for, design thinking** (chapter 5 endnote): design thinking supplies empathy/divergent thinking/iteration; Jobs Theory supplies the causal "why" language. Do not treat this source as a full research-methods manual; it deliberately withholds a single "black box" method.
- **No conflict with other ecomOS sources found in this pass** (this is a lone book in this folder for this task); a real tension to flag when the folder playbook is built: this source explicitly downgrades demographic/psychographic segmentation as *causal* evidence, while much standard ecommerce media-buying practice (lookalike audiences, demographic targeting) still relies on it for *reach*. The source itself allows demographics as a targeting layer, just not as the definition of the avatar's motivation — this is a distinction worth preserving rather than resolving.

### Adapting the book's methods to ecommerce customer avatars (card writer's / this pass's adaptation, not the book's own claim)

The book never discusses ecommerce, DTC brands, ad creative or landing pages. The mappings below are inferred for ecomOS's use case and are explicitly marked as adaptation, not sourced from the text.

- **Nonconsumption → cart abandoners and category non-buyers, not just competitor customers.** For a brand's avatar work, treat people who visited the category and bought nothing (or bought a manual/DIY workaround) as a job-discovery source, not noise to discard. `[needs brand data: site analytics on non-converting visitors, on-site search terms with no purchase, category browse-without-buy sessions]`
- **Workarounds → return reasons, support tickets and DIY/hack content.** The book's "duct-tape and Velcro" workaround signal maps to: customer-service tickets describing a jury-rigged use of the product, reviews describing "I use this for X instead," and return reasons that reveal an unmet job. `[needs brand data: support ticket themes, return-reason codes, review text mining]`
- **Negative jobs → checkout friction and pre-purchase dread.** "Things people don't want to do" maps well to ecommerce: sizing anxiety, fear of a bad fit sight-unseen, dread of return/refund hassle. Signaling and removing these (free returns, size guides, video reviews) is the ecommerce analogue of ING Direct's cafés or MinuteClinic's walk-in model.
- **Unusual uses → review mining and UGC.** Arm & Hammer's "watch how people actually use it" maps directly to mining reviews, UGC and social comments for off-label uses signaling an adjacent job (e.g., a supplement bought as a gift, not for the buyer).
- **Big Hire vs. Little Hire → first purchase vs. repeat purchase / subscription retention.** For DTC/subscription brands this is close to a direct match: track whether the product is actually re-consumed/re-ordered, not just bought once, before declaring a job "solved." This is one of the more directly portable ideas in the book.
- **Job spec → the avatar record itself.** ecomOS avatar records should carry the functional/emotional/social job statement, the real competitive set (including nonconsumption and out-of-category alternatives, not just competitor SKUs), the anxieties to defuse on the product page/checkout, and a verbatim quote for each — this is what card [294308de8309] and [e30632499b68] already prescribe, and it is a close, direct translation of the book's job spec.
- **Purpose brand → category-defining positioning for a brand or hero SKU.** Useful as an aspiration for brand positioning work, but the book's examples (FedEx, Uber, IKEA) are built over years of consistent operational delivery, not a single campaign; treat this as a long-horizon brand goal, not a landing-page tactic.
- **Reviews as job-matching evidence → product-page and PDP review curation.** This is the most direct, ready-to-use translation: surface reviews by use case, encourage recency/verified-purchase weighting, and write PDP copy that lets a wrong-fit buyer self-select out before purchase, to reduce returns and 1-star "wrong job" reviews. `[needs brand data: current review volume/recency mix, return-reason data by SKU]`
- **What does NOT translate cleanly:** the book's organizational chapters (7-9: processes, metrics, mission statements, org drift) are written for large, multi-department companies (GM, Intuit, Mercer, a university). For a small ecommerce/agency operation, the underlying instinct (measure the customer's job, not internal activity; let the job settle disputes) is portable, but the specific mechanisms (a Mayo-Clinic-style process owner, a CFPB-style policy committee) are not sized for ecomOS's brands and should not be copied literally.
