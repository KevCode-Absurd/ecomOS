## 1. What this source is for

A fast, low-cost pipeline for finding dropshipping product candidates from two channels: organic TikTok scrolling and paid ad-spy tools (PP Ads, Facebook Ad Library). It gives a validation stack to run on any candidate (competitor count, view/engagement audit, revenue estimate, sourcing margin) before committing money, and a simple framework (evergreen vs. trending) for deciding how much testing budget and patience a product deserves. It does not cover creative production, store build, or fulfillment setup in any depth (those are out of scope for this folder per its own header).

## 2. Principles

1. **Competitor profit is the primary viability filter, not "is it saturated."** Before anything else, require evidence that at least one other seller is moving the product and making money. The presenter argues this matters more than abstract checks like "solves a problem" or "is in demand." `[f966191090da]`
2. **Saturation is reframed, not treated as disqualifying.** There are too many people online to reach everyone with one product, so a big competitor does not mean no room is left; he cites teeth-whitening pens (associated with the brand HiSmile) as a category with continual new, profitable entrants despite heavy saturation. This is the presenter's opinion and it directly contradicts the common "pick an unsaturated niche" advice — treat both as situational, not resolved. `[f966191090da]`
3. **When a niche looks saturated, look for an untapped marketing angle instead of abandoning it.** Example: a clothing brand in the (very saturated) clothing niche targeting only "girlfriends of boyfriends 5'8\" and under" — the narrow angle both hooks attention in the ad and speaks directly to an underserved sub-demographic. Caveat: saturation can still raise paid-acquisition cost; an angle differentiates messaging, it does not guarantee cheaper traffic. `[f966191090da]`
4. **Split every candidate into evergreen or trending, because the expected effort and payoff differ.** Evergreen = sellable year-round, brandable for years, but slower to start and requires more ad testing/iteration before it works. Trending = seasonal or culturally viral (Christmas, Mother's Day, a pop-culture moment), delivers fast cash, and is likely gone within weeks. Match your goal (brand equity vs. quick cash) to the category before investing testing budget. `[4ff7704274e2]`
5. **Customization is treated as an independent demand signal.** Products the buyer personalizes (a phone case that scans and prints a photo, a vintage-style crystal frame for a custom image) are called out as advertising well on TikTok because "people love custom items." This is the presenter's general observation, not a tested benchmark, and it ignores that customization adds production/fulfillment handling. `[6cb10ef0ecb5]`
6. **Heavy, repeated saturation can still justify a short test if proof of concept is strong enough.** If you see at least 10 different accounts selling the exact same product, that is both a saturation warning and a validation signal. Judge brand potential honestly (the presenter says the ice-cube AC unit example "doesn't look like a great product" long-term) and treat it as a cash-flow test, not a foundation to overbuild on. `[f0dd2055cfb2]`
7. **Secondary sourcing channels (Twitter/X practitioners, Exploding Topics) are leads, not conclusions.** He calls Twitter content mixed quality ("some of them are just straight up cringe") and gives no execution detail for either channel. Every idea from these should go through the same competitor/demand/revenue/sourcing checks as a TikTok or PP Ads find. `[bdee2e77e91b]`

## 3. Procedures

### A. TikTok organic scouting (feed-training method)
1. Create a new TikTok account dedicated to product research.
2. Engage (watch fully, like) with every blatantly product-pitching ("dropshipper-style") video you see. These are recognizable by direct product pitches and reused sounds/hooks.
3. Keep scrolling; the algorithm progressively feeds more of the same once it reads the engagement pattern.
4. When a product catches your eye and meets the basic bar (problem-solving OR serves a passionate community), stop scrolling for discovery and pivot immediately into the validation stack (procedure G/H below) rather than continuing to browse.
5. Speed benchmark from the presenter's own run: found a viable-looking candidate (anime katanas) within about 40 seconds of scrolling on a fresh account — offered as an example of how fast this can go, not a guaranteed timeline.
`[ef25d34ce2bb]`

### B. One-hit-wonder audit on a creator's profile
1. From the viral video, open the creator's/account's profile page.
2. Count how many of their videos have over 1,000,000 views.
3. Require at least 3 videos above 1,000,000 views before concluding the product/account has repeatable viral pull rather than a fluke.
4. If it passes, continue to sourcing and competitor/revenue checks; if it fails, keep scrolling.
Caveat: this 3-video/1M threshold is a personal heuristic, not a measured benchmark, and high view counts can reflect the creator's personality rather than the product's market.
`[056889323762]`

### C. PP Ads filter workflow (paid ad-spy tool) `[verify UI]`
1. Open PP Ads and apply your preferred base filter (niche/keyword).
2. Sort by "Days running," longest-running ads first — used here as a proxy for baseline profitability (an ad that keeps running is presumed to be making money for the advertiser).
3. Set results to 500 per page to scan broadly per sort pass.
4. Scan the list and skip heavily branded, established mega-corporations (their own logo, polished branded site) — they leave little room to adapt/compete.
5. Look for generic, adaptable products a new seller could source and rebrand.
6. When something interesting appears, open the advertiser's website to judge whether it looks like a dropshipper or an established brand `[dropshipping assumption]`.
7. Reverse-image search the product photo to AliExpress to get raw supplier cost and margin floor (worked example: a travel bag sourced around $16, sold by the advertiser for $98).
8. Inside that advertiser's ad records, sort by ad impressions to find their top-performing creative; study the exact target audience, ad copy, and number of creative variants running as inspiration for your own angle.
`[c37e17bb2b06]`

### D. Revenue validation with a spy tool (e.g., ShopHunter) `[verify UI]`
1. Take a competitor's store URL (sourced from TikTok, PP Ads, or the Facebook Ad Library).
2. Enter it into a revenue-estimation tool such as ShopHunter.
3. Read the estimated monthly sales figure as directional confirmation only (worked example: one account estimated at roughly $90,000 for the prior month).
4. Use the tool's own search (e.g., searching "katana") to surface other stores selling the same/similar product and compare estimated volumes.
5. Cross-check with Facebook Ad Library ad-volume for the same advertiser (step E) before concluding the product is profitable at scale.
Caveat: these are third-party estimates, not verified sales data, and a single strong month can reflect a seasonality or ad-spend spike, not a stable baseline.
`[c4db64af21f5]`

### E. Facebook Ad Library keyword hunting (free) `[verify UI]`
1. Open the Facebook Ad Library.
2. Search generic direct-response phrases such as "50% off" or "shop now" (or a specific competitor's name).
3. Review the advertisers that surface — most likely dropshippers or direct-response ecommerce brands `[dropshipping assumption]`.
4. Inspect what products they sell, what the ads look like, and how many ads are active simultaneously.
5. Treat a high count of concurrent active ads as a signal the product/offer is working, and use the creative set as inspiration.
Caveat: ad volume is a proxy for spend and testing activity, not guaranteed profitability, and the free library is less filterable than a paid ad-spy tool.
`[3d818b03cb3b]`

### F. Reverse-sourcing a product image to find supplier cost `[verify UI]`
1. Right-click the competitor's product image (on their site or in their ad).
2. Run Google's reverse image search ("Search by Image").
3. Scroll the results until an AliExpress (or similar) listing appears; the presenter found a near-identical listing quickly in both worked examples.
4. Read the raw unit price on that listing as your margin floor (examples: anime katana selling at ~$75, sourced at ~$40 on the first listing found, "definitely" cheaper elsewhere; travel bag selling at $98, sourced at $16).
5. If the margin holds, message suppliers about customization or bulk pricing once you would be placing bulk orders — noted as something the established brands he found had already done.
Caveat: the listing found may differ in quality/size/spec from the competitor's actual unit; shipping time and supplier reliability need separate verification before ordering. `[dropshipping assumption]`
`[39e6f64e0acb]`

### G. Full worked example: anime katana (combines A, B, D, F)
1. Organic TikTok find: second video on a fresh feed (procedure A).
2. Accepted despite not solving a problem, because the anime fan community is judged highly passionate.
3. Creator-profile audit: confirmed at least 3 videos over 1,000,000 views (procedure B) — passed.
4. Price check: store sells the item for about $75.
5. Reverse-image sourcing: found on AliExpress for about $40 on the first listing (procedure F).
6. Competitor search: searched "anime katana" on TikTok, found a second seller with 2.8 million likes on one video.
7. Revenue check: ran the competitor's store through ShopHunter, got an estimate of about $90,000 for the prior month (procedure D).
8. Conclusion: competitors exist, demand signals are present, sourcing is workable — proceed to test.
`[5be11a6e0db5]`

### H. Master validation checklist (apply before committing to any product)
1. Confirm competitors exist and are making money selling it (principle 1) — the core gate.
2. Confirm the product either solves a problem or serves a passionate community.
3. Confirm demand through repeated viral evidence, multiple competitors, or high active-ad volume in the Facebook Ad Library.
4. Confirm sourcing: reverse-image search to find supplier cost/margin floor, and check shipping speed.
5. Confirm the marketing angle you would use is not already fully occupied — look for a sub-demographic or creative angle competitors have not used.
6. Only then move to store build, creative production, and testing; once you have seen what works for competitors, adapt it rather than starting from a blank page.
Caveat: this is a practitioner's screening framework, not a statistical model — any single signal can be wrong in isolation. Fulfillment tooling is explicitly out of scope for this checklist per this folder's own header note.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Treating any visible competitor as proof of "saturation" and walking away, instead of checking whether they are actually profitable and whether an untapped angle exists.
- Trusting a single viral video without auditing the creator's whole profile — risks chasing a one-hit-wonder rather than a repeatable product.
- Spending effort trying to compete head-on with fully-established mega-brands found in ad-spy tools, where there is little room to differentiate or adapt.
- Skipping the sourcing/margin check (reverse image search) before committing to a product.
- Treating passive lead sources (Twitter accounts, Exploding Topics) as primary research rather than as unverified leads that still need the full validation stack.
- Overbuilding a brand identity around a short-lifespan trending or heavily-time-boxed seasonal product (example: the ice-cube AC unit) instead of treating it as a quick cash test.
