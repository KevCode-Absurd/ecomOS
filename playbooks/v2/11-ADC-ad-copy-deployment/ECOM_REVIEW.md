# Ecommerce review: 11-ADC ad copy playbook

## 1. Score

Score: 3 out of 5.

The procedures, checklists and deliverables (sections 4, 5, 9) turn the copy into real store jobs: a static ad set with 3 variants, an email that treats the store's own list differently from a cold ad, a claim audit tied to platform policy, a variant plan judged on cost per purchase. But the decision-rule library underneath (section 3, 123 rules) is mostly general direct-response copywriting from print, SaaS and coaching sources, carried into ecommerce with a note or an era tag rather than rewritten for a store. The playbook says more than once that none of its 9 sources shows Meta limits, product-page rules, catalogue-scale copy, SMS or discount-code strategy, so it names several core store jobs as gaps rather than filling them.

Rule count, out of 123 rules in section 3:

- (a) store-specific, written for a store's own surface: 1. Only R102 (Meta primary text and headline behavior) has no generic version underneath.
- (b) generic but correctly applied to a store: 42. A general principle plus a real store note, example, term or conflict pointer (review mining, cost per purchase, PDP vs sales-page headline, store-list awareness, feed paragraph length).
- (c) generic with no store framing: 80. Reads the same for a SaaS trial page, a coaching funnel or a store: most of headlines, hooks, feature-to-benefit, editing and voice.
- (d) out of scope for a store: 0. No rule is written to apply only outside ecommerce; non-ecommerce sourcing shows up as an era tag and a caveat, not as a rule the agent would follow.

## 2. Prioritization check

No. Section 0's task table orders which sections to load, and rules cite conflicts in section 7, but nothing ranks levers by what moves a store's numbers most, and no rule varies by store type. Draft block:

### 3.0 Ecommerce priority order

1. Claim and compliance audit before anything ships (R107 to R114, P9). An unsupported claim can get the ad account banned; this outranks every craft rule, for any store.
2. Match copy depth to where the traffic is (R16, R23, conflict 7.1). A cold feed gets the short argument before truncation; the list and the PDP get the fuller case. A catalogue brand sets this per collection, not once.
3. Pick 1 idea and 1 appeal per SKU or collection, not per store (R18 to R21). A hero-SKU brand can hold 1 idea across the account; a catalogue brand needs a different idea per hero product.
4. Size the proof to the purchase (R8, R61 to R64). Low-AOV, impulse SKUs need 1 clean image and 1 cue; high-AOV, subscription and considered-purchase SKUs need reviews, comparison and demonstration first. `[general practice: the AOV split itself is not sourced; R8 supports it]`
5. Offer and guarantee wording matches the real mechanism (R68 to R72, R114). Subscription brands frame value across the plan; one-off brands frame the single order. `[general practice: subscription vs one-off split is not sourced]`
6. Keep product identity consistent across every SKU's ad and PDP hero (R33, R34, R104, conflict 7.4). A catalogue brand needs 1 template that still says what is sold.
7. Ad-to-page congruency (R84, R105). Matters most where the brand controls the landing page; less where a marketplace-heavy seller does not. `[general practice: marketplace note]`
8. Write for the list's real knowledge (R16, R52, R78). A store's owned list already knows the product.
9. 1 variable per test, judged on cost per purchase (R116, R120 to R122). This is the store's real measure of a copy change.
10. Format to the live placement limits (R102, R103, P12). Execution-level, fetched fresh each launch.
11. Voice consistency across SKUs without boilerplate (R86, R89). Matters more for a founder-led DTC brand than a marketplace listing. `[general practice]`
12. Editing and readability pass (P11, R92 to R98). Real, but last-mile polish, not the decision that changes results.

## 3. Rule rewrites (15)

Condition, sources and card ids stay as in section 3; only the wording moves toward a store.

1. R3: Write to 1 named shopper choosing between this SKU and the alternative in their cart, on the device named in the brief (phone feed, PDP, inbox).
2. R5: Write for a thumb scrolling a feed or a PDP on a phone; short sentences are a scanning aid, not a style choice.
3. R8: For a low-AOV or impulse SKU, lead on 1 image and 1 cue; for a high-AOV, subscription or considered-purchase SKU, add the spec table, reviews and comparison before the ask.
4. R12: Build 1 feature-and-benefit table per product line, not per SKU or variant, and flag rows that only apply to some sizes or colors.
5. R18: Anchor 1 idea per SKU or collection, never 1 idea for the whole store; a catalogue brand plans a different idea per hero product per cycle.
6. R26: The headline's promise is also the PDP's first line and the collection hero; write it once and reuse it where the brand allows.
7. R43: Close the ad's proof step with a store-owned signal, a star rating, a review count or a return-policy line, not a generic quality claim.
8. R53: Ladder every feature to the buyer's gain using the product's own spec-sheet units (denier, GSM, mAh, wash count), not a marketing adjective.
9. R55: Swap a vague quality word for the unit a shopper filters or searches by on the store (size, material, capacity, color, fit).
10. R57: Cut a spec bullet that answers no filter, size guide or "will this work for me" search; keep it only if a shopper would search for it.
11. R63: Where the brand has a review platform, quote the star rating and review count as proof, not a hand-picked testimonial alone.
12. R67: Name who the product does not fit by size, skin type, use case or climate; this cuts returns as much as it raises trust.
13. R76: Run belief shifts against the store's actual blockers: shipping cost, return hassle, sizing risk and authenticity.
14. R81: Label the single CTA with the store action it triggers (add to cart, shop now, get yours); a product grid counts as 1 action only when the email's whole job is "shop the range."
15. R86: Keep voice consistent across every SKU and collection page without turning bullets into boilerplate; check 3 product pages side by side, not 1.

## 6. Deliverables check

Mostly yes. The static ad set, email copy, headline bank, claim audit and variant plan (9.1, 9.2, 9.4, 9.5, 9.7) are things a store team would open and use, with fields an operator needs: angle, placement limits, claim sources, the variable tested. The gap is 9.3, "page section copy": strong on landing and sales page blocks, with no PDP-specific deliverable, even though the PDP is the store's highest-traffic page and this folder owns its words. Proposal: add 9.8 "Product page copy" (title, bullet block, spec block, size or fit note, FAQ answers sourced to the record), and rename 9.3 to "Landing and sales page copy" so the split is explicit.

## 7. The single highest-value change

Add the "3.0 Ecommerce priority order" block above, or one like it, and make every procedure branch on store type at its first step. A worker or an agent must currently read all 123 rules in section 3 to find what matters most for one store's copy job today. The playbook is honest about its sourcing and thorough on execution, but it treats a 30-dollar impulse SKU on a mobile feed and a 2,000-dollar considered purchase on a desktop landing page as the same writing problem. A short, ranked, store-type front door would turn a well-sourced general copywriting reference into a tool that says what to do first for this brand, this SKU and this AOV.
