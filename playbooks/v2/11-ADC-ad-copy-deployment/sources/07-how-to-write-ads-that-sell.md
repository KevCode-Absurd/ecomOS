## 1. What this source is for

It gives a copywriter a small set of durable selling rules to judge any ad, headline, PDP or email: write as 1 salesperson talking to 1 interested buyer, offer service instead of asking for the sale, never boast, tell the real steps you take to make the product good, use specific numbers, lead with reward not prevention, prove by demonstration, and put the main promise in the headline. It also covers how to research for the one "keystone" fact, why not to trust your own taste, and why not to drop an ad that still works. It is a judgment and review frame. It is thin on process steps, testing method and modern channels.

## 2. Principles

1. **Advertising is salesmanship in print, multiplied.** Its principles are the principles of salesmanship. Every ad question should be answered by a salesman's standard: "Would this help a salesman sell the goods? Would it help me sell if I met the buyer in person?" A mediocre salesman hurts a small part of the business; mediocre advertising hurts all of it, because it reaches thousands at once. Condition: applies to every creative and review decision. Evidence: Hopkins's stated framework, no measured test. [250834566833] [ea531107827e] [e02bc47498e3]
2. **Write to 1 person, not the mass.** "We must treat people in advertising as we treat them in person." Picture a typical individual likely to want what you sell, who has already shown interest (the headline or image caught them), and talk to that person. "However big your business, get down to the units, for those units are all that make size." Evidence: the sewing machine inquiry case (section 7). [b60274dadf3c] [ad76051fec65] [e02bc47498e3]
3. **Start from the buyer's expressed desire.** "Consider the person who stands before you with certain expressed desires." Answer the ad question in that person's terms, not in terms of your product facts. [ad76051fec65]
4. **Offer service; do not ask for the sale.** People you address are selfish, as everyone is. They care nothing for your interest or profit. "Buy my brand, give me the trade you give others, let me have the money" is an unpopular appeal. "The best ads ask no one to buy." They offer wanted information and cite advantages to users. The good salesman "pictures the customer side of his service until the natural result is to buy." "People can be coaxed but not driven. Whatever they do, they do to please themselves." Condition: Hopkins says these ads only seem altruistic; they are built on knowledge of human nature. Evidence: the hairbrush door-to-door case (section 7). [5558b5dedefc] [39699efcd305]
5. **The 2 greatest faults are boasts and selfishness.** "Argue anything for your own advantage and people will resist to the limit. But seem unselfishly to consider your customer's desires and they will naturally flock to you." "Do not boast... Boasting is repulsive." Crying a brand name "may sometimes pay to an extent but it can never pay like appeals which appear unselfish." [39699efcd305] [250834566833]
6. **A single selfish phrase can kill an ad.** Hopkins names "insist on buying this brand", "avoid imitations", "look out for substitution". "Forget yourself entirely. Seek in every word to increase your good impression. Say only what you think a good salesman should say if that prospect stood before him." [250834566833] [ad76051fec65]
7. **You cannot enter a crowded category on "buy my brand".** In a well-occupied field, "one must offer exceptional service to induce people to change from favorite brands to yours." Most advertisers do not offer that, so giving service where others fail gives a large advantage. Related lines: "Serve better than others. Offer more help than others and you are pretty sure to win." "Think only from your customer's perspective." [39699efcd305]
8. **Take the risk off the buyer.** "Ask a person to take a chance on you and you have a fight. Offer to take a chance on him and the way is easy." Hopkins gives no mechanism (guarantee type, trial length). Cards link this idea to trials and money-back guarantees (card adds). No card quotes this line directly. [30ea811c01b3] [04185b470730]
9. **Tell the pains you take, even if every maker does the same.** Makers are too close to their product and see only the ordinary. Facts "too common to be told" can give the first brand that tells them "an exclusive and lasting prestige". "Tell the pains that you take to excel." Condition: the facts must be true (card caveat). Evidence: Schlitz (section 7). Hopkins says this works in "many, many lines". [48f2e992c7ce]
10. **The maker should not write his own ads.** "No manufacturer should ever conduct his own advertising. He's too close to his factory." Makers tell what they take pride in, like plant size or the age of the business, instead of what the customer wants to know. "The advertising man must study the consumer." Conflict: the host later argues founders should "go direct" and front the selling (principle 17). See section 9. [ea531107827e] [48f2e992c7ce]
11. **Tell the truth, in a rugged and fascinating way.** Hopkins credits this to John Powers (paid $12,000 a year by Wanamaker). Truth was rare enough in ads that plain truth "created a sensation". Evidence: the bankrupt clothing store ad (section 7). No card covers John Powers or this rule.
12. **Lead with reward, not prevention.** "People will do anything to cure a trouble, but little to prevent it." "Prevention offers slight appeal to humanity in general." People want to be told of rewards: how to be happier, more cheerful, more beautiful. "Every advertising campaign depends on its psychology. Success or failure is determined by the right or wrong appeal." Hopkins says he never referred to disasters and never pictured the afflicted; every illustration showed attractive people and beautiful teeth. Condition: card caveats say some categories do sell on risk (insurance, security) and health or beauty claims may need legal review. Evidence: Pepsodent (section 7). [6111749e4303] [ea531107827e]
13. **A demonstration beats any argument.** "No argument in the world can ever compare with one dramatic demonstration." "A good product is its own best salesman." "It is uphill work to sell goods without samples." Evidence: silver polish, 1 in 10 by talk versus nearly all by demo (section 7). Host adds Estee Lauder: she moved money from the ad budget into making free samples, counting on reciprocity and word of mouth. [30ea811c01b3] [04185b470730]
14. **Give samples only to people who ask, and make them do something.** "I have never found that it paid to give samples to people who do not request it." "We must arouse interest in our product before it has value to anybody." "Promiscuous sampling" is "a very bad plan"; "products handed out without asking lose respect". "It is different when you force people to make an effort." Host example: an ad offers a free month or a vacuum cleaner trial if the reader fills something in, versus mailing vacuum cleaners to people who never asked. Conflict: the host's Estee Lauder story (stopping women on the street with free product) is unrequested sampling. See section 9. [04185b470730]
15. **Do not judge the market by your own taste.** "We must never judge humanity by ourselves. The things we want, the things we like may appeal to a small minority." The losses from advertising on personal preference "would easily pay the national debt". "We must submit all things in advertising to the court of public opinion." Hopkins compares it to crossing an ocean in his yacht without chart or compass. Evidence: products he thought would never sell that sold "like crazy". [9e96311d1944]
16. **Stay close to the buyer.** Keep no distance between you and the end buyer: up close you "can see what they reject" and "what appeal lights up their eyes". "Study salesmen, canvassers and fakers if you want to know how to sell goods." Hopkins listened for hours under torchlight to street pitchmen. He also hired street-smart, barely schooled people who understood people; one wrote copy "which would induce a farmer to mortgage his barn to respond" but needed every sentence edited for grammar. Card caveat: study the mechanics, not deceptive tactics. [eafac9149579]
17. **Give the brand a human personality.** "Personalities appeal while soulless corporations do not. Make a man famous and you make his creation famous. All of us love to study men and their accomplishments." Hopkins built a persona, "Professor Anderson", with a real person behind it, and did this "wherever possible". Host adds: the founder knows the product best and should do the selling (citing a comms adviser the transcript calls "Lulu Berve" and James Dyson's mentor). Card caveat: prefer a real named person; invented authority can break platform policy and trust. [762657ec71d8]
18. **Keep the ad that works.** "It is seldom wise to change. The best way to win new customers is usually the way that won millions." Ads grow monotonous to "the men who read every ad", who then want something new; customers have not read every ad. Host adds Ogilvy: "You're not advertising to a standing army. You're advertising to a moving parade." Condition: card caveats say modern platforms do show real creative fatigue and a small audience can tire of repeats. [a122da70017c] [ab5eef4de74a]
19. **The more you tell, the more you sell.** Hopkins says the motto "has never failed to prove out in any test we know". Do not cap a salesman to a word count when a prospect stands before him. "Give them enough to get action." Condition: applies to a reader who has already shown interest through the headline. Evidence: some of his best ads were about 15,000 words long, as an interesting story about a product the reader chose to read about. Card caveats: not padding; short formats and character limits still constrain; cold traffic is a case the source does not address. [84d9f8424c4b] [31dfb7e722f5] [e02bc47498e3]
20. **The headline's job is to pick out the people you could interest.** Like calling "Hey there, Bill Jones" in a crowd. Headlines work like news headlines: nobody reads a whole paper; readers pick by headline. Headlines "either conceal or reveal an interest". People are hurried and want to be amused or benefited. A product may interest someone more than anything else, but "they will never know it unless the headline tells them". Headlines must not mislead. "Address the people you seek and them only." [e2b526314c8d] [1c9885b21bef]
21. **Put the main appeal in the headline.** The best promise, meaning the real benefit the customer gets, goes in the headline. Host's example: the iPod line (he says "thousand songs in your pocket" once and "10,000 songs" once) `[web check: exact iPod tagline]`. [e2b526314c8d]
22. **Spend most of your copy time on the headline.** Hopkins spent far more time on headlines than on body copy, often hours on 1 headline, discarding scores before choosing, because "the entire return from an ad depends on attracting the right sort of readers". "The best of salesmanship has no chance whatever unless we get a hearing." Host adds Ogilvy's line "you can't save souls in an empty church", and his own view that you must entertain before you educate (card caveat: this is the host's interpretation, not Hopkins). [e2b526314c8d] [1c9885b21bef]
23. **The same offer framed differently can multiply returns.** Headline changes alone can multiply returns 5 to 10 times (Hopkins); Ogilvy later saw 19 to 1 (host's recollection). Other framing levers the source names: restricting the offer to a group, showing a price, and using specific numbers. Condition: historical observations, not expected lifts for any account. [55e663877849] [ab5eef4de74a] [2aab472f73cf]
24. **Human nature is fixed, so psychology rules are reusable.** "Human nature is perpetual... the same today as in the time of Caesar." "You will never need to unlearn" what you learn. Named levers: curiosity ("one of the strongest human incentives. We employ it whenever we can"), price perception, belonging, self-interest. Card caveat: this is Hopkins's assertion, not proof that any tactic works on today's platforms. [9dedb90ad732]
25. **People judge things largely by price.** Evidence: the $750,000 painting (section 7). Card adds: signal premium price openly where positioning supports it. [2aab472f73cf] [9dedb90ad732]
26. **Offers limited to a group beat general offers.** "An offer limited to a certain class of people is far more effective than a general offer", for example veterans, lodge members or executives. "Those who are entitled to any seemingly advantage by being part of a group will go a long way not to lose that advantage." Card caveat: do not fake a group the buyer cannot plausibly belong to. [2aab472f73cf] [9dedb90ad732]
27. **Be specific; use real numbers.** "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." "Best in the world" and "lowest prices" are "claiming the expected". "Actual figures are not generally discounted. Specific facts... have their full weight and effect." "The weight of an argument may often be multiplied by making it specific." Evidence: "My net profit is 3%" and the "78-second shave" (section 7). Condition: the figure must be true and provable. [203ef216f7b2] [31dfb7e722f5]
28. **Research until you find the keystone fact.** "An ad writer to have a chance of success must gain full information on his subject." A careful writer "will often read for weeks"; in many volumes he may find few usable facts, "but some one fact may be the keystone of his success". "Genius is the art of taking pains." A campaign "is usually preceded by a very large volume of data"; the ad "seems so simple and it must be simple to appeal", but behind it lie reams of data and months of research. Evidence: toothpaste and Rolls-Royce (section 7). [a5c303a75054]
29. **Learn by volume and small mistakes.** Hopkins credits "exceptional hours", not ability: working 2 or 3 times as much gives 2 or 3 times the mistakes and successes to learn from. He made many mistakes "in a small way", learned from each, "made no mistake twice", and from time to time found a principle that lasted. He says "I am not advising others to follow my example" on hours. [4716ea6e1fd5] [9e96311d1944]
30. **Stay close to the money.** Big pay goes to people who bring in orders or cut factory costs, because they raise profit, and "if you can show profits then you can command a reasonable share". Hopkins moved from bookkeeping (the expense side) to sales. Career and deal principle, not a copy rule. [eafac9149579] [43edc500fa16]
31. **Success in 1 field does not transfer to another.** "It is curious how we all desire to excel in something outside of our own province. This leads many men astray. Men make money in one business and lose it in others." Business principle, not a copy rule. [5c7c7f95fe62]
32. **Keep the operation simple.** Host's maxim "genius has the fewest moving parts": Albert Lasker ran the most profitable agency on the best copywriters alone, without art departments or large staff; Hopkins earned his income "at a typewriter, which I operated myself without a clerk or a secretary". No card covers this.
33. **Treat the work as a game you love.** Hopkins repeats that he loves work "as other men love golf" and that "we do best what we like best". Host links it to Claude Shannon, Charlie Munger ("intense interest in any subject is indispensable") and Les Schwab ("do it with gusto... in volume... repeat, repeat, repeat"). Motivation, not a copy rule. [4716ea6e1fd5]

## 3. Procedures

The source gives almost no step-by-step procedures. The steps below are assembled from Hopkins's stated tests. Steps marked "card adds" come from card translations, not the transcript. No platform UI is involved, so no `[verify UI]` steps apply except platform text limits.

### 3.1 Salesman test for any ad, PDP, landing page or email
1. Picture 1 typical buyer who already showed interest (clicked the headline or image), standing in front of you. [e02bc47498e3]
2. Read each line and ask: "Would this help a salesman sell the goods if he met this buyer in person?" and "Would I say this to that buyer face to face?" [250834566833] [e02bc47498e3]
3. Cut or rewrite lines that exist only to be clever, to be short, or to speak to a crowd. [250834566833]
4. Cut boasts and brand commands (see 3.2). [250834566833]
5. Check the copy gives enough specific information for the buyer to act, and ends in a service-framed close, not "give me your money". [e02bc47498e3]
6. Final check: does every sentence raise good impression and speak to the buyer's desire, not the brand's pride? [250834566833]

### 3.2 Boast and selfishness audit
1. Mark every self-referential line: company history, plant size, age of business, awards, "we're the best", "buy our brand". [39699efcd305]
2. Mark warning phrases: "insist on", "avoid imitations", "look out for substitutes". [250834566833]
3. For each mark, either delete it or rewrite it as the buyer benefit that follows from it (card example: plant size becomes faster shipping or lower risk, card adds). [39699efcd305]
4. Card adds: re-audit live prospecting ads monthly. [39699efcd305]

### 3.3 "Tell the pains you take" research and write-up
1. Go to where the product is made. Walk the process. Ask why each step exists. Hopkins toured the Schlitz brewery before writing. [48f2e992c7ce] `[needs brand data: production, sourcing, QC and fulfilment steps, with numbers]`
2. Record each step with its purpose and its numbers (how often, how many times, how deep, how long). [48f2e992c7ce]
3. Ignore the owner's reply that "everyone does this". Hopkins: "Others have never told this story." [48f2e992c7ce]
4. Write the steps into the ad or PDP as concrete pictures that give a vague claim ("pure", "premium") a visible meaning. [48f2e992c7ce]
5. Only use steps that are true and can be checked. [48f2e992c7ce]

### 3.4 Keystone fact research
1. Gather full information before writing. Read widely, including dry technical material never meant for marketers. [a5c303a75054]
2. Expect to read a lot and find little. Look for the 1 fact that can carry the campaign. [a5c303a75054]
3. Card adds: search supplier specs, factory documents, warranties, ingredient sheets, support tickets and review language. [a5c303a75054] `[needs brand data: supplier and product documents, reviews, tickets]`
4. Keep the finished ad simple even though the research behind it is large. [a5c303a75054]

### 3.5 Headline writing
1. Decide who the headline must pick out. Address those people "and them only". [e2b526314c8d] [1c9885b21bef]
2. Put the main promise (the real benefit) in the headline. [e2b526314c8d]
3. Make it amuse or benefit a hurried reader right away, and reveal the interest instead of hiding it. [1c9885b21bef]
4. Check it does not mislead. [1c9885b21bef]
5. Write many. Spend hours if needed. Discard scores before choosing. [e2b526314c8d]
6. Prefer the headline that brings the right readers, even if it brings fewer. [e2b526314c8d] [55e663877849]

### 3.6 Appeal choice (reward versus prevention)
1. State the appeal in 1 sentence before writing. [ea531107827e]
2. Card adds: write both a prevention angle and a reward angle for the same product. [6111749e4303]
3. Pick the reward angle as the default per Hopkins; show attractive people and the good result; do not picture the afflicted. [6111749e4303]
4. Keep all variants on 1 dominant appeal before testing (card adds). [6111749e4303]
5. `[needs brand data: category rules and past results on problem-led versus outcome-led ads]`

### 3.7 Sampling and trial offers
1. Build interest first with the ad or page. [04185b470730]
2. Offer the sample or trial only as a response the buyer asks for. [04185b470730]
3. Require a small effort (host: fill something in; card adds: form, email, quiz). [04185b470730]
4. Card adds: track claim rate and later purchase or repeat rate, since gating adds friction. [04185b470730] `[needs brand data: sample claim rate and sample-to-purchase rate]`

### 3.8 Intent-matched follow-up (sewing machine method)
1. Find what the person asked about. [b60274dadf3c]
2. Send the complete answer for that one thing (Hopkins: the sewing machine catalog with every style and price), not the full catalog. [b60274dadf3c]
3. Add proof from people near them (Hopkins: names of buyers in her town, and an invitation to see the machines and talk to owners). Card adds: local reviews, UGC or an in-person demo. [b60274dadf3c]
4. Card caveat: segment only where traffic per segment is large enough. [b60274dadf3c] `[needs brand data: traffic and orders per segment]`

### 3.9 Refresh decision for a working ad
1. Ask whether the urge to change comes from the team being bored or from the market. [a122da70017c]
2. Card adds: change only when measured results fall, not on an internal schedule. [a122da70017c] `[needs brand data: ad-level results over time, frequency, reach]`
3. Card adds: if you change, keep the winning appeal, claim and offer, and swap only the execution (image, format, channel). [a122da70017c]

### 3.10 Framing test with a fixed offer (card adds, built on principle 23)
1. Keep product, price and discount the same. [ab5eef4de74a]
2. Change 1 framing element: headline, group addressed, price shown, or specificity. [ab5eef4de74a]
3. Keep audience, placement and creative the same where possible. [55e663877849]
4. Measure purchase conversion for a sales goal, or qualified clicks if the headline's job is selection. [55e663877849] [ab5eef4de74a]
5. Small stores may need longer runs or bigger differences. The source gives no sample size or test length. [55e663877849]

### 3.11 Winning a role or large client (Hopkins's job pitch)
1. Apply, call and try to meet, as the baseline. [15cf1ae66178]
2. Have many third parties send letters vouching for your skill (Hopkins had "dozens"). [15cf1ae66178]
3. Publish your thinking where the decision maker will see it (Hopkins wrote a daily newspaper column on advertising). [15cf1ae66178]
4. Make a concrete ask with a clear deliverable and reward, as Lasker did (3 ads to start the campaign; any car on the street for Hopkins's wife). [15cf1ae66178]

## 7. Examples and case results

All examples are historical anecdotes told by Hopkins or the host. None is a benchmark or a promise.

- **Silver polish (example).** Hopkins, about 10, sold his widowed mother's homemade silver polish door to door. Talking sold about 1 woman in 10. Getting inside to demonstrate sold nearly all. [30ea811c01b3] [04185b470730]
- **Estee Lauder samples (example, host's addition).** She shifted ad money into producing free samples to give away, relying on the product, reciprocity and word of mouth.
- **Sewing machine inquiry (example).** The firm used to send every inquirer the full catalog. Hopkins changed it: send the sewing machine catalog with every style and price, the names of buyers in her town, and an invitation to see the machines and talk to owners. [b60274dadf3c]
- **Schlitz beer (example).** 5th in market share. Hopkins toured the brewery: plate glass rooms with filtered air for cooling, white wood pulp filters, pumps and pipes cleaned 2 times daily, bottles cleaned 4 times by machine, wells 4,000 ft deep (Hopkins's "artesian" wells; the card spells "artisan"), 6 months of aging, a mother yeast from 1,200 experiments. Owners said every brewer does this. Hopkins wrote it up, "gave purity a meaning", and Schlitz went from 5th to neck and neck with 1st "in a few months". [48f2e992c7ce]
- **Pepsodent (example).** Advertised as a creator of beauty instead of a decay preventer. Illustrations showed attractive people and beautiful teeth. Hopkins says he made $1 million. He bought shares for $13,000 at the start; they paid $200,000 in dividends and later sold for $500,000. [6111749e4303] [43edc500fa16]
- **Hairbrush maker (example).** 2,000 door-to-door sellers said "I was sent here to give you a brush. I have samples here and I want you to take your choice." Choosing 1, the housewife saw several she wanted and wanted to return the gift, so the seller got an order. [5558b5dedefc]
- **Bankrupt clothing store (example).** John Powers insisted on the truth or he would quit. Ad: "We are bankrupt. We owe $125,000 more than we can pay... if you come and buy tomorrow, we shall have the money to meet them... These are the prices we are quoting." Thousands came; the store was saved. No card.
- **Clothing seller (example).** "My net profit is 3%" instead of "lowest prices in America". Hopkins: "proved very impressive". [203ef216f7b2]
- **Razor maker (example).** "78-second shave" instead of "quick shaves"; indicated real test results; "a sensational advance in his sales". [203ef216f7b2]
- **Painting (example).** Most passed it by. Once told it cost $750,000, people came back and crowded round. [2aab472f73cf]
- **Group offers (example).** Offers limited to war veterans, lodge members or executives beat general offers. No results given. [2aab472f73cf]
- **Toothpaste research (example).** Hopkins read many volumes of "dry as dust" science and found in 1 the idea that "helped make millions". [a5c303a75054]
- **Rolls-Royce (example, host via Ogilvy).** A line from an obscure engineering document became the headline "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." `[web check: exact wording]` [a5c303a75054]
- **iPod (example, host).** "Thousand songs in your pocket" puts the whole value in a few words. [e2b526314c8d]
- **Professor Anderson (example).** A brand persona with a real person behind it. No results given. [762657ec71d8]
- **Hopkins's job win (example).** Applied, called, had dozens of people write in for him, and wrote a daily newspaper column on advertising; got the job at a firm with one of the largest US ad budgets. [15cf1ae66178]
- **Lasker's recruiting pitch (example).** Showed the copy he had bought in New York and Philadelphia for thousands of dollars, said neither of them would submit it, and offered any car on Michigan Avenue for Hopkins's wife in return for 3 ads to start the campaign. [15cf1ae66178]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Asking people to buy instead of offering service ("buy my brand"). [5558b5dedefc] [39699efcd305]
2. Boasting about the company, plant size or age of business. [39699efcd305] [ea531107827e]
3. Selfish phrases such as "insist on this brand", "avoid imitations", "look out for substitution"; 1 such phrase can kill an ad. [250834566833]
4. Treating people "in the mass", for example sending every inquirer the same catalog. [b60274dadf3c]
5. The maker writing his own ads and telling what he is proud of. [ea531107827e]
6. Keeping quiet about quality steps because "everyone does it". [48f2e992c7ce]
7. Selling prevention and picturing disasters or the afflicted. [6111749e4303]
8. Arguing instead of demonstrating; selling without samples. [30ea811c01b3]
9. Giving samples to people who did not ask ("promiscuous sampling"). [04185b470730]
10. Judging demand by your own taste. [9e96311d1944]
11. Changing a working ad because the team is bored of it. [a122da70017c]
12. Capping copy at a word count for an interested reader. [e02bc47498e3] [31dfb7e722f5]
13. Weak, vague or misleading headlines, or not spending enough time on them. [e2b526314c8d] [1c9885b21bef]
14. Platitudes and general claims ("best in the world", "lowest prices"). [203ef216f7b2]
15. Writing before gathering full information; sparing "the midnight oil". [a5c303a75054]
16. Thinking success in 1 business qualifies you for another. [5c7c7f95fe62]
17. Host's own example: big tech billboards that talk only about "me, me, buy my product".
18. Card-level warnings: fake specifics, fake groups, invented authority personas, exaggerated demos (they cause returns and bad reviews). [203ef216f7b2] [2aab472f73cf] [762657ec71d8] [30ea811c01b3]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Header mismatch.** The header (topic line, "Look for", "Scope mix", "Volume ~400 words") describes an Alex Hormozi CRO talk for folder 07-LPG. The body is a Founders podcast on Claude Hopkins, and the title says ad copy. The header's claims (above the fold seen by 100%, 60% never scroll, 1 test per week, A/B calculator, "so that" principle, Value Equation page mapping, "Get funded fast", "Ships in 24 hours") have no support in the text and no cards. Pass 2 must not cite this source for them. The library metadata for this source may need a fix (not done here).
- **Internal conflict: sampling.** Hopkins says never give samples to people who did not ask. The host's Estee Lauder story praises handing free product to women on the street. Hopkins's hairbrush case also gives a "gift" at the door, but the buyer chooses it. Common ecommerce practice (influencer seeding, PR boxes, free samples in orders) also sends unrequested product. Treat Hopkins's rule as his view; test it. [04185b470730]
- **Internal conflict: who should write the ads.** Hopkins says no maker should run his own advertising. The host says the founder should "go direct" and do the selling, and Hopkins himself used a persona. One reading: the founder can be the face, but the copy must be written from the buyer's side. The source does not reconcile this. [ea531107827e] [762657ec71d8]
- **Internal tension: keep versus change.** "It is seldom wise to change" sits next to "an identical offer made in different ways may bring multiplied returns". The source suggests testing framings, then sticking with the winner, but never says so as a procedure. [a122da70017c] [ab5eef4de74a]
- **Conflict with common ecommerce advice: long copy.** Hopkins says "the more you tell, the more you sell" with no word cap. Common paid social practice favours short primary text and short video. Cards limit the rule to readers who already showed interest and to formats that allow depth. [84d9f8424c4b] [31dfb7e722f5]
- **Conflict: creative refresh.** Hopkins (and Ogilvy's "moving parade") say keep a working ad. Common Meta and TikTok advice is to refresh creative often because of fatigue. Cards say decide with your own frequency and results data. [a122da70017c] `[needs brand data: fatigue curves by ad]`
- **Conflict: prevention and pain.** Hopkins says never picture the afflicted. Common DTC formats (problem, agitate, solve; before and after; "painkiller not vitamin") lead with the problem. Note the host's own label "sell painkillers, not vitamins" is his gloss; Hopkins's actual rule is reward over prevention, and a "cure a trouble" appeal still fits it. Cards say risk-led appeals can work for insurance and security. [6111749e4303]
