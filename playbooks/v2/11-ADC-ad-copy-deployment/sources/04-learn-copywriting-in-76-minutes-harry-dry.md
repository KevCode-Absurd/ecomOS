## 1. What this source is for

It gives a copywriter's screen and process for single lines and static ads: 3 questions to test every line, ways to turn adjectives into pictures and facts, a 3-part brief (who, what, how), and a worked example of 1 static ad built over about 2 days and 25 rewrites. It also covers simple writing, paragraph and structure rules, a newsletter format, quality standards and how to use AI. Use it when writing or reviewing hooks, headlines, static ad copy, PDP hero lines and email copy. It says nothing about ad platforms, testing, budgets or measured results.

## 2. Principles

1. **Test every line with 3 questions: Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Can nobody else say this?** Harry applies them "to every sentence". 3 no answers: "you've probably written a lot of rubbish". 3 yes answers: "you're on to something". Evidence: example ads, not data. Condition: a screen for memorability, credibility and ownability, not for demand. [eb5614dd91ba]
   - Fails all 3: a recruitment ad, "Don't just get a job. Change an entire industry." You cannot see it, cannot prove it true or false, and any recruiter could run it tomorrow. [eb5614dd91ba]
   - Passes all 3: New Balance, "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio." You can picture both people, it is true, and Reebok or Prada could not say it. [eb5614dd91ba]
2. **Rule 1, visualize: if you can't see it, you won't remember it.** Concrete words are things "you can drop on your foot". Abstract words ("a better way") fade. Memory game in the source: of "seamless transition, charging pitbull, muesli Irishman, better way, leg of lamb", people remember the 3 they can picture. Harry quotes Lisa Cron: "If I can't see it, it's not there yet." [eb5614dd91ba] [8e6c32baa35f]
3. **Rule 2, falsify: write sentences that can be proved true or false.** Harry's reason: a true-or-false line puts the writer's "head on the chopping block", so readers sit up. His example: Galileo was punished for saying the earth goes round the sun; "a harmonious connection with a celestial object" would have been ignored. [eb5614dd91ba] [d6f95f2facce]
4. **"You can't talk. You can only point."** (a tip Harry credits to a deleted Reddit account). Replace adjectives with things you can point at: a chart, an object, a person, a number. It "gets you off the adjective trail". Condition: it takes research; "This takes work. That's why it's better." [d6f95f2facce]
5. **Rule 3, distinct: never write an ad a competitor can sign** (Harry credits "Jim Derky" [web check: likely Jim Durfee]). It forces you to look deeper at what you sell. [954f8676cb20]
6. **Copy is the difference between near-identical products.** Strip the wrapper, copy and branding and many products "do kind of the same thing". Examples: Snickers vs Fuse (same look and taste, Fuse discontinued in 2006), Apple vs Samsung ("17 out of 20 American teenagers choose Apple"), Ogilvy's "we don't choose the whiskey, we choose the image". Anecdotes, not controlled evidence [web check: Fuse, Apple teen share]. [f57ce71a7241]
7. **Ask "what can I do that no one else is doing that people care about?"** Harry's example: footballer Dave Kitson had a modest career but wrote an anonymous insider book ("The Secret Footballer") that Harry says outsold Beckham, Lampard, Gerrard and Owen combined [web check: author and sales claim]. The product was "objectively so much worse", but the angle and the anonymous byline sold it. [f57ce71a7241]
8. **You should get it in 2 seconds ("One Mississippi, Two Mississippi").** Show the ad, count 2. If the reader does not get it, it is too slow. "Sometimes you know more in 2 seconds than you know in 2 hours." Heuristic only. [eb5614dd91ba] [220e0eb8c1d5]
9. **Not everyone has to get it.** Harry: "if everyone gets it, you're possibly doing something wrong." The host did not get the Athletic Greens ad ("You're going to need a smaller cabinet") because he does not take vitamins, and Harry says that is fine: it got his attention and he talked about it. This is opinion and it pulls against principle 8. [eb5614dd91ba] [c78fe9d7b47e]
10. **Position by what you replace.** Athletic Greens' cabinet line says "this does everything in one go" and attacks the morning-routine pain of taking vitamin A, D, C and zinc separately. A weaker version would be "one drink to replace them all". [c78fe9d7b47e]
11. **Make the buyer feel smart.** "The sport sedan for people who inherited brains instead of wealth" sells a plain car by making the buyer feel clever with money. Harry paraphrases someone: if you can make the customer feel they are making a smart decision, you're doing something right. [c78fe9d7b47e]
12. **Metonymy: swap the literal word for a picture word.** "1,000 songs in your pocket" really means "1,000 songs in your media player". "Pocket" is more visual, surprising and punchy, even though the line is not literally true. It was set against PC ads full of specs ("4 GB"). [fc16ebab7e1b]
13. **Judge an ad by how much it sells.** Harry contrasts the iPod ("everyone had an iPod") with the Vision Pro ("no one bought one") and says that is why copy matters. Opinion. [fc16ebab7e1b]
14. **Contrast and juxtaposition make lines stick.** "Supermodels in London and dads in Ohio" is funny because of the pairing. De Beers' "How can you make two months' salary last forever?" "shouldn't work" because 2 months and forever clash, and that is why it works. [988a4dabce09]
15. **Copywriting is arguing. The conclusion is to buy your product.** The ad body is the reasoning. [bf82611ee658]
16. **Small, believable claims beat big ones.** Harry's course ad promises "increase conversions from 1% to 2%", not "be a millionaire". The host calls it "sincere" and "believable". Condition: this is a positioning choice; the source gives no test. [bf82611ee658]
17. **Reframe a cost as a saving, and grow a small number by stretching the time frame.** "You'll spend 22,000 hours of your career writing. Spend 2 learning how to do it well." 4 hours a day sounds small; a career total sounds big. See section 9 on the arithmetic. [bf82611ee658]
18. **Copy moves the reader from a current attitude to a desired attitude.** Harry quotes "John Henley" (transcript spelling): the current attitude is the starting line, the desired attitude is the finish line; "you can't start a race in the middle". Picture 2 telephone poles; the copy is the wire. [6645e37e12f9]
19. **Copywriting has 3 pieces: who you're talking to, something to say, saying it well.** Most people think copywriting is only piece 3. [6645e37e12f9]
20. **Piece 2 needs a belief.** "Build things for more than the money": a vision, a belief, or something you think is wrong with the world. Examples: Zuora investors bought the "everything will be subscription" idea; Marketing Examples started because Harry was fed up with theory and wanted examples; the host started his podcast because no podcast covered how books get written. Condition: without a real point of view, the rest produces polished filler. [6645e37e12f9]
21. **Form follows function and the moment.** A tourist walking past a cafe needs "coffee and pastry = £5", not a big menu of latte prices and 17 kinds of croissant. [6645e37e12f9]
22. **Copy and design are one thing.** Harry writes in the final surface (Figma for ads, the email tool for newsletters) and puts billboards among other billboards. [e93a8cf681bd] [220e0eb8c1d5]
23. **Remove elements until 1 carries the ad.** Neil French, as Harry quotes him: most people think an ad needs header, subheader, picture and logo; "if you can do an ad with just one of those elements, you're on to a winner." Craft opinion. [7272fafc80c7]
24. **Line 1's job is to get line 2 read.** Harry says he got this from "Dave Gart", who got it from Joe Sugarman. Example: "It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad / the same time it takes a Model S to go from 0 to 60." [7272fafc80c7] [954f8676cb20]
25. **Explain a new product by comparing it to known ones.** The Cybertruck was confusing, so "better truck than an F-150, faster than a Porsche 911" (from an Elon Musk tweet) explains it through known products. [988a4dabce09] [8e6c32baa35f]
26. **Shorter is better in copy "most of the time".** The host's edit ("tougher than an F-150, faster than a 911") drops a word, makes "better" concrete, and creates parallel halves. Harry agreed. [8e6c32baa35f] [988a4dabce09]
27. **Facts guarantee you say something.** "Most people when they open their mouths say nothing": "wallpaper, word-shaped air, balloon smoke." A fact does not have to be a number. But not every fact is interesting ("350 million Americans" is not). Find the fact with a story inside it, then build on it. "If in doubt, give me a fact." The host is less keen on facts ("they're fine"), so this is Harry's view. [188f4fe29d32] [579d7f6fa475]
28. **Precision gives weight.** Vague commentary ("they're just not playing well") is weak; a fact ("they're playing Trippier at left back when he's a right back") is strong. The host's golf version: "averages 11 fairways, today 7". "Don't say inequality. Show me a graph." Keep an "arsenal" of facts about what you sell. [579d7f6fa475]
29. **Conflict drives memory, because people remember relatively.** Pickle juice makes orange juice taste sweeter. Forms: before/after, problem/solution, how they do it vs how we do it. Hinge it on an implicit "but" without writing "but" over and over. [a173f608eaa2] [4ed85e762d46]
30. **3 enemy types (A, B, C).** A = a different approach to the same problem. B = beliefs ("I believe this, you believe this"). C = competitors (Apple vs PC). Harry: "I don't take this too seriously." [e05c0ccdecf9]
31. **Simple writing comes from rewriting.** "You can't write simply. You can only rewrite simply." [189a7e6d8231]
32. **Kaplan's law of words: words that aren't working for you are working against you.** "Harry's law of words": you aren't taking Kaplan's law seriously enough. It applies to ideas too. [23276eda58ce] [867743b221ba]
33. **The strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope.** "And" on a landing page is "normally never a good thing" ("we sell jeans and t-shirts and socks and we have dressing rooms"). Hiut Denim's line is "We make jeans." Same for album covers: 1 image (Warhol banana, Dark Side of the Moon prism, Bowie's lightning bolt) beats "seven disparate images" (Bowie's "Reality"). 34. **Structure = dividing lines + parallelism.** Split a big idea into named parts. The host adds a move from a 1971 paper he calls "That's interesting": show that 2 things people think are different are 1, or that 1 thing is really 2. [fefe5e4ba91b]
35. **Your standards are your work.** Harry ships only "9 out of 10 plus". The host's mentor: set a high quality bar and never let it fall, even when people get angry or stop working with you. [f98be7ed26de]
36. **Taste, conviction and experience are what the writer brings; AI has none of them.** "What's writing? You see something, you believe something, you write about it." AI is "predictive": it lays out words "from all the stuff which has been laid out before". It is "a great research tool". ## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 3-question line test
1. Take the draft ad, hook, headline or PDP line. Review it line by line, not as a whole. [eb5614dd91ba]
2. Close your eyes. Can you picture it? If not, run 3.2.
3. Can it be proved true or false? If it is only opinion ("great quality"), run 3.3.
4. Could a competitor paste it on their page and still be telling the truth? If yes, run 3.4.
5. Count: 3 no = rewrite; 3 yes = keep going. Then run the 2-second test (3.7).

### 3.2 Abstract-to-concrete zoom-in
1. Take a sheet of paper. Draw a line down the left side. Write "abstract" at the top and "concrete" at the bottom. [8e6c32baa35f]
2. Write the vague word or phrase at the top.
3. Ask "what do I actually mean here? what am I actually saying?" Rewrite it one step more concrete each time, moving down the line.
4. Stop when you reach an object, person, place or action a reader can see.
5. Worked example: "regain fitness" → regain means "getting off the couch after 6 months" → fitness means "running" → how far: "5K" → "Couch to 5K". Harry says this is the most downloaded fitness programme of all time, made by a Boston TV producer, not Nike or Adidas [web check]. [8e6c32baa35f]
6. Worked example: "worn by pretty people in big cities and old people elsewhere" → "worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio".
7. Run 3.1 on the result.

### 3.3 "Only point" rewrite
1. Set the rule: you may only write things that are objectively true or false. No "good-looking", "smart", "premium". [d6f95f2facce]
2. For each adjective, ask: why is it true? Point to something.
3. Worked example (blind date): "good-looking" → "6 foot 2, looks like Ryan Gosling"; "intelligent" → "reads on the tube". The host cites a dating meme: "6'5, works in finance, trust fund".
4. Worked example (selling gold): not "it's a great investment" but point at the 50-year gold price chart, what gold did in the last crash, a family's gold stash, a castle, or how much gold Warren Buffett holds.
5. Research which pointed fact actually motivates the buyer. The source leaves this step open ("You have to research stuff"). [needs brand data: product specs, test results, customer reasons to buy]

### 3.4 Distinctiveness check
1. Ask: if a rival put this line on their site, would it still be true for them? If yes, it fails. [954f8676cb20]
2. Go back to research, not adjectives. Find a fact only your product has. [needs brand data: specs, history, founder story, sourcing, users only this brand has]
3. Models: Volvo speedometer ad ("Your car has 5 numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has 6. One could get the impression the people who made your car lack a little confidence."), where text and visual align. Corvette: "They don't write songs about Volvos" (6 words, lots of white space, an enemy, and a claim only Corvette can make). Hinge: "the dating app designed to be deleted".

### 3.5 Brief before writing (who, what, how)
1. Write the current attitude (point A) and the desired attitude (point B). [6645e37e12f9]
2. Piece 1, who you're talking to. Name the person and the moment: someone on the tube looking at their phone, someone who has used your app for 6 months and not upgraded, a tourist walking to the beach. [needs brand data: audience, channel, awareness state]
3. Piece 2, what you have to say. Write the belief or "why" behind the product. Ask "why did you start this business in the first place?" [needs brand data: founder belief, point of view]
4. Piece 3, say it well. Only now write the catchy line. Example brief for Hinge: "an app for people who want long-term relationships and are fed up with dating apps"; the writer turned it into "designed to be deleted" using alliteration (d, d, d) and juxtaposition (who makes an app that wants to be deleted?).

### 3.6 Write in the real surface
1. Write where the reader will read. Ads in Figma, newsletters inside the email tool (Harry uses ConvertKit), landing pages on the page. Harry says he "never" writes anywhere else, because a pasted line may wrap to 3 or 4 lines. [e93a8cf681bd] [220e0eb8c1d5] [verify UI]
2. Drop in placeholder images early and write around their shape. [e93a8cf681bd]
3. For a billboard, place the ad in a scene with about 7 other ads and people walking past (Harry thinks this was Ogilvy's advice). Ask: does it grab attention against that noise? [220e0eb8c1d5]

### 3.7 2-second test
1. Show the ad to someone. Count "One Mississippi, two Mississippi". [eb5614dd91ba] [220e0eb8c1d5]
2. If they do not get it, it is too slow for a glance context. For a street sign, even 2 seconds is too long: "It's instant."
3. Decide whether the audience is meant to be the in-crowd (principle 9) before rejecting a line only the in-crowd gets.

### 3.10 Rewrite for simplicity
1. Write the line or paragraph. Copy and paste it and rewrite it. Repeat until you have 4 or 5 versions. [189a7e6d8231]
2. Allow many versions to be bad; the freedom to be wrong helps you find your own way.
3. Show reviewers several versions, not 1. With 1 version Harry's brother (his editor) said only "I like it" or "I don't"; with 3 he could say "that one's best, use that sentence but put it in that one". [189a7e6d8231] [6914df3c2bd8]
4. Cut with Kaplan's law. Burrito test for paragraphs: you should be able to throw it and it should not come apart in the air. Pull 1 sentence out; if the paragraph still works, the sentence should not have been there. [23276eda58ce]
5. For more depth Harry names Zinsser ("On Writing Well") and "Elements of Eloquence" (Forsyth). [189a7e6d8231]
6. Harry is unsure he would recommend the multi-version habit: "it's caused me a lot of turmoil over the years".

### 3.11 Structure a piece
1. Divide the big topic into named buckets. Copywriting → who, what, how. [fefe5e4ba91b]
2. Look for "1 thing that is really 2". Example: an essay on getting 10,000 readers opened "the first step in getting 10,000 people to read your work is to get 100 people to read your work". Part 1: things that don't scale, 100 readers. Part 2: momentum, the next 9,900.
3. Make the parts parallel. Think chapters: a course of 17 loose videos needs something "cohesive" to hold on to.

### 3.12 Newsletter issue (Harry's format)
1. Treat it as "less about the news, more about the letter" (quote Harry attributes to "Hanley"). [4ed85e762d46]
2. Open with time, place and what is going on. Model: the opening of Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets (an argument at breakfast at number 4 Privet Drive). Look out of the window. Harry's example: "Live from London. Weather update: trees shuddering, majestic hail."
3. Body: 5 or 6 items ("five examples"). Dense.
4. Put conflict in almost every item. Example: on Loom, first show how every other screen recorder positions itself ("easier to use screen recorder"), then Loom's move: "the world's gone remote, but remote communication sucks", so "async video messaging", a new category. Harry says Loom is now worth more than the whole screen recorder bucket combined (his characterization) [web check]. [a173f608eaa2]
5. Keep every paragraph to 2 lines or fewer (3.13).
6. Close with a conclusion.
7. Intro example: "Hey, it's Harry. It's 3:47 a.m. I'm in the big smoke alone. Laptop, green tea, looking at the whiteboard like a dozy dog. Six words on it. Five with lines through. Intro glares at me. I glare back..." Aim for it to feel "out of the oven... fresh bread, warm to touch".

### 3.13 Paragraph length
1. Cap paragraphs at 2 lines. Harry has not written a longer paragraph since March 2021. [23276eda58ce]
2. His 3 reasons: short paragraphs are "like monkey bars" (transcript: "monkey butts"), easy to swing between for readers on a train; a 3-line paragraph often means he is not explaining well, so he asks "do we need that word? that sentence?"; strong lines need room to breathe.
3. He says this is "idiosyncratic", not good or bad.

### 3.14 Create conflict in a story
1. Tell the plain version: "I got on the train, got a coffee, we started talking." Boring. [a173f608eaa2]
2. Find the "but": "I woke up at 8:52... my mum runs up the stairs: 'You cannot do this interview in those blue shorts'... I get here and you ask why I've got longer trousers on."
3. Write the turns naturally, not "but this happened, but this happened".

### 3.15 Judge "done"
1. Take a walk around the garden. Come back. [f98be7ed26de]
2. Ask: can I take anything away? Does 1 line flow to the next?
3. If you cannot add or remove a word and make it better (the New Balance line is the model), it is done. If it is below 9 out of 10, it does not go out.

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

- Writing abstract lines you cannot picture ("seamless transition", "better way"). [eb5614dd91ba]
- Adjective trails that cannot be proved ("good family, great values", "funny"). [d6f95f2facce]
- Lines any competitor could sign. [954f8676cb20]
- Skipping "who am I talking to" (Snapchat's Super Bowl spend). [6645e37e12f9]
- Telling a passer-by everything (prices, 17 croissants, "follow us on Instagram") when they need 1 thing. [6645e37e12f9]
- Thinking copywriting is only "saying it well" without something to say. [6645e37e12f9]
- Judging an ad alone on a blank canvas instead of among other ads. [220e0eb8c1d5]
- Drafting in a doc and pasting into the final surface, where lines wrap differently. [220e0eb8c1d5] [e93a8cf681bd]
- Feature and spec lists instead of the one thing that matters (PC ads vs iPod). [fc16ebab7e1b]
- Big claims ("be a millionaire") instead of believable ones. [bf82611ee658]
- Stopping at "fine": Harry kept going past a version he liked because he "wouldn't sign this ad off". [6e39a617d9e1] [f98be7ed26de]
- Judging each early spitball line; let the tap run. [6e39a617d9e1]
- Trusting your own judgement after 2 to 3 hours on one ad. [6914df3c2bd8]
- Showing a reviewer only 1 version. [189a7e6d8231]
- Using "and" to stretch scope. [867743b221ba]
- Paragraphs where a sentence can be removed and nothing breaks. [23276eda58ce]
- Mascots or gimmicks that clash with a serious promise. [6e8d1ddacee4]
- Writing "but, but, but" literally instead of building the turn naturally. [a173f608eaa2]
- Writing from secondhand phrases instead of observation ("yellow taxis"). [a359f59b90c4]
- Treating AI as the author; shipping its output without your taste. [80c71cd2a847]
- Starting the writing day on TikTok or Instagram. [7b1e7c794ef4]
- Vague commentary without facts ("Tiger didn't want it bad enough"). [579d7f6fa475]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Internal tensions**
- 2-second rule vs "if everyone gets it, you may be doing something wrong". Harry holds both. The source does not say when to pick which. [eb5614dd91ba] [c78fe9d7b47e]
- Harry loves facts; the host finds them "fine" and "sort of dry" until he gives his own flight and golf examples. [188f4fe29d32]
- Metonymy ("1,000 songs in your pocket") is literally untrue, which sits oddly beside "can I falsify it?". The source does not resolve this. [fc16ebab7e1b]
- Rewrite count: "about 20 rewrites" early, "2 days and 25 rewrites" later.

**Arithmetic error in the source**
- 4 hours x 300 days x 30 years = 36,000 hours, not "give or take 22,000". The card [bf82611ee658] repeats 22,000 without noting this. Do not reuse the method without redoing the sum in code.

**Card errors against the source text**
- [867743b221ba] ties "worn by supermodels in London, dads in Ohio" to Hiut Denim. The source ties it to New Balance; Hiut's line is "We make jeans."
- [e05c0ccdecf9] gives the Volkswagen winter ad as an enemy type C example. The source gives Apple vs PC for C; it uses the Volkswagen ad for layout and calls the 1%/2% pair "a bit of a before and after".
- [6e39a617d9e1] says "about several days". The source says about 2 days.
- [6645e37e12f9] says the 17-item menu was "for someone already walking in". The source only says the other cafe had a big menu with latte prices and "17 different croissants".
- [6e8d1ddacee4] says Harry removed a mascot "from a landing page he was working on". In the source he removed it from his own remake of the Hinge ad.
- [6914df3c2bd8] says rejected options were kept elsewhere in the layout. The source does not say this.
- [188f4fe29d32] attributes the flight example to the source generally; in the transcript it is the host's pet peeve.

**Historical and attribution claims to check** [web check: each before reuse]
- Galileo "sentenced to 10 years house arrest by the Romans" (history says otherwise); De Beers 85% and "Mary Gerity 1948"; Ogilvy +50%; Apple 17 of 20 teens; Couch to 5K "most downloaded"; Kitson and The Secret Footballer sales; Snapchat ad cost ($77m vs $7m in the same breath); Loom valuation claim; Asana spend; names "Jim Derky", "John Henley", "Hanley", "Dave Gart", the 1971 "That's interesting" paper.

**Missing**
- No conversion data, test results or measured lift for any technique. All are craft opinion and famous-ad examples.
- No guidance on testing variants in market; judgement comes from taste, a small reviewer circle and the 2-second test.
- No channel specifics (Meta, TikTok, Google), formats, character limits or policy rules.
- No ecommerce examples. Ecommerce use (PDPs, bundles, cost per use) appears only in the cards as translation, not from the source.
- "Say it well" tools are named (alliteration, juxtaposition, metonymy, parallelism) but not taught as a full system.

**Conflicts with common ecommerce advice**
- Direct-response ecommerce usually favours instant clarity for cold audiences. Harry accepts lines the out-crowd will not get.
- Common practice ships many ad variants fast and lets spend data pick winners. Harry spends about 2 days and 25 rewrites on 1 ad and judges by taste and a few trusted reviewers.
- The 1-element ad (no logo, no picture) conflicts with common static-ad advice to show product, offer and brand. Neil French's rule is from print, not feeds.
- Small, modest claims vs the common push for bold hooks. Harry chooses sincerity as a positioning choice, not from tests.
- The source's "shorter is better most of the time" and 2-line paragraphs line up with common mobile-first advice.
