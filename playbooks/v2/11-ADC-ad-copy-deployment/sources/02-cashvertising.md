## 1. What this source is for

It gives a copywriter a full kit for writing direct-response copy that makes people act: what people want (the Life-Force 8 and 9 learned wants), 17 persuasion principles, 41 copy, design and offer techniques, and 5 checklists (101 items). Use it to pick the core desire an ad appeals to, turn features into benefits, write and test headlines, raise believability with proof, guarantees and specifics, remove buying friction, add honest urgency, and QA an asset before launch. Much of the design, media and direct-mail advice is print-era and needs translating to ecommerce before use. The copy psychology (benefits, specificity, mental movies, proof, readability, offer testing) carries over most directly.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 What people want

1. **Advertising exists to get action, not applause.** Advertising is "selling in print" (Daniel Starch). An ad that only informs is "a salesperson who can't close". Judge it by orders or leads, not by "Wow, great ad!" or awards. Ogilvy: "Ninety-nine percent of advertising doesn't sell much of anything." [329ff6de64b5] [0e09cddd6797]
2. **People care about themselves, not about you.** Starch's readership reports showed "more than half" of magazine readers ignored ads that bragged about factories, staff, square footage, equipment and "unusual camera angles". H.E. Warren (1935) said sellers must know people's needs and wants. Condition: the author says this holds "for most products and services (there are exceptions)". [40638b840ce1]
3. **The Life-Force 8 (LF8) drive more sales than all other wants combined.** People are "biologically programmed" with 8 desires: (1) survival, enjoyment of life, life extension; (2) enjoyment of food and beverages; (3) freedom from fear, pain and danger; (4) sexual companionship; (5) comfortable living conditions; (6) to be superior, winning, keeping up with the Joneses; (7) care and protection of loved ones; (8) social approval. The claim that they drive more sales than all others combined has no citation; it is the author's view. Condition: pick an LF8 your product can really satisfy. [5cfd138f2ca5] `[needs brand data: which LF8 desire the brand's buyers name in reviews, returns and past winning ads]`
4. **The 9 learned (secondary) wants are real but weaker.** To be informed; curiosity; cleanliness of body and surroundings; efficiency; convenience; dependability or quality; expression of beauty and style; economy or profit; bargains. They are learned, not hardwired, so the author says they are "not as bankable" as the LF8. Use them as support. [5cfd138f2ca5]
5. **Desire is tension that seeks action.** Formula: Tension, then Desire, then Action to satisfy the desire. Examples: hunger (LF8 2); a stranger chatting online with your 8-year-old (LF8 7); an office chair that hurts your back after 10 minutes (LF8 5). An LF8 appeal creates a drive to act "as soon as possible".
6. **"The first use of any product is inside the consumer's mind."** People enjoy reading how others satisfied an LF8 (vicarious fulfilment). Specific, visual words make the reader demonstrate the product in their head, and imagining use raises desire. "The less imagery you convey, the less your message occupies consumers' brains, the less likely it is that you'll influence them." Evidence: author's reasoning and examples, no study. [8998cd84ed0c] [b74a2213dcd8]
7. **The 5-step chain of an effective ad (Chapter 1 summary).** (1) Know the LF8. (2) Base the appeal on them. (3) Write copy that makes prospects "visually demonstrate your product" in their heads until they want the result, then choose your product to get it. (4) Make them believe it works (social proof, guarantees). (5) Push them to act (inertia, deadlines, ease). [5cfd138f2ca5] [8e185cccc2ed]
8. **Emotion first, logic to justify.** Young and Rubicam: "People buy because of emotion and justify with logic." For costlier products you must also give enough reasoning so the buyer can justify the spend. [a9db8de6d903]

### 2.2 The 17 principles of consumer psychology

9. **Fear sells, but only with all 4 parts.** Fear causes stress, and stress causes the desire to act. Pratkanis and Aronson (Age of Propaganda) say fear works best when it (1) "scares the hell out of people", (2) offers a specific recommendation to overcome the threat, (3) the action is seen as effective, and (4) the reader believes they can do it. Remove any 1 part and it fails. Too much fear can paralyse ("deer in the headlights"). Conditions: use only if your product truly resolves the fear, or the appeal "will fail miserably"; target fears that are specific and widely recognised (sunscreen and melanoma is easy; detergent that prevents UV damage to clothes is hard); tap existing fears, do not create new ones. Fear only moves people to investigate; you still need proof and a push to act. [23a92b334a3e] [724e63d042f4] [23512cbd6f3e]
10. **Deadlines and scarcity are a fear-of-loss tool.** "Limited offer", "one-day sale", "while supplies last" scare people into acting before they miss a saving (secondary want 9, bargains). [7181b1222823] [bca698979925]
11. **Ego morphing: sell the identity.** "By purchasing the 'right stuff,' we enhance our own egos and rationalize away our inadequacies" (Pratkanis and Aronson). Create an image the buyer wants to merge with. True prospects already hold the values or want them; owners also use the product to express who they are. Luxury ads use little persuasive copy and lean on image. It works best on traits society rates highly: attractiveness, intelligence, economic success, sexual prowess. Stec and Bernstein's Balance Theory (1999): people with the trait buy to show it; people without it buy to look as if they have it. Condition: "Not all businesses can use such an ego-driven approach." [52d902a537ae] [e7d2a6958db3]
12. **Transfer: credibility by osmosis.** "If your prospects don't believe you, you've flushed your ad money down the toilet." Symbols of respected institutions (medical, science, national agencies, church, trade bodies) make buyers question your argument less. Best: an official endorsement. Next best: a recognised seal or logo. Mechanism: peripheral thinking. The prospect sees the symbol, then scrutinises less. Condition: works on peripheral thinkers; not enough alone for high-cost, carefully weighed buys (card). [f530f60c2969] [ac7fd5aceb1e]
13. **Bandwagon: link the product to a group.** Belonging is third in Maslow's hierarchy after physiological and safety needs. 3 group types: aspirational (want to join), associative (share values), dissociative (want to avoid). Rule for aspirational groups: prospects must be able to identify with the people shown. Associative influence can come from appealing to the group's values or from distancing from another group (youth culture vs "the oldies"). Axes: age, class, sex, geography, politics, education. Put "at least equal effort" into how buying makes them part of, keeps them in, or shows they are not part of a group, as into features. Support cited: Stec and Bernstein's Consensus-Implies-Correctness heuristic; Cialdini's comparison cues. Condition: dissociative framing alienates the rejected group by design (card). [545774aaa2ca]
14. **Means-End Chain: sell the benefit of the benefit.** Many buys serve a future goal. Copy and images should show the positive end result, so the buyer weighs the outcome instead of the product's pros and cons. People do not want a shovel, they want holes for trees and flowers; not a microwave box, but time; not paint and leather, but getting from A to B, with ego appeal as "icing". [012ce7b91762] [376eaacb3ff8]
15. **Transtheoretical Model: persuade by stage.** Prochaska (1994). 5 stages: precontemplation (unaware of the product or the need), contemplation (aware, thinking about it), preparation (planning, needs benefit, content and price detail), action (buying), maintenance (habitual buyer). Your audience spans all stages at once. 2 options: one ad that covers all 5 stages, or a series that moves from stage 1 to 5 over time. "It's far easier to reinforce existing consumer attitudes and behavior than to change those values." [f6643f8906df] [2723c7e5dcb7] `[needs brand data: share of traffic that is new vs returning vs repeat buyers]`
16. **Inoculation: make them defend you.** William J. McGuire (Yale). 3 steps: warn of a coming attack, make a weak attack, encourage a strong defence. The more actively people defend, the firmer the belief. The attack must be weak or it can weaken or flip the attitude. Use it to pre-empt competitors: tell buyers what to look out for so they judge rivals "through your filters". Condition: only with legitimate information and a product that is truly better. [d1717caa4ac6]
17. **Belief re-ranking: shift beliefs without a fight.** People defend beliefs even when they know they are wrong. Two routes: change the focus (emotional images of fear, humour, guilt, or facts and examples that show an alternative reality) or change the importance of a belief (easier than changing it). Best: reinforce beliefs buyers already hold with evidence or relatable examples, and add new beliefs that do not conflict. Never say "you're wrong". Instead of "Milk is healthier than soda", show soda's risks and milk's benefits. The author adds that prospects "must remain unaware that you're attempting to influence them" so they own the decision. Some will reject any ad; "a 100-percent response rate is still the stuff of advertisers' dreams". [dd9d7f53de83]
18. **Elaboration Likelihood Model: central vs peripheral route.** High personal relevance (costly or important) means central processing: pour on facts, stats, evidence, testimonials, studies, reports, case histories. Low relevance means peripheral processing: colourful pleasant images, humour, popular topics, celebrities, but still give basic data (inkjet paper: size, colour, weight, sheet count, TAPPI brightness) and say how you beat rivals. Central-route attitudes last longer, resist counter-persuasion and predict behaviour better (research, uncited). `[web check: ELM findings on attitude persistence]` [42c77acaccca] [9ad90e7b96fa]
19. **Cialdini's 6 cues (CLARCCS) for peripheral buys.** Comparison, Liking, Authority, Reciprocation, Commitment/consistency, Scarcity. Cialdini spent 3 years undercover (car sales, telemarketing). Condition: use for peripheral-route products; "fuhgeddaboutit" for luxury or any high-cost, high-thought purchase. [ed3999a8a4e4]
    - Comparison: "Everybody else is doing it, why aren't you?" Internal and external pressure to conform.
    - Liking: works when the buyer likes the rep, the ad's characters, or a fellow user. Attractive people are seen as more trustworthy and likable (Down and Lyons, 1991). Men look most at pictures of men, women at pictures of women, through identification. `[web check: Down and Lyons 1991 citation]`
    - Authority: the "man in the white coat". Actors who played TV doctors kept selling even after the FCC made them say "I'm not really a doctor, but I play one on TV". `[web check: this FCC anecdote]`
    - Reciprocation: give first and people feel they must give back (2-cent pen, $1 bill in a survey mailing, the Hare Krishna rose). It must be a gift of real value, not a junk trinket, not something they asked for ("that's a favor") and not payment for something they did ("that's a thank you"). [91b1b1b9e677] [e9b208a61248]
    - Commitment/consistency ("Four Walls"): a chain of questions, each answer leading to the next, until the buyer is boxed into buying (Cialdini, 1980). Stronger in person because social pressure drives it; in print you need long copy and design to carry the reader to the close. [6f689c1afa38]
    - Scarcity: "we want what we cannot have". Scarcity equals a deadline plus exclusivity. [bca698979925]
20. **Message organisation comes before persuasion.** "If you don't understand what I'm saying, I can't persuade you." Disorganised ads can leave a wrong impression that hurts "for a long time". "Simple is better, but simple isn't necessarily easy." [b261e875d93b] [043af87cd47d]
21. **Examples beat statistics, but match the mix to the product.** Vivid examples cause "self-demonstration" and need less mental effort (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986). "Until you can get your prospects to imagine themselves using your product... they're not going to take the next step." Include both where relevant, and set the proportion so neither buyer type is alienated. The product tells you the mix (see section 4). [7885a72eb3c6]
22. **Two-sided messages persuade more, if you still argue one side.** Allen (1991): two-sided messages beat one-sided only when they defend your position while attacking the competition. Appear fair: compliment the rival, then show why you are better. Comparison charts that show your advantages "at a glance" are "extremely effective" because peripheral thinkers read them as "all the research has been done for me". Condition: central thinkers will check your facts. `[web check: Allen 1991 meta-analysis]` [6fd2919611fb]
23. **The takeaway.** Telling people why they should not buy raises credibility and, for true prospects, desire. The author does it in his introduction ("this book is not for you"). [5ba65827a517]
24. **Repetition builds familiarity; redundancy keeps it fresh.** Repetition grows familiarity, then acceptance, affinity, comfort, trust and the sale. Advertising aims at "marginal differences" that add up. Too much repetition annoys: Petty and Cacioppo (1979) found students who heard an argument 3 times backed it, but agreement fell at 5. The author says not to read this as a 3-run cap: "if it's making you money, by all means, keep it humming". Redundancy (variations of the same message in new formats and wording) extends an ad's life. Multiple sources with slightly different arguments convince more than 1 source (5 people telling you chocolate is healthy). [17b39c491ef9] [c38941fe0dbc]
25. **Rhetorical questions: use for retention, not as proof of persuasion.** Research is split. For: Burnkrant and Howard 1984; Enzle and Harvey 1982; Howard 1990; Howard and Kerin 1994; Petty, Cacioppo and Heesacker 1981; Swasy and Munch 1985; Zillmann 1972; Zillmann and Cantor 1974. Against: Cantor 1979; Munch, Boller and Swasy 1993; Munch and Swasy 1988; Pentony 1990. Gayle, Preiss and Allen (1998) found "no appreciable influence on persuasion". Swasy and Munch (1985) say they can make people feel pressured and see you as less of an authority. Agreed use: questions that emphasise a point may help memory. [ecc5de375921]
26. **People buy when believed value exceeds price (WIIFM).** The $20 envelope exercise: people always ask "What's in the envelope?" before trading, and hesitate even to admit they have $20 (fear of loss). Show a $20 bill in a clear bag and dozens trade $1 for it. [408a28943dcf]
27. **Evidence converts belief into purchase.** Evidence is "any factual statement, object, or opinion not created by a source that is used by that source as support" (Reinhard, 1988): facts, figures, testimonials, endorsements, research, charts, videos you did not create. Buyers want to believe but do not want to be ripped off. Evidence also signals a legitimate business. Most effective for important or expensive buys; peripheral thinkers are swayed too if the evidence is easy to grasp (colourful charts, graphs, quotes from respected professionals). [a9db8de6d903]
28. **Heuristics: most brains take shortcuts.** Stec and Bernstein (1999) name 3: Length-Implies-Strength, Liking-Agreement (Balance Theory) and Consensus-Implies-Correctness. Length-Implies-Strength: a long ad with many credible facts, many testimonials, or a long list of reasons reads as "there must be something to it". Length does not make a claim true; the author's "Torpy's Tricky 200" story (a smear list, 95% quotes out of context, "not 1 in 1,000" readers fact-check) shows how the shortcut can mislead. [952aca21b0fb] [5c8f187766d6]

### 2.3 Core copy doctrines (Chapter 3)

29. **Simplicity is the foundation.** "Write so people can understand." Eugene Schwartz: "Write to the chimpanzee brain." John Caples: "Your ad gets only a fraction of their intelligence." Short words and sentences make reading easier for everyone, whatever their education. [17183d94160c] [3cc6113534e9]
30. **Benefits, not features, in every asset.** A feature is a component; a benefit is what the buyer gets. People read with "What's in it for me?" running non-stop. Nobody cares about your new equipment (unless it benefits them), your 10th anniversary (unless prices drop), your staff photos, baby or cat. [dc660f4f168c] [40638b840ce1]
31. **Put the biggest benefit in the headline, "always, without exception".** Ogilvy: unless the headline sells, "you have wasted 90 percent of your money". The author says 60% of ad readers read headlines only (no citation). The headline must also select the audience ("the ticket on the meat", Ogilvy). [56c0f6af65ab] [db040c273327]
32. **Shorter headlines are read more, but content decides.** Rule 1: always put the biggest benefit in. Rule 2: if 2 headlines are equally good, the shorter one will likely be read by more people. [87ab9b6867da]
33. **Every ad needs a deadline.** Without one, the offer reads as always available and inertia wins. Hard ("Sale Ends August 21st") or soft ("Quantities are Strictly Limited"). "Always feature deadlines." The card adds that the limit must be real. [7181b1222823] [61bd1ae6936b]
34. **A headline must grab attention and make people read on.** Caples' 4 qualities: self-interest, news, curiosity, quick easy way. Curiosity must not come at the cost of targeting: you want "the right eyeballs", not all eyeballs ("SEX!" over patio furniture). [935888c8c1f1] [2f196d76cd7a]
35. **Differences attract attention.** "In a litter of 10 puppies, the purple pooch gets all the attention." Most ads are square or rectangular for the publisher's convenience, not because those shapes work best. [6de4c7a693e4]
36. **Extreme specificity beats vague claims.** "Even if every other restaurant in the city does the same exact thing, because no one else is saying it, the one who does say it, wins!" Specifics make the product feel better and the rivals seem to do less. Works partly through Length-Implies-Strength even when nobody needs the detail. Condition: the product must be at least as good as rivals. [9f88626f46bc] [91a62eefd6d6]
37. **Facts alone do not sell; sensory internal representations do.** All experience is Visual, Auditory, Kinesthetic (feeling), Olfactory, Gustatory (VAKOG). Heighten these "internal representations" so the reader tours the product before buying. A pool cleaner described only by facts ($250, 2-year warranty, safe to swim with) does not sell; the sensory version does. Not every sense fits every product. [b74a2213dcd8] [4366c3a8be93]
38. **The business that describes more looks better.** Advertisers who use vivid, complete descriptions seem "more qualified, better equipped, more conscientious", and rivals who do not seem not to do those things. [4366c3a8be93]
39. **Getting action takes 2 steps: make it easy, then ask.** People are "hypersensitive" to effort and "rather not get involved". The author disclaims: this raises the odds, it does not guarantee action. [d8e25cc5a2b0] [8e185cccc2ed] [3d848b082e13]
40. **Without a difference, buyers have no reason to prefer you.** Commodities like sugar and salt are not preferred. Build a unique selling proposition (USP), but it must be true; if the USP is not who you are yet, "make it who you are". The product must be excellent "or you may educate your potential buyer away from you". [0463438d3951] [6468d5100e59] [902431ce25d6]
41. **Long copy outsells short copy, if it stays interesting.** The author's claim is absolute: "No qualifications. No disclaimers." Ogilvy: "In split run tests, long copy invariably outsells short copy." Halbert: "Copy can never be too long, only too boring!" Sackheim: "The only reason for using short copy is when there isn't much to say." Long copy serves both the buyer who needs every detail and the one who can stop and order after the headline. Condition: no rambling, no padding. [bef86f46c5c6] [db10ac89cee2]
42. **The market tells you what it wants.** "You can't tell the marketplace what to buy from you." No response means the ad, price or offer is wrong, not always that nobody wants the product. Research comes before creative. [58e43cf450ab] [d7cbcd4ec542] [e0ea9b58d9ae]
43. **Guarantees lower pre-purchase stress.** Buyers feel vulnerable between paying and experiencing the product; stress rises with price. The buyer's mind weighs skepticism against the desire to believe; a strong guarantee lightens the skeptic side. Longer, stronger guarantees raise sales and, the author says, cut returns (see section 4). [83551014328e]
44. **Online advertising follows the same rules as print.** "So much for today's techies who keep parroting... 'The rules for online advertising are different!'" A banner does well or poorly by how well it follows the basic principles. [5861e7fcfc77]
45. **Do not be clever. Be clear and interesting.** "In advertising, it's not clever to be clever." Creative does not mean effective. Buyers need benefits, facts, an offer and reassurance. Copy should always be interesting, never boring, but it does not need tricks. Rosser Reeves: when sales fall, you want the curve to turn, not "fine writing". Claude Hopkins: "Don't try to be amusing. Spending money is a serious matter." [0e09cddd6797] [db040c273327]
46. **The marketplace is the final judge.** Epilogue: "Even our best efforts might crash and burn, despite following all the rules." Test.

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Choose the core appeal
1. List what the product does for the buyer. Map each result to an LF8 desire, then to any of the 9 learned wants. [5cfd138f2ca5]
2. Pick the LF8 the product most credibly satisfies. Make it the dominant appeal in headline, main image and first lines (card). `[needs brand data: review language, return reasons, best past ads]`
3. Test framings of the same product. The book's evidence is Haldeman-Julius retitling 5-cent books; the card suggests the same product under different LF8 headlines, judged on purchase conversion.
4. Write copy that makes the reader "demonstrate" the product in their head (3.6).
5. Add belief: proof, testimonials, guarantee (3.12, 3.17).
6. Add the push: ease, deadline, explicit ask (3.15, 3.16).

### 3.2 Build a fear appeal
1. Confirm the product truly resolves the fear. If not, do not use fear. [23a92b334a3e]
2. Pick a fear that is specific and widely recognised. If it needs "a little digging", uncover it with facts (the Purell case used hand-washing surveys and dirty-money data). [724e63d042f4]
3. Include all 4 parts: the threat; a specific recommendation; proof the action works; proof the reader can do it (the karate school must show she can fight off an attacker "using your system", not just print crime stats).
4. Add believability boosters: testimonials, video demos, free lessons or trials.
5. Make the fix easy to act on (Purell's key-chain bottle holders).
6. Do not overdose. Too much fear causes paralysis.
7. Follow with persuasion and a push to act. Fear only opens the door.

### 3.3 Find the end benefit (Means-End ladder)
1. State what the product does ("The sign tells people who you are").
2. Ask "What's the benefit of that?" Write the answer.
3. Repeat until you reach the buyer's final value (who you are, then do business with you, then sell products, then "make money").
4. Make copy and images show that end result. [012ce7b91762] [376eaacb3ff8]

### 3.4 Feature to benefit ("BIG DEAL") drill
1. Write each feature as a plain component.
2. React as the prospect: "BIG DEAL! What's in it for me?"
3. Answer starting with "You benefit by...". Example: multiple ink tanks, then "You benefit by saving big money because you don't have to replace the entire cartridge when only one ink color runs out." 500-sheet tray, then fill it less often ("most other printers hold half as much"). Ultra-draft button, then 50% less ink than others' draft mode.
4. Cut anything with no buyer gain (anniversaries, staff photos, equipment). [dc660f4f168c]
5. Card caveat: do not turn a benefit into a number your product data cannot support. `[needs brand data: measured product facts behind any number]`

### 3.5 Plan a sales letter or long page
1. Write down every benefit. Do not skip this. Benefits only, not features. [799bcba1403a]
2. Rank them by importance to the customer, not to you.
3. Put benefit 1 in the opening.
4. Work ease and speed into the first sentence where true ("Do you want a fast, easy way to...").
5. Open with a question, ideally "Do you want...?" plus a strong benefit. The first sentence's only job is to get the second one read. [6d882bfcd851]
6. Get to the point. Compare the cure-for-cancer letter: 23 years of career history (bad) vs "I have discovered the cure for cancer." (7 words, good). Tear-gas letter: gloomy crime stats (bad) vs "Can you protect yourself against a 220-pound rapist?" (good).
7. Personalise: "Dear Bob" beats "Dear Friend". If you cannot personalise, name the transformed buyer ("Dear Weekend Warrior", "Dear Soon-to-Be-Millionaire"). Never "Dear Occupant".
8. Talk like a person, in the reader's world ("Hey Justin..." not "It is recommended that you act promptly...").
9. Follow AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. The checklist version adds proof: attention, interest, desire, proof, action. Every component (envelope, letter, brochure) must first win attention. [043af87cd47d] [376eaacb3ff8]
10. Restate each benefit from several angles: how the buyer feels, how others see them, safety, savings, what the savings buy. Speak to both peripheral and central thinkers. [bef86f46c5c6]
11. Pile on benefits, pictures, testimonials and a warranty.
12. End with a P.S. that restates the offer, repeats contact details and pushes action; it is "often read first". [1e562730b6b2]

### 3.6 Direct a mental movie (PVAs and VAKOG)
1. Start from the vague line and add, in order: place, object, action, sensory detail ("Go somewhere and do something" to "Go to the kitchen, open the oven, and pull out the pizza", then the full sensory pizza). [8998cd84ed0c]
2. Use action words; they "create moving pictures".
3. Swap bland phrases for powerful visual adjectives (PVAs). See the DON'T/DO pairs in section 7. [4366c3a8be93]
4. Run each of the 5 senses: what it looks like, sounds like, feels like, smells like, tastes like. Use the ones that fit (a printer skips taste but can describe paper texture). [b74a2213dcd8]
5. Tour the reader through use and the after-state (pool cleaner: looks like a 2-foot blue manta ray, climbs walls, "chuga-chuga-chuga", water "sparkles like a Caribbean lagoon").
6. Optional "step-out" line that breaks the ad frame to feel real-time ("While I was typing this, my wife came in and told me the filter was LOADED").
7. Add a sticky name or phrase ("The Orange-Juicy Way to Wash!", lather renamed "Sweet Orange-Cream Bubbles").
8. Keep the claims true; PVAs must not inflate income or health claims (card).

### 3.7 Readability pass
1. Score the copy with Flesch Reading Ease (in Microsoft Word, "a button-click away" `[verify UI]`). Scale 1 to 100; higher is easier. [a2f59bfd45f7]
2. The book's counting rules: count contractions, hyphenated words, abbreviations, figures and symbols as single words (shouldn't, extra-rich, TV, 12, &, $17, 5%). Count syllables as spoken; figures and symbols count as 1 syllable; use the pronunciation with fewer syllables. Count as a sentence each unit ending in a period, colon, semicolon, question mark, exclamation point or dash. Compute average syllables per word and average words per sentence. The book stops there and does not print the formula's coefficients. `[web check: Flesch formula coefficients]`
3. Read the grade table in section 4. If the score is low, "shorten the words and sentences until you get the score you want" (Flesch).
4. Targets: about 11 words per sentence; 70% to 80% one-syllable words; refer to people at least 14 times per 100 words. [d2d7d23b7635] [210eb332ef8f]
5. Prescription 1: short, simple words. Replace enervated, famished, elephantine, recalcitrant, nefarious with tired, hungry, big, stubborn, evil. Cut "and you'll enjoy same", "the aforementioned benefits", "you can liken this to", "herein are 10 reasons". [4960ce995f42] [581f8a1a98cd]
6. Prescription 2: 1 thought per sentence.
7. Prescription 3: the short-short paragraph trick. Ask a question or make a quick statement; answer in the next paragraph in a few words ("Would you like to know a secret way of making money by simply watching TV? / I thought so. Now let me explain..."). Do not overdo it. Keep normal paragraphs to about 4 or 5 short sentences. Ogilvy: limit the opening paragraph to 11 words.
8. Prescription 4: load the copy with pronouns, especially "you" and "I". Start and end sentences with "you"; put it in the headline. [210eb332ef8f]
9. Use definite words (nouns, proper names, pronouns, verbs, specifics). "Joey ate chocolate" beats "Someone did something".
10. Vary length so it does not sound robotic; do not use only 3-letter words. [3cc6113534e9]
11. Other indexes if wanted: Fog Index (years of schooling needed), Flesch-Kincaid (US Department of Defense), McLaughlin SMOG (1969), Forcast (US Army manuals). "Let my computer do the work."

### 3.8 Write the headline
1. Write the #1 buyer benefit. That is the headline's core. [56c0f6af65ab]
2. Make it select the audience. "Need Lamps?" beats "We'll Help You Brighten Up Your Life and Save Big!", which "could be for paint, window cleaning, or even antidepressants".
3. Pick a starter from the 22 (section 6) and fit it to Caples' 4 qualities: self-interest, news, curiosity, quick easy way. [935888c8c1f1]
4. Add a news angle where true (launch, availability, or a tie to current events, weather, sports).
5. Add specifics: numbers, time, price, guarantee (all must be true). [db040c273327]
6. Run the "what is sold?" test: can a reader who sees only the headline tell what you sell? If not, rewrite. [0e09cddd6797]
7. Prefer the shorter of 2 equally good versions. Watch the drop above 12 words. [87ab9b6867da]
8. Format: initial caps ("This Is Initial Caps"); ALL CAPS only if about 4 to 5 words; much bigger than body copy and bold; break lines at natural phrase points; consider putting it in quotes (the checklist says quotes "can boost reading 25 percent", no source). [388f0c750aad] [14dcce0a0c2a]
9. Write several candidates and test them. Change the headline first when an ad fails (3.19).

### 3.9 Bridge headline to body (12 lead-ins)
Use one device that flows from the headline. The book's example headline: "Just Released! Psychologist's Study Reveals Little-Known Speaking Patterns That Immediately Put Rude Salespeople in Their Place." [40367b3bec34]
1. Continue the thought in the headline.
2. Ask a question.
3. Quote a respected authority.
4. Give a free taste (a usable tip).
5. Challenge them to prove it works ("Read pages 8 and 9... then go to the dealership...").
6. Start with a story of skepticism ("When we first received the manuscript... we were skeptical").
7. Tell what others are saying (a signed testimonial; bandwagon).
8. Play reporter (dateline: "Philadelphia, PA: A New York psychologist has just released the findings of a seven-year study...").
9. Get personal with you, you, you.
10. Tell a dramatic story (the book reuses the authority quote as its example here, so the text gives no separate model).
11. Give super-detailed specs ("8 1/2 x 11-inch leatherbound... over 327 pages, 10... chapters, and 45... tools").
12. Use a very short first sentence ("Don't you hate it?", "It's so annoying!", "I can't stand it!").

### 3.10 Extreme specificity inventory
1. Ask: "What can I say about my product or service that may be obvious to me, but my market knows little about?" [9f88626f46bc]
2. List processes, sourcing, time, money and effort spent, craftsmanship, counts, years, guarantees.
3. Ask: "How can I point out the major advantages of my product and make people begin to question the quality of my competition?"
4. Write it as education, with sensory detail (see the pizza, Fratelli's and Handyman Jack's examples in section 7).
5. Check every fact is true and provable (card). `[needs brand data: sourcing, process, materials, counts, years, certifications]`

### 3.11 Inoculation and comparison copy
1. Warn of the coming attack ("Our competitors will tell you...").
2. Make a weak attack: name what rivals say or do.
3. Supply the defence: "...but they don't tell you... At [us], we..." Repeat the pattern per point (mozzarella, dough, delivery). [d1717caa4ac6]
4. Take the consumer-advocate stance: say what is good, bad and suspect in the category.
5. For a two-sided message, first compliment the rival sincerely, then show the change that makes you better ("Acme makes an excellent fly swatter..."). [6fd2919611fb]
6. Where possible, add a comparison chart of your advantages. Ask: faster? easier? cleaner? healthier? more fun? less expensive? more effective?
7. Only state claims you can prove. `[needs brand data: verified competitor facts and own product facts]`

### 3.12 Collect testimonials with permission
1. Write or email customers: "We want to make you famous!" Ask for honest opinions for a new ad, brochure or website, with their photo if they wish. [5ae1f82d7e99]
2. Ask quotable questions. The web designer example: What do you think about our services? What response are you getting? How do we compare with others you've used? What do you like best about how we do business? Are our prices reasonable? Would you recommend us? Would you hire us again?
3. Add a release under the questions. The book's wording: "I give [Company] permission to use my quotes above, in complete or edited form, [ ] with / [ ] without my name and city/state (your address/phone number will not be used) for advertising/publicity purposes. As full compensation, [Company] will provide me with 10 copies of the first printed piece in which my testimonial appears." Add signature and date lines.
4. Give "consideration": send the promised copies, or enclose $1.00 "to help cover any expenses". The author calls this what binds the agreement. This is lay legal advice. `[web check: current consent and endorsement rules for the brand's country and ad platforms]`
5. Pay return postage (a self-addressed stamped envelope).
6. Send the copies.
7. Use volume: many testimonials and customer photos, not 1 (Length-Implies-Strength). [952aca21b0fb]

### 3.13 Survey that sells (a sales letter in survey's clothing)
1. Write a short personal letter on your stationery, mail-merged so it does not look mass-produced ("Dear Eileen: May I ask you a favor?..."). [2995d9888762]
2. Ask 5 or 6 questions: how they feel about the product or service, prices, whether they will buy in the next 1 or 2 months. Use 1 to 10 scales.
3. Add: "If you were the owner of my business, what would you do differently?"
4. At the end, thank them with an offer you "would have offered anyway", open only to respondents, with a date. Book example: a $10 16-ounce protective spray for $5, postage paid, "when you return this completed survey by [DATE]", "HALF-PRICE SPECIAL AVAILABLE ONLY TO SURVEY RESPONDENTS!", a "[ ] YES, DREW!" box and payment options.
5. Enclose a postage-paid reply envelope so payment slips in with the survey.
6. Why it works: people like giving opinions, and the reply is already in hand. The "thank you" framing justifies the low price and makes it exclusive. The feedback "could be worth more than the profit".

### 3.14 Research the market before a promotion (Survey Power)
1. Define who to ask (book example: everyone within 5 miles of a pizzeria, or just your own customers). [e0ea9b58d9ae]
2. Ask: what do you like, dislike, want, prefer, how could it be better, biggest complaint, how often you buy, the most you would pay (for an 18-inch pie with the works), would an extra (2 free drinks) bring you more often.
3. Reward replies (a free slice) and set a short reply window to control cost.
4. Format for ease: a double postcard with a perforation and a Business Reply Mail indicia, or a 1-page letter plus 1-page survey (both can fit on the 2 sides of one 8 1/2 x 11 sheet) and a postage-paid envelope. [ace71faa6f49]
5. Use multiple choice and 1 to 10 semantic differential scales ("how likely are you to return within the next two weeks?").
6. Build offer, price and copy from the answers.

### 3.15 Make buying easy, then ask
1. Pre-fill what you can (name, address); supply a postage-paid envelope; give a toll-free number. [d8e25cc5a2b0]
2. Accept as many payment types as possible (cards, checks, money orders, PayPal; the hot list adds fax and mail orders).
3. Offer several shipping speeds, ground to overnight ("you could lose business if you don't ship fast enough").
4. Offer gift wrapping.
5. Offer a strong guarantee (3.17).
6. Offer a payment plan ("Just $14.99 a month for three months"). The hot list says installments for products over $15 ("3 easy payments of just $10.99") are "shown to boost response 15 percent", and a "Bill Me" or credit option can boost response "50 percent or more". No sources. `[web check: installment and pay-later lift]` [3d848b082e13]
7. Offer web ordering, "especially when your competition doesn't".
8. Tell people exactly how to order, step by step. Retailers: address, phone, short directions, parking, hours.
9. Say "It's Easy to Order!" or a variant ("It's Easy to Get a FREE Estimate!").
10. Ask for the order outright ("Order Now!").

### 3.16 Add a deadline
1. Choose hard (a date) or soft (limited quantity, first N buyers). [7181b1222823]
2. Put it in the ad, the letter, the brochure and on the response device itself. [98e718ecef90]
3. Book phrases: "Call Before April 5th"; "Supplies Are Strictly Limited"; "Offer Expires May 15"; "Price Guaranteed Only Until August 3"; "Offer Good Only Before 4 p.m."; "Seating Is Limited to 50 Participants"; "NO Rain Checks Will Be Issued"; "Good Only for the First 50 Callers".
4. If you use a personal deadline on the envelope ("Please Read This Letter Before 9:30 Tonight!"), justify it in the letter (an order cut-off).
5. Card condition: the deadline or limit must be real. Fake countdowns and always-on sales hurt trust and can break consumer law. [61bd1ae6936b] `[web check: local rules on urgency claims]`

### 3.17 Write the guarantee
1. Offer the longest, strongest guarantee in your industry (the author gives a full 1-year money-back guarantee on many products). [83551014328e]
2. Choose a long term (6 months, 1, 5, 10 years, lifetime) over 30, 60 or 90 days.
3. Consider a bolder form (double your money back) only if you can absorb it.
4. Show it off: certificate border, your signature under it, on ads, brochures, website, everywhere.
5. If rivals are weaker, ask in bold type: "Why Do Our Competitors Guarantee their [PRODUCT TYPE] for Only 90 Days?" then "Do they know something about their [PRODUCT] that they're not telling you?"
6. Make sure operations can honour it (card). `[needs brand data: current return rate, refund cost, margin]`

### 3.18 Build a USP and authority position
1. Ask: What's unique about my offer? Am I the only one offering it? What's unique about me? [58d1c3f4971b]
2. Name the business or system as a category of one ("The Hardware Superstore!", "The Office Cleaning Technicians!", "Experts of the RapidSell System!"). If the system does not exist, build it, then name it. [0463438d3951] [6468d5100e59]
3. Make it congruent: hire people who fit, uniforms with logo, repainted van, new stationery and brochure. "What I say I am and how I'm perceived are now congruent."
4. Tell people why you are great, proactively (Nifty Fifty's lists never-frozen beef, beef ground fresh daily, fresh-cut fries, the frying oil, and shows the kitchen through a window).
5. Position each offer too, with a "specifically unique", true hook (section 7). [902431ce25d6]
6. Authority, step 1: believe your knowledge is valuable. Step 2: publish it in many forms. [4036507c46bc]
7. Get a professional headshot and put it on everything (ads, emails, web pages, letters, contracts).
8. Write 3 to 10 page reports (print and PDF) with your photo, name caption and business name on the cover, then numbered tips inside ("RESUME RESPONSE BOOSTER #1: Layout & Design"). Offer them free in ads and to walk-ins, with a coupon inside.
9. Run editorial-style Q&A "columns": about 3 short questions and answers, your photo and name at top, and at the bottom "Have questions about...? Send them to: [address]". Some papers print them free.
10. Also: e-zine, seminars, workshops, free clinics, book, radio and TV interviews, blog.

### 3.19 Test offers and diagnose a failing ad
1. If an ad fails, do not conclude nobody wants the product. [58e43cf450ab]
2. Change the headline first; it "will make or break your ad in the first few seconds". [d7cbcd4ec542]
3. Then check the price against the market.
4. Then try a different offer. A "Grand Opening" ad offers nothing; try a free evaluation or 50% off the first visit.
5. Reframe the same deal: "2 Visits for the Price of 1!" or "Buy One Get One FREE" instead of "50% off" (the author says testing showed BOGO wins; no data given). `[web check: BOGO vs percent-off framing]`
6. Segment offers by audience and month ("April Special: FREE Spinal Exams for Runners", then Body Builders).
7. Add a wanted extra you can deliver cheaply ("FREE Massage with Every Visit!").
8. Try structural offers: family special, couple deal, senior rate, every 5th visit free, a free gift with the first visit, a free expert report.
9. Keep testing "until you find one that delivers results that knock your socks off". Ask: "How can I give my customers more?"
10. Judge by response: calls, redemptions, orders. Key each ad to track it. [388f0c750aad] `[needs brand data: baseline conversion rate and traffic per variant, to size tests]`

### 3.20 Editorial (camouflage) ad
1. Read several stories in the target publication to learn its tone. [0073339a70d1]
2. Write the ad as a news story: same typeface, column width, leading and headline style.
3. News-style headline and lead ("Local Hypnotist Announces New Way to Quit Smoking in 48 Hours").
4. Give the same benefits in a reporter's voice; add customer quotes.
5. Never sound too enthusiastic or "hypey".
6. End with a call to action; offer a free report for more response.
7. Card caveat: a hypey ad in news clothing reads as deceptive. `[web check: native-ad and advertorial disclosure rules for each platform and country]`

### 3.21 Grabber mailing and split test
1. Attach a small relevant object to the top of the letter (coin, $1 bill, bag of sand, NSF check, candy wrapper plus a coupon for the candy, a badly printed brochure, a bad wedding photo). [1557d7445b77]
2. Open by naming it and tying it to the promise ("As you can see, I have attached a crisp one-dollar bill... I'm going to show you... how to save 100 of these bills...").
3. Make sure it will not rip the envelope; use bubble-lined envelopes for bulky items.
4. Test: 500 letters with the grabber vs 500 without; compare response.

### 3.22 Direct-mail package (the Granny Rule)
1. Make it look personal, not slick: plain white envelope, hand-written address in blue ballpoint, real stamp, warm hand-written style letter that uses the reader's name. "Granny gets every one of her letters opened." [98e718ecef90] [ca19e5bc922a]
2. A slick package works only for a very well-targeted list.
3. Pick lists by fit first, then recency, frequency and money spent (a "hotline" list of recent, repeat, high spenders is best).
4. Teasers: prefer none or a neutral one ("PERSONAL ATTENTION OF:", "PERSONAL LETTER FOR:", "IMPORTANT LETTER FOR:"). Use an informational teaser only when the product is pin-point targeted to the list.
5. Envelope options: bright colours; a blood-red "URGENT" stamp; many low-value stamps; a hand-written note by the name ("Eileen: Here's the Information We Talked About! -Drew"; cold: "Cyndi: Please Tell Me if I'm Wrong About You").
6. No mailing labels. Use a printer, a letter shop's handwriting font, or pay a seniors' group to hand-address.
7. Letter font: once Courier; now serif faces like Times, Schoolbook, Bookman, Palatino.
8. Response device: call it a "Personal Trial Certificate" or "Personal, No-Obligation Trial Certificate", not an "Order Form"; pre-fill name and address; print on canary yellow; restate the offer; big "YES!" by a ticked box; small picture of what they get (Caples); play up the guarantee; address, phone, email and web at the bottom; name and address on every piece; deadline on the form.
9. Enclose a business reply envelope or a live stamp; stamp it in red "RUSH! ORDER ENCLOSED!" or "24-HOUR PROCESSING".

### 3.23 Print layout and space
1. Ogilvy layout: top 2/3 one big photo; headline directly under it; body copy below, starting with a drop cap; logo lower right. Reverse Ogilvy: top 1/3 photo, headline, 2/3 copy. [6e8715d19594]
2. Put a selling or human-interest caption under every picture.
3. Use a pro designer (3.24).
4. White-wrap: buy more space and centre the original ad in white. Extra space should not exceed 60% of the ad's area; about 20% extra gives the best gain per cost (E.K. Strong). [92fae8cc56b2]
5. Coupon border: surround the offer, order form or bonus with a bold broken block border; the author used a 3-point border with a very light yellow tint, and prefers small rectangles as the "dots". [91febd568d09]
6. Circle ad: set copy normally (not curved) inside a circle within the square space; fill the corners black or a colour. [6de4c7a693e4]
7. Half-Page Island: 2 columns wide, 3/4 of a page deep; papers often put only news beside it. Small ads suit a 2-step sale (inquiry, then full info); a full sale needs more space. [95b5fa5fb959]
8. For more attention, multiply ads in one issue (formations in section 6) or add colour before size (section 4). [202b932aacd1] [1b874981702b]

### 3.24 Hire a designer
1. Call local ad agencies, ask for the art or creative director, say you run a small business and need a freelance designer for an ad, brochure or flyer. Ask for several names. [9d29226e84a6]
2. Online: review the portfolio, ask the rate, and ask if billing is hourly or per project. "Don't sound big."
3. Low budget: ask graphic-arts schools for students.
4. Always ask for samples before paying.

### 3.25 Give a reciprocation gift
1. Pick something of real value the recipient will think is thoughtful: a sample, a gift certificate, 30 minutes of free time with no obligation, a month of lessons. [e9b208a61248]
2. Give it unasked, not as a reward.
3. Book examples: groomer, a flea and tick collar; bakery, rye bread or cookies; bike shop, a water bottle; shoe repair, a shine; karate school, a free month; printer, 50 free copies; florist, a bunch of flowers; cigar shop, a lighter; pizzeria, a slice.
4. Time it to a moment of need (Moving Targets sends new movers certificates for free pizza, an entree or an oil change). [91b1b1b9e677]
5. Deliver the experience well; the gift only opens the door.

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

### 5.1 The 46-point "Killer Ad" checklist (verbatim items, grouped)
"Check all that apply to your ad; the more, the better." [388f0c750aad] [f63f25f551b5]

Headline
- Does it feature your product's biggest benefit? ("The #1 most important rule.")
- Is it a real grabber? Does it elicit an emotional response?
- Does it use any of the 22 headline starters?
- Is it significantly larger than your body copy? Boldfaced too?
- Is it powerful enough to get people to read your body copy?
- Does it make some kind of offer?
- Is it authoritative, and not wimpy?
- Is it set in initial caps? (ALL CAPS only if about 4 to 5 words.)
- Is it in quotes? ("can boost reading 25 percent")

Body copy, first sentence
- Does it use one of the 12 lead-ins?
- Does it flow naturally from the headline?
- Does it get right into benefits, instead of bragging about your company?
- Does it almost force them to read the second sentence?
- Is "you" one of the first few words?

Body copy, general
- Does it focus on how the reader will benefit?
- Does it say why to buy from you rather than a competitor with the same product?
- If the product is exciting, does the ad sound exciting?
- Does it progress logically: attention, interest, desire, proof, action?
- Are you selling only 1 product at a time? (Delis and furniture stores can show more, catalog style.)
- Do selling subheads break up long copy?
- Is the copy colourful, with PVAs where appropriate?
- Is it believable, not overblown?
- Is it respectful, not insulting to the reader's intelligence?
- Is it emotional (positive or negative)?
- Do you use extreme specificity?
- Are words, sentences and paragraphs short? Simple words?
- Is print set in a serif face (Schoolbook) and web copy in a sans serif (Arial, Verdana)?
- Do you say what to do in a super-simple way? (1. Clip this coupon. 2. Bring it to our store by August 21. 3. Save 50%.)
- Do you outright ask for the sale?
- Did you set a deadline, if appropriate? ("Most of the time it is!")
- Are many benefits shown as bullets or numbers?
- Do you use testimonials? ("If you don't have them, get them!")
- Is your business name and phone number large and noticeable?
- Did you include your logo? (Every time; it builds brand equity.)
- Do you give directions, maps or landmarks?
- Did you key the ad to track responses?

Layout and design
- Did a professional designer produce it? ("Not a layout person!")
- Is the headline big and bold?
- Is the headline broken at the right words? (Wrong: "Now You Can Throw Away Your / Glasses and Enjoy 20/20 Vision Again!" Right: break after "Glasses".)
- Is the ad easy to read with a clear focus, so the eye is pulled to certain areas first?
- Is there enough white space? Did you white-wrap it?
- Are paragraphs indented?
- Are separate elements kept to a minimum? (No "million little tint blocks", 3 bursts, 2 bullet blocks, a corner flag and 4 reverse panels.)
- Is the art relevant? ("Please, no babies for steel-belted tire ads!")
- Are typestyles kept to 1 or 2, 3 max?
- Is there a picture of a person looking at you?

Card additions not in the book: mobile layout, page speed, trust badges, payment security. [f63f25f551b5]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Writing ads about yourself (factory, staff, equipment, anniversary, pets) instead of the buyer's gain. [40638b840ce1] [dc660f4f168c]
2. Listing features without benefits. [dc660f4f168c]
3. Ads that "only inform" and do not ask for action; ads that win awards but do not sell. [329ff6de64b5]
4. Being clever, cute or punny in headlines; "creative" is not "effective". [0e09cddd6797]
5. Curiosity headlines that pull the wrong audience ("SEX!" for patio furniture, a mortgage envelope). Confusing clicks with sales. [5861e7fcfc77] [935888c8c1f1]
6. Headlines that do not select an audience ("We'll Help You Brighten Up Your Life and Save Big!"). [56c0f6af65ab]
7. Vague, amorphous claims ("Homes of Distinction", "better ingredients"). [9f88626f46bc]
8. Writing like a "constipated Harvard Law professor": big words, long sentences, school-taught filler. [4960ce995f42]
9. Fear with no real solution, creating new fears, or so much fear that people freeze. [23a92b334a3e]
10. A strong "weak attack" in inoculation copy; it can flip the buyer against you. [d1717caa4ac6]
11. Telling buyers they are wrong; head-on attacks on beliefs trigger defensiveness. [dd9d7f53de83]
12. Using CLARCCS cues alone on luxury or high-cost items. [ed3999a8a4e4]
13. No deadline: "Take your time deciding" kills response. [7181b1222823]
14. Cheap trinket gifts (logo key rings) instead of gifts of value. [e9b208a61248]
15. Timid copy written to sound "pretty, elegant, and graceful" instead of for response. Afraid to write vivid copy; copying everyone else's "business inbreeding". [b74a2213dcd8]
16. Assuming "no one reads long copy". [bef86f46c5c6]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Age and channel.** The book is from 2008 to 2009 and is built for print ads, direct mail and early web. Most numbers come from print readership studies between 1912 and 1991, and online data from 1995 to 2002. None of it measures purchase conversion on modern ecommerce, social feeds, short video or mobile. Treat every number as directional and test it. `[web check: any study before relying on its number]`

**Evidence quality.** Most studies appear with author and year only. The author never shows his own test data. Claims with no source at all: LF8 drive "more sales than all other human wants combined"; 60% read only headlines; BOGO beats 50% off; headline in quotes +25%; "Bill Me" +50%; installments +15%; long guarantees cut returns; animated banners +15% to 40%; mystery ads +18% CTR; captions read by 2x as many; the colour-psychology anecdotes; Ponds used the first testimonial in 1926. The section 7 historical sales figures (Listerine 53%, Odorno +112%, Canada Dry +25%, Quaker, Hormel, Haldeman-Julius) are anecdotes. `[web check]` each before quoting to a client.

**Internal inconsistencies.**
- Age of Propaganda is dated 2001 in the fear section and 1991 in the ego section. Cialdini's Influence is cited as 1980 and 1998.
- Headlines: short headlines are read more (Rudolph, Starch), but Ogilvy says "longer headlines sell more products". The author settles it with 2 rules: biggest benefit always, then shorter if equal.
- Supercharger 5 says "WRITE long copy"; supercharger 6 says "BOIL it down; cut out the fluff." Read together: long, but no padding.
- Price endings: the text says 88 conveys value, but its own cited test (Schindler and Kibarian) found 88 did no better than 00.
- The web headline example quotes $199 in one version and $250 in the other.
- Lead-in 10 ("dramatic story") reuses lead-in 3's authority quote, so no story model is given.
- Fonts: serif for print, sans serif (Arial, Verdana) for screens. This is consistent, but the online advice rests on 72 and 96 dpi screens.
- The colour-pair table, the white-wrap table and the Haldeman-Julius table are garbled in the export. The pairings given above are the most likely reading.
- The Flesch section lists the 5 counting steps but omits the formula's coefficients.

**Ethics and law (book vs today).**
- The author says prospects "must remain unaware that you're attempting to influence them". He calls persuasion ethical only with a quality product. He tells the "Torpy's Tricky 200" story (95% out-of-context quotes) as a heuristic lesson, not a model to copy.
- "Always feature deadlines" and soft limits ("Supplies Are Strictly Limited") need real limits today. Fake urgency can breach consumer-protection law. `[web check: urgency and fake-countdown rules in the brand's markets]`
- Editorial "camouflage" ads that blend into news may need an "advertisement" or "sponsored" label today. `[web check: native ad and advertorial disclosure rules]`
- Testimonial collection: the $1 or copies "consideration" is lay legal advice. Modern endorsement rules on incentives and disclosure apply. `[web check: endorsement and review rules]`
- Fear copy on health and hygiene (Lysol, Purell, mites) implies claims that need substantiation today. `[web check: health claim rules]`
- Authority: lab coats and actors playing doctors are the book's examples of the cue, not something to copy. Only real, permitted endorsements are safe (cards).

**Conflicts with common ecommerce advice.**
- Long copy: the author says long copy wins "no qualifications, no disclaimers". Common ecommerce practice favours short, scannable mobile PDPs and ads. Card db10ac89cee2 says the source treats length as situational; the book does not. Only Sackheim's line ("short copy when there isn't much to say") gives a condition. Note too that the book's own online evidence (UIE, MarketingExperiments, Online-Learning.com) is about pages and scrolling, not ad units.
- Deadlines on every ad clash with advice to avoid always-on discounting and to protect margin and brand.
- "Buy 1 Get 1 Free beats 50% off" is stated as tested, with no data.
- Serif body text: the book itself says sans serif for screens. Today's web norm agrees, so use its print advice for packaging, inserts and print only.
- Email: 69% open rates and the "60% can read HTML" figure are early-2000s numbers and are far from current norms. Privacy rules and inbox changes also affect "recipient name in every subject line". `[web check: current email benchmarks]`
- "Put your face on everything": fits founder-led and service brands; the book gives no evidence for product brands.
- Colour ranking (blue first) is generic. Brand identity and category colour codes (Coca-Cola "owns" red) can override it; the book's own Canada Dry case shows this.

**Card deviations to watch** (card text that differs from the book).
- [d7cbcd4ec542] says change the offer before the headline. The book says change the headline first, then check price, then change the offer.
- [58e43cf450ab] describes "Editorial Energizers" as sidebars and pull quotes, and "Survey Power" as disguising research as editorial. In the book, Editorial Energizers are whole ads written as news stories, and the disguised item is a sales letter in survey form.
- [91a62eefd6d6] describes the "Ogilvy Principle" as studying Ogilvy's views. In the book it is the 2/3 photo, 1/3 copy layout rule.
- [2f196d76cd7a] says to add "grabbers" before the headline and ties them to the 12 lead-ins. In the book, grabbers are physical objects attached to letters; the 12 lead-ins are body-copy openers.
- [db10ac89cee2] says the source treats long vs short as situational. The book does not.
- [037f584ed120] lists Cialdini's cues with "social proof"; the book's list says "comparison".
- Many cards add ecommerce translations (PDPs, carts, countdowns, pay-later, carousels). These are the card writers' ideas, not the book's.

**What is missing.**
- No method for finding which LF8 a given audience responds to, beyond testing. `[needs brand data: review mining, survey answers, past test results]`
- No sample sizes, significance or test length for any test it recommends. Only the grabber test gives a size (500 vs 500), with no success rule.
- No pricing strategy beyond price endings and value framing. No margin checks on free gifts, guarantees, double-your-money-back or BOGO. `[needs brand data: margins, return and refund costs]`
- No guidance on video, social feeds, platform ad formats or creative fatigue metrics. Channel mechanics live in 24-DEP (deployment) and 10-MCR (creative).
- Little on retention beyond the survey letter and the maintenance stage of the Transtheoretical Model.
- The author admits the NLP "open loop" idea behind question copy has no scientific support. He also says rhetorical-question research is mixed.
