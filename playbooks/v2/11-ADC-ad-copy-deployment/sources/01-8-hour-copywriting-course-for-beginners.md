## 1. What this source is for

It teaches a beginner how to write direct-response copy, starting with 200 to 300 word emails and moving to landing pages, video sales letters (VSLs), email sequences, automations and simple funnels. For an ecommerce operator it is most useful as: a writing and editing checklist (4-part copy formula, attention, pacing, belief shifts, proof, one CTA); 3 email skeletons (CJN, PPAS, HSO); a line-by-line review habit with dozens of worked edits; a landing page block map (eyebrow, headline, deck, scroll stoppers, bullets, value stack, urgency, FAQ); a basic lifecycle email set (welcome, sales and deadline, revival); and a funnel vocabulary (cost per lead, lead conversion rate, AOV, ROAS). It is weak on physical-product specifics, testing and measurement.

## 2. Principles

1. **Simple copy wins, even for smart readers.** Write no more complicated than a third-grade reading level, "because that's what most people read at". Condition: all copy, including B2B and "sophisticated" audiences; the source says there is "no downside to simplifying". Simple copy reaches weak readers and helps strong readers understand faster ("win-win"). Avoid technical jargon even when writing to business owners. Evidence: presenter opinion; he cites Alex Hormozi explaining complex tactics simply. Simple does not mean vague: the source still wants specific, qualified promises. [495714e45312] [474c5d2ad56a] [ccb66d506164]
2. **Every piece of copy does 4 jobs: attention, consumption, persuasion, action.** Attention gets past the brain's "spam filter"; consumption makes it easy and intriguing to read; persuasion shifts beliefs; action ends on one effective CTA. Condition: all formats. The beginning matters most: he cites MrBeast spending half or more of the effort on title, thumbnail and the first 10 seconds, and the same for ad hooks and sales-page headlines. [583aa3b7fca0] [f90f84d09846]
3. **Lower the effort to read, raise the intrigue.** "The best way to get your message heard is to decrease the effort required to consume and then increase the intrigue." Write lean (no fluff). A long intro that takes time to reach the point gets no readers. [f90f84d09846]
4. **Attention has 3 parts: a promise that is not spammy, intrigue that is not confusing, emotion that is not clickbait.** Promise: say what the reader gets (avoiding pain counts as a benefit). Intrigue: give hints the reader cannot guess ("guess what we're eating... it starts with M"). Emotion: high-tension, dramatic moments, but the payoff must match the headline and deliver value. Clickbait is a subject line or thumbnail that does not match what is inside, which leaves the reader feeling cheated. [ccb66d506164] [f90f84d09846]
5. **Spammy promises cost reach.** Over-the-top claims ("$$$ make $10,000 in 20 days") look fake, can push email into spam, and on Meta can get an ad account shut down if anything even looks like spam. Be honest "like you're talking to a friend" and follow platform rules. Evidence: presenter's illustrative open-rate drop (see section 4); no study. [f90f84d09846] [web check: current Meta advertising standards and Gmail spam rules]
6. **Challenge a commonly held belief to create intrigue ("the big challenge").** Controversially disagree with a common belief; people want to know why. Restating advice they have heard 100 times ("take action and implement") gets skimmed; invert it ("the exceptional stop learning") or add a "not statement" ("take action, but not the way most people do"). Condition: the payoff must follow, and the angle must be justifiable. Source notes blunt confrontation suits roughly 18 to 24 year old men and not older readers or women (his opinion). [8919626d89c1] [ccb66d506164] [88a07c18db1b]
7. **Readers have "TikTok brain": judge copy by value per minute, not total value.** Source says attention is now 60 seconds or less. A 200-word high-value email beats a 10,000-word one because nobody reads the long one. His own example: he recorded 7 hours for his earlier course and published the best 4. [d65a7b03c6a3] [96efc25c802b]
8. **Pace like an action movie.** All fast is boring; all slow is boring. Slow down (longer sentences) to explain the key point; speed up (short punchy lines) to excite and to recap; mix them; use transitions. Do not stack long blocks at the top and short lines at the bottom, and do not write all short lines ("reading vertically"). [a5360ff26b1f] [ce4adcb0c5bf]
9. **Keep open loops open until you are ready to open the next one.** Save the punchline for the end; reveal it while planting another setup. The common beginner failure is giving the punchline too early, which kills the reason to keep reading. [0cf0f5e70b0b]
10. **Persuasion is belief shifting, and people rarely lack knowledge.** "If someone has a desire it's almost always their beliefs that stop them." Value lives in the "how" (how to curb cravings), not the "what" everyone knows (eat fewer calories). Understand and state the reader's current belief before you try to move it. [68cf28c41359]
11. **Convince by showing why you are convinced, not by telling them.** Telling causes friction and objections; nobody can argue with your own experience. "Here's how my arms went from this to this in 8 weeks, I'll show you how I did it" is "the angle you want to go for 90% of the time" (his heuristic). Swap "it's actually the opposite" for "in my experience it was the opposite". [60835ecfe3d0] [e9741d568a97]
12. **Correcting causes friction; understanding first does not.** Saying "wrong" makes people dig in. Say "I see how you could think that; I thought that too until I found..." [68cf28c41359]
13. **Belief follows evidence, not demands.** Kidnapper analogy: you can say you believe someone is Jesus under threat, but you only truly believe after water turns to wine. Supply evidence strong enough that holding the old belief becomes hard. Trust on the internet is "at an all-time low", so proof must be "beyond a reasonable doubt". [574fd7aa2de4] [68cf28c41359]
14. **An ounce of proof weighs the same as 100 lb of promise** (the outreach section says 1,000 lb). Promise less, prove more. Speak from curiosity ("this has worked for hundreds of others, let's see if it works for you") and to the right avatar ("if you're coachable and serious"), never "it works for everyone". Testimonial marketing is "the most safe yet effective form". Promises must be attainable and hyper-specific. [017ae2c141f9] [792856b9a23c]
15. **Stories are the strongest belief shifters, but most people write a chronology.** A chronology lists events; a story has a main character, a clear goal, adversity, and a lesson handed to the reader. Put the lesson at the end: skimmers jump to the end and ask "so what?", and a lesson stated first sounds like something they have already heard. [f6de27013260] [e8cdc6cbf78e] [4a2f08fa92d1]
16. **Relive the story, do not retell it.** Retold: "I was working long hours, low pay, no promotion, so I started my business." Relived: one specific day, physical detail, a line of dialogue, the moment of decision. Source says relived stories get more engagement and emotion (practitioner claim). [a3f3d55f4e85] [adc834e923a1]
17. **Put the reader first.** Use "you", not "I, we, us". Give a what's-in-it-for-me (WIIFM) near the start of most copy. Before a story, tell the reader why they should listen and tie it back to them. Cut CTAs that are about the writer ("if you'd like to hear more about me"). [51f77f32f63c] [a98bb10fd49f]
18. **People buy from people they know, like and trust (KLT).** Top of mind (repeated exposure), proof you understand their situation, and personal copy "like chats between friends". Condition: stated as universal; the card notes low-price impulse products need less relationship. [2cabf960513c] [2976c3241814]
19. **Perceived value must exceed price.** "Nobody buys something for $100 that's worth $100." Benefit bullets are the most efficient way to raise perceived value; compare and contrast against the alternative ("throwing stones"). [f5368da16a22] [29770c1882af]
20. **"Now or never" drives action, but only situational urgency and scarcity keep goodwill.** Tie urgency to a real, fact-based situation. Non-situational tactics ("first 10 buyers get a bonus") are fine only in promotions and only sparingly because they cost goodwill and read as salesy. Fake scarcity on infinite digital goods ("our ebook will sell out") makes no sense. Scarcity = only 100 left; urgency = limited time. [8f116e83a3f3] [073d27caa4b0]
21. **Action's biggest enemy is confusion.** Give one CTA, simple and specific. Too many options (follow me, buy the course, buy one of 4 products) means no click. Every funnel step has one action. Source also allows an optional "double CTA" (the same CTA twice, or a P.S.) and repeats the CTA in deadline emails. [b1fc36d11cab] [cab8c603e36c] [744657e341ac]
22. **Use problem-solution loops to give value and still earn the click.** Every solution opens a new problem ("more solutions, more problems"). Close one loop with real value, then open the next and put its solution behind the CTA. Say what to do, not how; the how is the click. [0cf0f5e70b0b] [3e63ec1c1227] [a98bb10fd49f]
23. **Withholding is not the same as having nothing.** Problem, agitate, then "if you want to know how, click here" with no mechanism, hint or intrigue raises clicks but lowers purchases ("I might click but I ain't buying"). Do not chase vanity metrics. [0cf0f5e70b0b]
24. **Copy is assembled, not written.** Copywriting "isn't writing, it's synthesizing": compile the brand's own material, then drop it into a known structure. "Steal the ideas, not the words." [b88d5fdf5214] [7fd437ffe114] [b245a0ded890]
25. **Emails are the base unit of longer copy.** A VSL and a sales page are "a bunch of emails". Learn short emails first (200 to 300 words), because they are quick to finish and easy to critique. [100d390ce854] [59621c0a5c22] [a81371537582]
26. **Unique mechanism: sell the thing that sells the thing.** Name the feature that makes the product different and better (vacuum with a "V12 engine" that does not lose suction; "new rubber stud technology" winter tyre vs a plain winter tyre bought on price). Explain why it works and why the alternatives fail, so the product escapes commodity price comparison. Source claims "price doesn't matter" then (opinion). The mechanism must be real; his examples are invented for teaching. [ecb28758fd66] [79bb35cef343] [384b4df664c6]
27. **Match the brand's voice and the avatar.** A CPA does not talk like a business-opportunity coach ("your dream life is 12 weeks away"). Heavy emojis clash with a no-nonsense persona. Do not narrow a hook to a slice of the list ("if you're 40ish") unless you segment. Source also says men and women "think and speak differently" and should get different lead benefits (his generalisation, not data). [3f95809c1b82] [88a07c18db1b] [177dbbe025fd] [134e00efad90]
28. **Entertainment copy is a minority tool.** Gimmicks (no-subject emails, joke openers) work occasionally, but if every email is a gimmick readers go blind. Source's own list is about 80% plain benefit emails with a specific intrigue. Play safe with a new client; add entertainment only after the emails are working. [99d753c33b55]
29. **Email builds goodwill like a bank.** Value and entertainment emails are deposits; promos are withdrawals. The most common owner mistake is "promo promo promo" to a list that has had no deposits. [520f11f16e90] [48a87de9b79b]
30. **Why repeated email works: the incident-motivation-complacency cycle (IMC).** People join a list after an incident (stepping on the scale after 6 months, a shirt that no longer fits, friends teasing). Motivation leads to action, action hits resistance, resistance leads to complacency, until the next incident. Repeated sends are there when the next incident hits. Same logic as McDonald's and Coca-Cola advertising though everyone knows them: be top of mind at the right moment. [e8ba196b022f]
31. **Know how people got on the list before you write.** Newsletter opt-ins want value, entertainment and updates; lead-magnet opt-ins want the promised thing; buyers already paid. Each expects different email. In ecommerce, the source says the list is buyers plus people who gave an email for a discount, so everyone already knows the product. [3ce42487fd05] [8a4864a0a123]
32. **Tracking changes behaviour.** "If you're not tracking you're slacking." Tracking a metric "is enough to bring attention to it and optimise by default" (his analogy: tracking calories or time). Start with small metrics, add more later. Without tracking you are hoping for luck. Keep one method for at least 1 month as a control, then try to beat it. [07386e6caf36] [6649b7b1fce5]
33. **AI is a tool, not the writer.** "Saying AI will replace copywriting is like saying calculators will replace mathematicians." Never paste long ChatGPT copy; use it for ideas, analogies, explanations, examples, stories and line-level improvements. [57ebdcf883f4] [5f65d6e25e3c]
34. **Copy is one layer of a marketing stack.** Email sequences, automations, VSLs, landing pages, offers and funnels together make the money ("copywriting is an introduction to digital marketing"). Marketing "has a lot to do with numbers". [0f415a3ed151] [0bba65ed7c6c]
35. **Structure can be stolen; claims cannot.** For VSLs, study category leaders and copy the structure closely ("structure is something you can basically steal"), then fill it with your own facts. [7da6cda41cbe]
36. **Quality over quantity, but quality comes from quantity.** "To learn quality you must first produce quantity"; your 101st email beats the best first email you could write. Habits must be implemented before they can be optimised ("start sloppy"). [977fa0fc63ca] [7fd437ffe114]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Write a first email: compile, ideate, structure, polish [b88d5fdf5214] [7fd437ffe114] [a81371537582]
1. Pick one business to write for (source: from a swipe file of landing pages). Do not practise by writing copy about copywriting; write for a real business, and put its link at the top of the draft so a reviewer can check the angle. [977fa0fc63ca]
2. Compile (about 25 minutes) into one document: the brand's common sayings; angles you notice; hooks (how their videos or posts open); the offer, including the backend offer; "why what most people teach is inferior" (their critique of alternatives, for example science-based training vs "bro science"); stories (what happened, who, how it ended, the main lesson), focusing on stories that show a belief shift and identify with the audience; lines where they prove they understand the audience. Also note how people get on their list. [b245a0ded890] [243af58d06bd] [3ce42487fd05]
3. Optional AI distillation: find the creator's most popular YouTube video, open the description, click "more", then "Show transcript", copy it all and paste into ChatGPT; repeat for a few videos. Prompt (source wording, trimmed): "Distill this YouTube video into a few paragraphs. Tell me the most important points covered. Tell me if there are any common phrases used. Tell me anything that seems like a core message. Tell me if there are any stories; if so describe the story, what happened and how the characters and the story fell. Tell me any points where the speaker heavily relates to the audience and proves he knows exactly what the audience is going through, what they might think and why." A 17-minute video takes about 30 seconds this way. Then ask it for angles, justifications and stories. [verify UI: YouTube transcript button] [4e85d10ef802] [42ebacbe34c3]
4. Ideate (about 5 minutes): pick one angle and one lesson.
5. Structure (about 15 minutes): choose CJN, PPAS or HSO (3.2 to 3.4) before writing.
6. Polish (about 15 minutes): run the edit checklist (3.6).
7. Sleep on it and reread the next day with fresh eyes, to catch lines that only make sense because you wrote them.
8. Get it reviewed (source: his Discord or Skool community), and review 3 other people's pieces for every 1 you submit.
9. Timing: about 1 hour for the first email, about 30 minutes for later emails because compile and ideate are reused. Source later says he writes 2 emails an hour while narrating (about 20 minutes each). Expect beginners to take longer; focus on reps, not perfection; leave ego out and thank harsh reviewers.

### 3.2 CJN: Challenge, Justify, Need [930c67d53174] [f95389e976bd]
The source names this structure himself ("something I personally came up with") but says the pattern has been used for years.
1. Challenge: state something counterintuitive to a common belief, so readers want to know why ("Squats are terrible for your legs"; "8 cups of water a day is unhealthy"; "8 hours of sleep is unhealthy").
2. Justify the new belief with reasons or a mechanism (squats: balance, early fatigue, hard to keep form, injuries; water: needs vary with body size, activity and climate, drink when thirsty, most hydration comes from food).
3. Need: tie the shifted belief to one CTA ("If you want to maximise your legs without the hassle and risk of squats, check out my video"; "read our guide").
4. Caveat the source states openly: "there are so many ways to bake a cake", you could write the opposite email and justify it too, and he says "is this true or not" is beside the point. For this project, only use a challenge the product can honestly justify (see section 9).

### 3.3 PPAS: Problem, Promise, Agitate, Solution [3e63ec1c1227] [a3f13080cb06]
The source's rename of PAS, to show where the WIIFM goes.
1. Problem: a specific problem the reader has, in about 3 to 4 lines ("Low motivation? Try this").
2. Promise (WIIFM): you know how to fix it, in about 2 to 3 lines ("Here's a way to stay motivated and fired up to go every day").
3. Agitate: show you understand it, and why fix it now; where they end up if they never fix it (about 7 to 8 lines). Example: missed workouts stall progress, every skipped workout sets you back, momentum gets harder to regain.
4. Solution: the CTA (about 7 lines), telling them what to do, not how ("Download this app... click here to start your free trial").
5. Do not order it problem, agitate, WIIFM: by the time the WIIFM arrives the reader is "depressed on the inside". Tease the problem, relate, say you know the fix, then go deeper into the problem.
6. The source warns this is not a natural speaking pattern and takes practice.

### 3.4 HSO: Hook, Story, Offer [4a2f08fa92d1] [f6de27013260]
1. Hook: an attention line or question ("What date will you be debt free?").
2. Story: your own experience, not "here's how you can" (which invites "you don't know my situation"). Hit 2 beats: why you are convinced; that you understand their problem better than they can describe it ("When you can describe their situation better than they can, they will naturally look to you for the solution").
3. Be hyper-specific: not "I had a lot of bills"; instead "a missed payment here, a late payment there, borrowing money every once in a while, until one bad thing happened and all my bills piled up at once".
4. End the story with the lesson ("If you don't know what day you'll be debt free, you might never be").
5. Hook the lesson into the offer ("If you're someone like me... the best thing you can do is commit to figuring out your finances, and that's what we specialise in").

### 3.5 Story-with-delayed-reveal promo email (worked example) [13f8ab113b12] [60835ecfe3d0]
1. Subject: "Why I sold my residential properties (and after hearing this you might too)".
2. Relate to the desired outcomes (freedom, passive income, tax benefits).
3. Big challenge: "I soon came to realise real estate provides almost none of those things, at least if you do it how everyone else teaches it."
4. Concrete pain: 2 a.m. calls about broken toilets, tenants dodging rent, the stress of finding deals.
5. Your experience before the reveal: "I tried something new and instantly it was like a breath of fresh air." Do not name it in the next sentence. Describe the experience and why it is better, then reveal it. Do not reveal first and praise after.
6. Proof (results), one CTA ("click here to join").
7. Close with situational urgency shown as your conviction ("I'm so glad I went all in while there's still so much opportunity... in a few years it probably won't be as accessible").
8. End stories with a main lesson; write like a friend talking to a friend, not an English exam.

### 3.6 Edit checklist (from the live critiques) [977fa0fc63ca] [f614c8ab3ac7] [7de71d52b34a] [610c9fac80ba] [0666118ff844] [28321b2f379b]
Run structure first, then language. The source's order of priority: things that block action first (ambiguous subject, several CTAs, filler).
1. Subject line: is it ambiguous? ("My boss fired me and helped me" reads as the boss helping after firing; say "My boss firing me was the best thing that ever happened", then shorten.) Is it tied to the email's angle? ("Shoot the gun" with no context confuses.) If the body has a great line ("I got fired on my first day"), move it into the subject. Preview text can rescue a weak subject. Aim for 40 characters or fewer. [134e00efad90] [0622a180b998]
2. Do not refer back to the subject line inside the body; readers may have forgotten it. [134e00efad90]
3. Lead: cut it. The source says his first note on about 80% of critiques is "cut stuff in the beginning". The opening is light: a hook that sets tone and context, not a haymaker packed with dream state and pain. Opening sentence about 14 words maximum, ideally 7 to 10 (check with Google Docs Tools > Word count [verify UI]). First sentence is a short statement, not a run-on over 3 lines. Ask "is this lead-worthy?"
4. Replace throat-clearing with a perplexing question: not "I hope all the value I have provided has not been collecting dust" (4 lines saying nothing), but "How come some people start Amazon FBA, struggle for 6 to 12 months and never make money, while others...?" [f0259cdd1faf]
5. Cut "what if I told you". Do not reuse the first name if it appeared 2 lines ago. Cut over-specific details early ("teaching 6 to 9 year old kids").
6. WIIFM: is there one near the top? A specific benefit beats a generic one ("shave 6 to 12 months off your journey" beats "make money").
7. One email, one idea, one CTA. Cut sections that are "a whole other email". Too much objection handling or too many ideas makes an email feel long even at 288 words. [b1fc36d11cab] [cab8c603e36c]
8. Make vague promises tangible: not "the 3 key lessons you need to take control of your financial future" but "an outreach method I used to close a $1,500 client up front... the exact emails that made him $50,000 in one month, broken down line by line". [177dbbe025fd] [744657e341ac]
9. Word economy: "People ask me all the time, hey Justin... and I look at them and I'm like" becomes "People ask why I'm successful. I tell them." "A lot of copywriters are always looking for that brand new super promising outreach method" becomes "Copywriters love new outreach methods" (5 words). "Most people can't stick to their workouts" replaces "It's always surprising to me to see almost 90% of people can't stick with their training plan after their first month."
10. Cut "very" and "really" ("very bright future" = "bright future"); "no always or nevers in copywriting, but you pretty much never want" them. Replace "almost" and "barely" with a real number ("had 200", "had less than 200"). Remove doubled words ("SMMA agency" says agency twice).
11. Fix unnatural phrasing: write it how you would say it to a friend ("they started worrying about their finances more", not "they no longer avoid their financial health"). Remove literal-sounding phrases ("cut off their belly fat"). Remove repeated sentences.
12. Keep bold "big lesson" lines tweet-sized, about 7 to 8 words. End lessons with a tweetable line ("You make progress doing 1 thing 100 times, not 100 things 1 time").
13. Keep bullets short; long bullets defeat the purpose. Do not centre small body text. [28ee2e3aa4ff]
14. Remove AI tells: "I hope this message finds you well", "take your business to the next level", "revolutionize", "don't miss out", magic-wand and baking-a-cake analogies, the double dash he calls "very ChatGPT", repeated stock phrases. [5f65d6e25e3c] [28ee2e3aa4ff]
15. Don't name the scary commitment; say how it helps ("a 12-week 1-to-1 coaching program" sounds costly; describe sitting down with a professional and what changes). "Great marketing shouldn't feel like marketing." [e9741d568a97]
16. Preface objections so skimmers who fit do not leave: "Most people feel like investing is only effective if you start early..." rather than stating the objection as fact.
17. Do not write "I don't want to waste any more of your time" (implies you already did).
18. Check pacing at a glance before reading: long lines, long CTA, long P.S. mean add short one-liners and a short "scroll stopper" line ("Let's face it.") where skimmers will stop. [ce4adcb0c5bf]
19. Grammar and redundancy: run through ChatGPT or the Hemingway app ("make this simpler, shorter, easier to understand"), then read it aloud yourself. [7de71d52b34a] [28321b2f379b]
20. Emojis: 1 or 2 in an email, more in an Instagram caption; use them to convey emotion or point at the single main lesson; match the persona. [d65a7b03c6a3] [134e00efad90]

### 3.7 Belief-shift sequence [68cf28c41359]
1. Name the limiting belief in the reader's words ("I can't lose weight because I hate being hungry").
2. Challenge it ("Starving yourself isn't the answer").
3. Sympathise ("Most people are told fewer calories equals less weight, but they've been lied to").
4. Prove the old belief wrong with a concrete failure mode (hunger leads to binge cycles).
5. Prove the new belief ("Losing weight is about strategic ways to stay full while eating fewer calories"), with proof (side-by-side photos and macros).
6. Sell from the new belief ("If you want a meal plan built around your time and taste, click here").

### 3.8 Choose proof (6 types) [574fd7aa2de4] [2b2ec22dc668]
1. Analogies ("building a business is like growing a garden: tools, patience, care").
2. Quotes (Steve Jobs: "Innovation distinguishes between a leader and a follower").
3. Statistics ("80% of new businesses fail within the first 5 years", which he calls real) [web check: this statistic].
4. Cost narration ("I've spent 5 years and $10,000 researching the best fitness routines to develop this program").
5. Hypothetical examples ("Would you rather have a 90% chance of growing year over year or a 3% chance to double revenue?"; later "50% chance of $1 million vs 0.1% chance of $2 million").
6. Borrowed authority ("According to Harvard Business Review, companies that invest in employee training see a 24% increase in profit") [web check: HBR figure].
Be a "stat shower", not a claim-maker: a bare claim is translated as "this dude is full of crap"; a claim with proof reads "makes perfect sense". [792856b9a23c]

### 3.9 Hooks, intrigue and drama [ccb66d506164] [0cf0f5e70b0b] [8919626d89c1]
1. Hook study exercise: read the top Reddit posts of the year or all time and ask why they grab you ("My son was diagnosed with something far worse than cancer" tells you what it is worse than, not what it is; "Don't Google yourself: horror story"; "My son's teacher has been giving some strange homework").
2. Intrigue build (worked example): hook ("Imagine doubling your Instagram followers in just 4 months without spending a dime. Here's how I did it."); keep it clear ("First you need to understand the power of engaging content, but not just any content"); add mystery ("There's a special type of post that gets me 7 to 10 times more engagement"); build slowly ("No, it's not reels, carousels or single photos"); end with a tease and the click ("It combines all of them into one... click here").
3. Drama build (worked example): dramatic hook ("I'm quitting copywriting and starting drop shipping. Here's why", with the quote marks signalling it is what others say); build suspense; reveal the twist ("Even I thought of quitting many times, but I'm glad I didn't"); end with a dramatic but truthful promise.
4. Specific hook test: "Add 2 inches to your biceps in just 90 days. Here's how." is specific to who and what; "grow your arms" is vague and not fresh. [d65a7b03c6a3]

### 3.10 Landing page: anatomy and build [a274357484b9] [4b264d3e8066] [4f95db866881] [6ba3eae9a81e]
1. Pick the format by job: VSL plus blocks (video sells, blocks support); TSL (text sales letter) plus blocks; opt-in page (short; capture name and email; picture of the free thing plus a form). One page, one goal, no About or Contact navigation.
2. Above the fold, answer: who is this for, why keep reading, what do I do next.
3. Headline stack (source: how 90% of headlines look): eyebrow (calls out the reader or adds to the big promise: "Attention entrepreneurs"); main headline (the specific big promise); deck (raises perceived ease or effectiveness: "even if you've never done this before", "works in 90 days", a guarantee). Place the eyebrow where it reads as part of the page, not where it looks like a browser tab. [4b264d3e8066] [3e3d5f0de011]
4. Typical VSL page order: headline, video, button, text blocks with scroll stoppers, bullets, testimonials, value stack, button, FAQ ("how 90% of your video sales letters are going to look").
5. Blocks to reuse: "What is X / Why X" (so what: what makes it different and better; usually one of the first blocks); Meet your instructor or founder; Relate to the reader ("X is right for you if you struggle with..., if you're sick of..., if you know the old models are broken..."); feature plus benefit bullets; testimonials (on basically every page); value stack (each item with a value, total value vs today's price); urgency to act now (closes nearly every page; best is situational); FAQ at the very bottom in dropdowns for people who scroll straight down and want bare facts. [4f95db866881] [073d27caa4b0] [c4995809f1dc] [29770c1882af]
6. Never assume readership: ask "if someone scrolls to the middle, what makes them stop?" Use centred, bold, short scroll-stopper lines in text blocks ("Introducing Training Grounds"). [ce4adcb0c5bf]
7. Build (source demo, tool not named in the transcript [verify UI]): drag-and-drop blocks (header, hero, two-column text, text), edit text, fonts and sizes, add a video block and a form block. No coding.
8. Design: use a background rather than blank white or black (his preference; he admits some make millions with plain white). Example: search "4K black and red background", set it as background, lay a black colour over it with reduced opacity so text stays readable. Subtle gradients look higher quality.
9. Steps: find a design you like and copy it; create basic graphic elements; fill out the blocks; add contrast and sections; test every button and action. Connect forms to the email list using the tool's tutorials. [6ba3eae9a81e]
10. Mock a sales page in Google Docs before building (headline, scroll stopper, text, repeat) with placeholder words, formatted to look like a real page. [8574c8647abb]
11. Heatmap check: run the page through an attention or heat-tracking tool (captioned "exposed."; card reads "Expose" [web check: tool name]). Red shows where eyes go. On his example page eyes went to the face and a bare "Start now" button, which nobody clicks because they do not know what is inside. Rebuild so the headline gets the first look, then re-run. [22214793398e] [3b6b0416a183]

### 3.11 VSL: research, formula and structure [f90f21ab88ce] [6194d941ef49] [de12e750842c] [7da6cda41cbe]
Research recipe:
1. Specific avatar call-out; research their fears, obstacles, wants and desires; write out what their day looks like.
2. The urgent problem your product solves.
3. The dream outcome.
4. The step-by-step process to get there with your product.
5. 2 to 3 pillars of the unique mechanism: why your way works, why alternatives are inferior, with analogies.
6. Why you are the credible option (knowledge, results, who you have worked for).
7. How your way saves time, money and relationships (specific benefits).
Common structure:
1. Call out the audience and give them a problem.
2. What most people do and why it does not work, with proof and analogies (throw stones at alternatives).
3. Belief shift to what does work, why it is better, how exactly it works.
4. Proof: borrowed authority, stats.
5. More results: testimonials ("some of the best marketing of all because you're not making any claims").
6. Your credibility: how many helped, how long, cost narration.
7. CTA plus a possible unique selling proposition (USP); what to do next and why now.
How to write one: go to top businesses in the category, study their VSL structure, copy the structure, fill in the recipe with your own facts.
Worked example (coaching, not ecommerce): headline "Watch the video to see the hands-free six-figure marketing funnel we build 100% for you that runs on autopilot and generates 5 to 10 clients every week"; deck "Guaranteed results or we pay you $1,000". Opening: call-out ("Attention online fitness coaches"), big promise with a number ("add an extra $20,000 per month in profit"), avoiding what they hate ("without sending cold DMs or posting endless content"). First CTA (an application form) comes at about 1:00 to 1:30; the rest of the video is for people not yet sold. Offers with guarantees need an application to filter who they accept.

### 3.12 Unique mechanism and three-pillar offer [c598568de7df] [ecb28758fd66] [79bb35cef343] [384b4df664c6]
1. Describe what most people in the category do or teach, in relatable terms.
2. Say why it is inferior (the specific failure it causes).
3. Say why yours is different.
4. Pillar 1: what you do that works best, why it works, a justification, and "basically always" an analogy. Pillar 2: same. Pillar 3: "why you" (what you have helped people achieve) or another reason.
5. Name the mechanism and translate it into a plain payoff (EPOC "afterburn" becomes "even when you're not training you're a fat-burning machine").
6. Use it to audit competitors: if you can pick out their what-most-teach, why-inferior and pillars, you have found the core of their marketing.
7. USP: an offer with risk reversal ("pizza in 30 minutes or it's free"; "if we don't get you X by Y you don't pay", some add "and I'll give you $1,000").

### 3.13 Ecommerce email to an existing list (source's nose-strip critique) [8a4864a0a123] [e9741d568a97] [adc834e923a1]
1. Do funnel research first: find the main link (the store), how people join the list (popup "unlock $10 off", 10% off for email or phone, or past purchase).
2. Assume the reader already knows what the product is; do not open with "what is this?". They are asking "do these actually work, how, and why these rather than others?"
3. Address the 3 buying blockers the source lists (his quick brainstorm, not exhaustive): risk (small packs such as 5-pack increments; a guarantee "send it back and we'll refund"), price (a discount such as 20% off, "what a lot of ecom people do"), effectiveness (how and why it works, what it does differently).
4. Write it as your own "I noticed" experience with specifics (slept 8 hours but tossed and turned, woke hours early, then more energy, broke gym plateaus, friends now use it too).
5. CTA with risk reversal ("give it a try risk-free with a 30-day guarantee; if you don't love them, send them back").
6. Source note: typical ecommerce emails are "click here to get 20% off, a picture of the product, a bit about how it works", not structured value emails. He also says brand-new stores that may not be profitable are poor freelance clients.

### 3.14 Store email system: welcome, sales and deadline, revival [e326516d4bdb] [71a40b459b34] [48a87de9b79b] [520f11f16e90]
1. Welcome sequence. Email 1 is a utility email that depends on how they joined: newsletter (what they will get), lead magnet (the resource plus what is next), purchase (where to access it plus what is next). Later emails: value, entertainment, belief shifts (why alternatives are inferior, why yours is superior), objection handling, CTAs to more value (for example a YouTube video, which he calls cross-pollination and top-of-mind "omnipresence"), soft product links dropped in a normal sentence. Goal: train opens and clicks. Length 3, 5 or 8 emails "doesn't matter all too much". Examples: Jeff Nippard "If you only take one supplement to build muscle, this is it"; Brandon Carter "Make this mindset shift ASAP".
2. Sales or promo and deadline sequence (after the welcome). Recipe: some value, a belief shift, CTA to the product, more about the product, CTA again. Soft sell in a P.S. to make them aware ("P.S. We just launched our new course... if you want to know more, click here") [22a2130d998e]. Hard sell emails (a $7 workshop with feature-plus-benefit bullets) still carry value. Deadline email: motivate the change, state the time left ("only 48 hours left"), more belief shifts, CTA, why, CTA again (double CTA) [3afc3ff48150].
3. Subscriber revival sequence for people with no opens in 30 to 60 days (see 3.16).
4. Cadence sketch (illustration): day 1 welcome, day 3 value, day 5 soft sell, day 8 hard sell; lead temperature goes from cold to hot. [7ef08521112a]
5. Newsletter automation example: emails 1, 4 and 7 days after signup; a purchase can trigger an immediate email. Broadcasts go to the whole list or a segment, now or scheduled. [55bc15e13681]

### 3.15 Build an automation in ActiveCampaign (source demo) [be2cafef8511] [verify UI]
1. Automations > Create an automation. Pick a "recipe" (template; "thousands" available) or start from scratch.
2. Choose a trigger: subscribes to a list, submits a form, opens or reads an email, clicks a link in an email, visits a web page, event recorded, shares an email, tag is added.
3. Add an action: send an email, SMS, notification email, transactional email.
4. Add a Wait step (1 day, 5 days, 2 hours) or an If/Else condition; these are "the top 2 you're going to be using".
5. Example branch: if they opened any email in the last 30 days, send "thanks for clicking"; if not, send "why no click". Or: welcome email; if not opened, resend a welcome; if opened, send a promo.
6. Engagement rule: engaged people are more receptive to promotion; promote less to the unengaged. Openers can skip value and go to soft sell, then hard sell; non-openers get more value first. [7ef08521112a]
7. Keep it simple at first.

### 3.16 Subscriber revival (list hygiene) [5e43074e0a7e]
1. Trigger an automation at about 30 days of no opens (source window: 30 to 60 days).
2. Write a plain goodbye: subject "Final email"; "It's been great... we haven't heard from you in a long time, so this is the last time we'll ask: do you still want our newsletters? If not, we won't send them anymore. P.S. You're welcome back anytime."
3. Remove people who do not engage. Reason given: sender reputation and deliverability (primary inbox vs promotions tab vs spam). His spam-folder clients saw 1% to 3% opens.
4. See section 9: opens are an unreliable trigger for many stores today [web check].

### 3.17 Funnel map and funnel math [70cb58d82d58] [43446f25064d] [1e11308ca301] [ab161338ac21] [be8e1c2afb00] [58af3dd961fe]
1. Map the funnel (Miro, or a "74 funnel swipe file"). Source example: Facebook and YouTube ads plus Instagram and YouTube content, each pointing to the others, all promoting one free email newsletter ("Fitness coaches, if you want to learn how to reach six figures per month, I'll show you how in my free newsletter"). "All of this hinges on the free email newsletter."
2. Newsletter sequence sends to a landing page for a $7 low-ticket product.
3. At checkout, offer a $77 upsell as a checkbox with no new card details ("people are much more likely to say yes if they've already said yes").
4. Upsell buyers go to an $8,000 high-ticket application; decliners get the $7 product in full, and its content promotes the high-ticket offer ("content sales funnel").
5. Compute the 4 metrics (numbers recomputed in code; the source says "hopefully my math was correct"): cost per lead = spend / leads = $100 / 1,530 = about $0.065 (source says "6 cents"); lead conversion rate = buyers / leads = 92 / 1,530 = about 6.0%; AOV = $7 + 15% x $77 = $18.55; revenue = 92 x $18.55 = $1,706.60; ROAS = revenue / spend = about 17x. Source then says 17x means "$10,000 in, $170,000 out", which assumes nothing changes with scale.
6. Real funnel he bought: an ad for "KPI Mastery" ($45); before purchase a popup offered 10% off for an email (so they can re-contact you if you leave: "you forgot to buy"); after purchase, "we have a special gift for you": a $50 "Success Blueprint" upsell. He observes "any clothing business" asks for an email for 5% or 10% off.

### 3.18 AI-assisted copy [57ebdcf883f4] [5f65d6e25e3c]
1. Write the email yourself first.
2. Prompt "Make this email better and really funny" (or "better, funnier, more clever, use alliteration") and lift only lines that beat yours.
3. Analogies: "Give me 10 analogies that are simple and kind of funny for [X]"; refine with "make them shorter", "add some funny ones too", "make them more simple, one-liners", "give me 10 different ones". Outputs he kept: "Better to use one good worm in a small pond than throw a dozen hooks into the ocean and catch seaweed"; "chop one tree 1,000 times in one spot, not 1,000 spots once". Named mechanism ("unicorn trading") also came from ChatGPT.
4. Other prompts: "I'm trying to [goal]. What questions can you ask me to get the best result?"; "Give me one-line analogies that explain how something is as [adjective] as [X]" ("as useless as pedals on a wheelchair", "building a skyscraper in quicksand"); "Give me quotes that explain X" (borrowed authority); "Give me stats that prove why X". Verify every AI quote and statistic before use (card caveat).
5. Wrong way: "Write me an email that..." produced "Transform your business with a hands-free marketing funnel" (plain, 60 to 70 characters, gives the whole email away) and "I hope this message finds you well".
6. Keep prompts simple; do not chase prompt rabbit holes.

### 3.19 Deliverability and sending hygiene (from outreach and email sections) [verify UI] [web check]
1. Send from a warmed-up address with a sending history; a brand-new address sending about 20 a day lands in spam; if you must use a new one, warm it up over time with a warm-up tool (captioned "waves").
2. Use a separate "swipe" email address to sign up to many lists in your niche, download lead magnets and buy genuinely wanted low-ticket products ($7, $27, $57) to see post-purchase flows; good emails become swipes, bad ones show what to do differently. [78b0365368fe]
3. New Instagram accounts sending about 50 DMs a day risk being shut down; start small or use an older account.

### 3.20 Follow-up cadence (outreach, transfers to post-lead nurture only loosely)
1. No reply: one quick bump 3 days later ("just bumping this up in case it got buried"), used once.
2. Still nothing: a new angle 2 to 3 days later, then another angle 2 to 3 days later (serious instead of funny, a different value offer).
3. Still zero reply: move on. A "no" or "we already have someone": circle back after at least 1 month, referencing their new launch or promo. Warm leads who go quiet: keep following up from new angles every few days or weeks. "It's easier to get someone from a maybe to a yes than from a no to a maybe."
4. Breakup email ("this is the last email; I'll take down the video I recorded for you shortly") "gets so many replies" (claim).

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

1. **4-part score.** Check: does the asset have a hook that gets past the "spam filter", easy and intriguing reading, a belief shift, and one clear action? Good: all 4. Bad: a strong hook that leads nowhere (breaks at consumption or persuasion). Fix: rewrite the weakest stage first. [583aa3b7fca0]
2. **Skim test.** Check: scroll the draft without reading. Good: short scroll-stopper lines and a mix of short, medium and long lines; the offer can be rebuilt from bold lines. Bad: a wall of long lines, centred small text, or all one-liners. Fix: add a short line where the eye should stop; left-align body text. [ce4adcb0c5bf] [28ee2e3aa4ff]
3. **Lead test.** Check word count of the first sentence and whether the first 4 lines say anything. Good: 7 to 10 words, a statement or perplexing question. Bad: greetings, "I hope", "what if I told you", a run-on. Fix: cut; move the best line up. [f614c8ab3ac7] [f0259cdd1faf]
4. **Tell vs show count.** Check each result claim. Good: first-person demonstration with proof in the same block. Bad: "you can lose 20 lb in 8 weeks". Fix: rewrite as "here's how I..." or a named customer story. [e9741d568a97] [792856b9a23c]
5. **I/we vs you count.** Good: reader-dominant with a WIIFM near the top. Bad: founder biography that ends in the writer. Fix: add a WIIFM line before the story; cut self-referential CTAs. [51f77f32f63c] [a98bb10fd49f]
6. **Chronology check.** Check whether the story has goal, adversity, turn and lesson at the end. Bad: a timeline of numbers ("went viral, 67k followers; Eva 300 to 1,000 in 3 weeks; click here"). Fix: add the moment of trying something new, the doubt (luck? algorithm?), the repeat proof, lesson last. [e8cdc6cbf78e] [f6de27013260]
7. **CTA count.** Good: 1 action (optionally repeated). Bad: several different asks. Fix: cut to one; move others to other emails. [b1fc36d11cab]
8. **Clicks vs buys.** Check: are clicks up but purchases flat after teaser-style emails? Bad sign: problem-agitate-"click to learn how" with no mechanism or hint. Fix: give a hint of the mechanism or a real curiosity gap. [0cf0f5e70b0b] [needs brand data: click-to-purchase rate by email]
9. **Subject-body match.** Check the subject and preview text against the body angle. Bad: a mystery subject with no link to the content, or a body that re-explains the subject. Fix: rewrite subject from the email's actual angle. [134e00efad90]
10. **Voice match.** Check the draft against the brand's own posts, packaging and support replies. Bad: business-opportunity hype in a calm expert brand; heavy emojis for a serious persona. Fix: use the brand's own phrases from a compile document. [3f95809c1b82] [needs brand data: brand voice samples]
11. **Avatar fit.** Check qualifiers ("if you're 40ish") against list make-up. Bad: a hook that excludes most readers. Fix: broaden or segment. [177dbbe025fd] [needs brand data: list demographics]
12. **Promise honesty.** Check each promise for spam words, fake-sounding claims and platform compliance. Fix: specific, attainable, qualified promises. [f90f84d09846] [017ae2c141f9] [web check: Meta ad policy for the claim type]
13. **Urgency honesty.** Check: is the deadline or stock limit real? Bad: evergreen countdowns, "sells out" on infinite stock. Fix: tie to a real restock, season, batch or price change. [8f116e83a3f3]
14. **Landing page first look.** Use an attention or heatmap tool; good: headline first; bad: a face or an unlabelled "Start now" button draws the eye. Fix: sharpen and reposition the headline; label buttons with what happens next. [22214793398e]
15. **Landing page block audit.** Check for: eyebrow, headline, deck; so-what block; relate-to-reader block; feature plus benefit bullets; testimonials; value stack; urgency; FAQ dropdowns; every button works. [4b264d3e8066] [4f95db866881] [3e3d5f0de011]
16. **Funnel leak check.** Compute cost per lead, lead conversion rate, AOV and ROAS from real data and compare stages. Keep ROAS as period total value / total spend. [43446f25064d] [needs brand data: spend, leads, buyers, order values]
17. **List health.** Check open and click rates against the store's own history; if promos dominate and engagement falls, add value deposits; check inbox placement. [520f11f16e90] [needs brand data: ESP engagement and deliverability reports]
18. **Tracking check before more effort.** If something "isn't working", first ask whether it is tracked; change one variable at a time. [07386e6caf36] [6649b7b1fce5]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Complicated, jargon-heavy copy, including for B2B. [474c5d2ad56a]
2. Long leads that say nothing; cramming dream state and pain into the first lines. [610c9fac80ba]
3. Ambiguous or context-free subject lines; subjects that give the whole email away; subject lines over 40 characters. [134e00efad90] [0622a180b998]
4. Spammy promises and non-compliant claims. [f90f84d09846]
5. Clickbait: a headline the content does not deliver. [ccb66d506164]
6. Giving the punchline too early; revealing the product before describing why it is better. [0cf0f5e70b0b] [13f8ab113b12]
7. Problem-agitate-"click to learn how" with no mechanism or hint: clicks without buyers (vanity metrics). [0cf0f5e70b0b]
8. Telling instead of showing; trying to convince instead of showing your conviction. [e9741d568a97] [60835ecfe3d0]
9. Correcting the reader ("wrong") instead of understanding first. [68cf28c41359]
10. Chronology instead of story; lesson in the middle or at the start; retelling instead of reliving. [f6de27013260] [a3f3d55f4e85]
11. Stories about the writer; CTAs about the writer. [a98bb10fd49f]
12. Multiple CTAs; too many ideas or too much objection handling in one email. [b1fc36d11cab]
13. Vague "3 key lessons" promises ("fairy tale copy"). [177dbbe025fd]
14. Filler, "very", "really", "almost", redundancy, repeated sentences, long bullets, centred small text. [7de71d52b34a] [28ee2e3aa4ff]
15. Excluding most of the list with narrow qualifiers; analogies that exclude readers ("when you immerse yourself in gaming"). [177dbbe025fd]
16. Leading with the hard part (discipline, difficulty) instead of the attractive benefit ("wrap it in sexy bacon"). [88a07c18db1b]
17. Voice mismatch with the brand; too many emojis for the persona; gimmick openers in every email. [3f95809c1b82] [99d753c33b55]
18. Fake scarcity on infinite goods; overusing "first 10 buyers" tactics. [8f116e83a3f3]
19. "Promo promo promo" to a list with no value deposits. [520f11f16e90]
20. Explaining the product to an ecommerce list that already knows it. [8a4864a0a123]
21. Pasting raw ChatGPT copy; AI clichés. [57ebdcf883f4] [5f65d6e25e3c]
22. Assuming readership on landing pages; a vague button as the page's focal point; an eyebrow placed where it looks like a browser tab. [ce4adcb0c5bf] [22214793398e] [3e3d5f0de011]
23. Just "selling the program" ("trust me bro") instead of the mechanism. [384b4df664c6]
24. Not tracking; switching method every day (niche hopping), which ruins the control. [07386e6caf36]
25. Practising by writing copy about copywriting. [977fa0fc63ca]
26. Copying templates word for word ("steal the ideas, not the words"). [42ebacbe34c3] [b245a0ded890]
27. Referring to the subject line inside the email. [134e00efad90]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Truth vs persuasion.** CJN is taught with "is this true or not... you should be able to justify essentially everything" and examples ("squats are terrible", "8 cups of water is unhealthy") that are contestable. This conflicts with the project rule of no invented claims. Use CJN only with claims the product can prove. [930c67d53174]
- **Income and health claims.** Many examples use income claims ("$1,000 a day", "$20,000 a month extra") and health claims (nose strips, "fat-burning machine"). These are regulated on ad platforms [web check: Meta and FTC or ASCI rules for income and health claims].
- **Gender and age generalisations** ("women want fun and time with kids; men want money and supercars"; confrontation works for 18 to 24 year old men) are the presenter's opinion with no data. Test against segment data. [88a07c18db1b]
- **One CTA vs ecommerce practice.** The source's one-CTA rule is for info-product emails. Common ecommerce emails carry product grids and several links; the source itself says typical store emails are "20% off plus a product picture". Treat the rule as "one primary action". Conflict noted.
- **Revival on opens.** A 30 to 60 day no-open trigger is short for many stores, and opens have been unreliable since Apple Mail Privacy Protection (after this source's framing) [web check: current guidance on open-rate reliability and sunset windows]. The card itself warns a recent buyer may simply not need emails. [5e43074e0a7e]
- **200 to 300 words.** A practice and info-product norm. Ecommerce campaign emails are often shorter and image-led; flows like the nose-strip rewrite fit the range. [a81371537582]
- **Statistics.** The email ROI numbers are inconsistent within the source ($36, $42, $44 per $1) and "40 times" is an old third-party figure; all come from Google results without dates. Do not quote to clients without a live check. [fbb591f5fa80] [e8ba196b022f]
- **Proof numbers.** The 25% to 30% vs 5% open drop, 80/20 plain vs entertainment, "90% of the time" show-don't-tell, 14-word leads and 40-character subjects are heuristics, not tests.
- **Funnel math.** The source's arithmetic is partly garbled in the transcript ("0.06", "$150", "$155"); the recomputed figures above are correct for the stated inputs. The "17x means $10,000 in, $170,000 out" line ignores diminishing returns at scale.
- **Internal inconsistency.** Proof weighs "100 lb" in one section, "1,000 lb" in another. Double CTA is both "optional" and a pattern he suggests on nearly every critique. Lesson-at-the-end is required for stories, but the PPAS structure puts the promise early; both can coexist (promise early, lesson late).
- **Thin on how.** No testing method, no sample sizes, no A/B process, no segmentation rules beyond join source, no guidance on SMS, product pages, reviews, shipping, returns, or catalogue-scale ecommerce. VSL guidance is for coaching offers. Landing page build names no tool. Deliverability advice is brief.
- **Card not traceable to the transcript.** Card [b990935f853d] ("Offer products for the problem, not literally the category") cites a "dick skin" bundle and the presenter's "own supplement brand". Neither appears in this source text; do not rely on it as this source's evidence.
- **Card over-reach.** Card [6ba3eae9a81e] says the page builder shown is ActiveCampaign's; the transcript does not name the tool. Card [e326516d4bdb] says "3 to 8 emails"; the source says 3, 5 or 8 all work.
- **Scope.** About 40% of the source (outreach, niches, prospecting, sales calls, pricing, personal brand, mindset) is freelance business advice; kept here only where a rule transfers. Presenter motives: the course is a lead magnet for his paid mentorship and community.
