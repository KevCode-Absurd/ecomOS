## 1. What this source is for

It gives an operator a way to judge any ad, offer or letter as a salesman would, and to prove it with keyed returns before scaling spend. Use it to brief and review copy (headline that hails the right buyer, specific claims, full story, positive tone, service offer), to set up tests (keyed ads, small test markets, cost per customer, payback), and to cut spend that cannot show a return. It is strong on principles and cases, thin on step-by-step method, and silent on every modern channel.

## 2. Principles

### A. What advertising is

1. **The only purpose of advertising is to make sales.** It is "not for general effect", "not to keep your name before the people", and "not primarily to aid your other salesmen". Treat each ad as a salesman: make it justify itself, compare it with other salesmen, figure its cost and result, "accept no excuses which good salesmen do not make". Condition: all advertising, per the text. Evidence: argument, no data. Hopkins calls this chapter the most important in the book. [6ebd6afa8fcc]

2. **Advertising is multiplied salesmanship, so mistakes multiply.** It talks to thousands at once and costs in proportion: "Some people spend $10 per word on an average advertisement." A salesman's mistake may cost little; an advertiser's "may cost a thousand times that much". "A mediocre salesman may affect a small part of your trade. Mediocre advertising affects all of your trade." So be more exacting with ads than with a sales rep. [6ebd6afa8fcc]

3. **The salesman test.** Ask of every element: "Would it help a salesman sell the goods?" and "Would it help me sell them if I met a buyer in person?" Hopkins applies it to slogans, clever conceits, fixed word limits, large type, "queer and unusual" styling and "dressy" ads. If you would not use it face to face, do not rely on it in print. Card caveat: a filter, not a substitute for keyed data. [6ebd6afa8fcc]

4. **Fine writing hurts.** "Literary qualifications have no more to do with it than oratory has with salesmanship." Fine writing and unique style take attention from the subject and "reveal the hook". Any visible attempt to sell "creates corresponding resistance". The best ad men he knows were house-to-house canvassers who "know how to use words that convince". Write briefly, clearly and convincingly. [6ebd6afa8fcc]

5. **Do not entertain, boast or show off.** "Ads are not written to entertain." Those drawn by entertainment "are little likely to be the people whom you want." "Money spending is a serious matter." "Don't boast, for all people resent it." Writers who "try to be performers" seek applause instead of sales. Hopkins calls this "one of the greatest advertising faults". [6ebd6afa8fcc] [60c2c5bb44cc]

6. **Write to one typical buyer, not the mass.** "Don't think of people in the mass. That gives you a blurred view." Picture a typical man or woman likely to want the product, whose attention your headline has already gained, and do what a good salesman would do "with a half-sold person before him." [6ebd6afa8fcc]

7. **The maker is the wrong judge of customers.** The maker knows manufacturing and the dealer side, but "this very knowledge often leads him astray"; "His interests are not in their interests." The ad man places himself in the buyer's position "to the exclusion of everything else". Top cause of failure: "trying to sell people what they do not want". Second: lack of true salesmanship, with ads "written to please the seller". [76127c3b3070]

8. **People are selfish. Offer service, not a request to buy.** "They care nothing about your interests or profit." "The best ads ask no one to buy." They often quote no price and do not say dealers stock it. They offer wanted information, advantages to users, a sample, to buy the first package, or goods on approval, "so the customer may prove the claims without any cost or risks." "People can be coaxed but not driven." Condition: stated for all ads; the cases are hard-to-sell lines. [dad9da2fb2e6]

9. **Advertising can be run as a science.** Principles are learned by keyed ads and traced returns, "largely by the use of coupons". "When one method invariably proves best, that method becomes a fixed principle." Final conclusions rest "always" on "cost per customer or cost per dollar of sale", because one ad may bring many worthless replies and another fewer valuable ones. `[text: universal]` for the method; `[era mechanics]` for coupons. [2d66d3646e8c] [243d845edcb1]

10. **Mail order is the model for all advertising.** It is "the severest test"; cost and result show at once; judgment errs "often nine times in ten". A mail-order ad that keeps running is "the best advertising, not theoretical", because it "has resulted from many traced comparisons". "The nearer we get to proved mail order copy the more customers we get for our money." General advertisers depart from it for 1 of 2 reasons: they do not know what mail-order men know, or they deliberately give up returns for pride. The second is allowed ("Most of us can afford to do something for pride"), but "let us know the cost of our pride." `[text: universal]`: "What real difference is there between inducing a customer to order by mail or order from his dealer? ... They should not [differ]." [2ac7ed4c4328] [60c2c5bb44cc]

11. **Individuality in campaigns; no imitation.** "No two advertising campaigns are ever conducted on lines that are identical. Individuality is an essential. Imitation is a reproach." The book leaves out things "which depend on ingenuity". [2d66d3646e8c]

### B. Headlines and appeals

12. **The headline picks out the people you can interest, and only them.** Like calling "Hey there, Bill Jones" in a crowd. "Create a headline which will hail those people only." A blind headline or clever conceit may draw "many times as many" readers, but mostly "impossible subjects", and the real prospects "may never realize that the ad refers to something they may want." Headlines work like news headlines: an article about the most beautiful woman in the city headed "Egyptian Psychology" would never be read by her. `[text: per line]` for which appeal wins; "back of all lie fixed principles." [c1863a8437f7]

13. **People read ads, but only ads that look useful at a glance.** Hopkins has "again and again" seen "20 percent of all the readers of a newspaper cut out a certain coupon." But people "do not read ads for amusement"; a double-page dress ad gets no glance from a man, a shaving cream ad none from a woman. Readers "skip three-fourths of the reading matter which they pay to get." They "will decide at a glance, by your headline or your pictures." Corsets do not interest men and children; cigars do not interest non-smokers; razors do not attract women; rouge does not interest men. "Address the people you seek, and them only." [c1863a8437f7]

14. **Headlines swing returns more than anything else in the ad.** "It is not uncommon for a change in headlines to multiply returns from five or ten times over." Hopkins had keyed returns "on nearly two thousand headlines used on a single product", with nearly identical body copy. The writer "often spends hours on a single headline"; "scores of headlines are discarded". Record the headline with every keyed return. [c1863a8437f7] [fe816803de4e]

15. **Your favorite appeal is rarely the best.** "The appeals we like best will rarely prove best, because we do not know enough people to average up their desires." Soap example: "Keep Clean" is too commonplace; "No animal fat" may not matter; "It floats" might interest; a beauty or complexion headline "might attract many times as many". Car example: a headline about "a good universal joint" may fall flat; "The Sportiest of Sport Bodies" might outpull it "fifty to one". Hypothetical examples, not results. [fe816803de4e]

16. **Run many appeals, weighted by proven pull.** One product "fosters beauty ... prevents disease ... aids daintiness and cleanliness"; tests show which quality most readers seek. "One sort of appeal may bring half the returns of another, yet be important enough to be profitable. We overlook no field that pays." Know "what proportion of our ads should, in the headline, attract any certain class." "If we are using twenty magazines we may use twenty separate ads", because circulations overlap and different people answer different approaches. [fe816803de4e] [c1863a8437f7]

17. **Feature the claim with the widest appeal; bury narrow ones.** A toothpaste may prevent decay and beautify teeth; tests "will probably find" beauty "many times as strong". "The most successful tooth paste advertiser never features tooth troubles in his headlines." A soap may cure eczema and improve complexion; eczema "may appeal to one in a hundred while the beauty claims would appeal to nearly all. To even mention the eczema claims might destroy the beauty claims." Claims that move "say one-fourth" of possible customers "may be featured to advantage in a certain percentage of headlines" and "should probably be included in every advertisement". Decide by traced returns, not guesses. [91b7cb943a89]

18. **Cure sells; prevention does not.** "People will do much to cure trouble, but people in general will do little to prevent it. This has been proved by many disappointments." "A heading which asserts one claim may bring ten times the results of a heading which asserted another." [91b7cb943a89] [e9d818096148]

19. **Audience share drives cost.** Hopkins has "no statistics" on asthma. If a canvass showed 1 in 100 suffer, the advertiser "would need to cover a hundred useless readers to reach one he wants"; his cost per result "might be twenty times as high" as for an article that appeals to 1 in 5. "The cost of advertising largely depends on the percentage of waste circulation." [91b7cb943a89] [e9d818096148]

### C. Specific claims

20. **Specific facts are believed; generalities and superlatives are discounted.** "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." "Best in the world" and "Lowest price in existence" claim only the expected and "are usually damaging": they suggest "a tendency to exaggerate, a careless truth" and make readers "discount all the statements that you make." A specific claim "is either telling the truth or a lie"; "Actual figures are not generally discounted." "The weight of an argument may often be multiplied by making it specific." "If a claim is worth making, make it in the most impressive way." [975c891bc15a]

21. **Exact beats elastic, at the same space.** "More light" leaves doubt; "three and one-third times the light" shows tests. "Prices reduced" makes no mark; "reduced 25 percent" gets full value. "Used the world over" is elastic; "Used by the peoples of 52 nations" worked, and "many others followed". "One statement may take as much room as another, yet a definite statement may be many times as effective." [975c891bc15a]

22. **Tell ordinary process facts nobody else has told.** A brewer described a plate-glass cooling room with filtered air, a white wood-pulp filter, bottles washed 4 times by machine, wells 4,000 feet deep, and 1,018 yeast experiments. "All claims were such as any brewer might have made ... But he was the first to tell the people about them." Hopkins calls it the greatest success in beer advertising. Also: "there is nearly always something impressive which others have not told. We must discover it." [975c891bc15a] [7d36e2443edd]

23. **Make impressive claims exact, and prove them first.** A drink "known to have a large food value" was sent to a lab: 425 calories per pint, equal to 6 eggs. "Often chemists are employed to prove or disprove doubtful claims." An untrue impressive claim "may prove a boomerang" and "may bar our ads from good mediums". "It is remarkable how often a maker proves wrong on assertions he had made for years." In scientific lines "a censor is appointed" and "an authority passes on every advertisement". [6959d40e0dc8] [91b7cb943a89]

24. **Stated as fact means it must be fact.** The $100,000 formula line is stated "as a fact". The card adds that inventing such a figure is a false claim. [652fd908ec22] [975c891bc15a]

### D. Psychology and offers

`[text: universal]` for the psychology itself: "Human nature is perpetual. In most respects it is the same today as in the time of Caesar. So the principles of psychology are fixed and enduring. You will never need to unlearn what you learn about them." But Hopkins also says "There are endless phases" and "An identical offer made in a different way may bring multiplied returns"; note each winning method "for use when occasion offers". The offer mechanics below are `[era mechanics]`.

25. **Curiosity is "one of the strongest human incentives."** Puffed Wheat and Puffed Rice "were failures before that factor was discovered". Lines used: "Grains puffed to 8 times the normal size", "Foods shot from guns", "125 million steam explosions caused in every kernel". [652fd908ec22]

26. **Cheapness is a weak appeal.** People "want bargains but not cheapness". "They want to feel that they can afford to eat and have and wear the best. Treat them as if they could not and they resent your attitude." Hopkins frames this as "Americans are extravagant", a national and period generalization. [652fd908ec22]

27. **People judge largely by price.** "They are not experts." A British National Gallery painting listed at $750,000 was passed by until people read its cost, then they "surround it". A department store's $1,000 Easter hat drew more women than the floor could hold. See also principle 45 on price as inducement. [652fd908ec22]

28. **A neighbor's warrant beats a distant guarantee.** Guarantees are "so commonly sold that guarantees have ceased to be impressive." One concern "made a fortune" with a dealer's signed warrant: the local dealer promised in writing to refund on request. "It has always proved effective." [652fd908ec22] [abac911ba2b0]

29. **Pay-later beats money-back.** "Try it for a week. If you don't like it we'll return your money" was beaten by "Pay in a week if you like them", which "proved many times more impressive." The horse story: 2 equal offers; the one that said "Come and pay me then" won. Cigars, typewriters, washing machines and books were sent on approval; "people are honest. The losses are very small." `[era mechanics]` [652fd908ec22]

30. **Personal ownership moves people.** Adding the buyer's name in gilt on each book, "with scarcely another change in the ads", sold "some hundreds of thousands of books". A letter saying a leather-covered book with the man's name on it was waiting, sent on request with an information form, got "nearly all men" to reply and give selling data. Generic small gifts "get very small results". [652fd908ec22]

31. **Offers limited to a class beat general offers.** Examples: war veterans, members of a lodge or sect, executives. "Those who are entitled to any seeming advantage will go a long way not to lose that advantage." [652fd908ec22]

32. **Invite comparison instead of warning against substitutes.** "Look out for substitutes" and "Be sure you get this brand" had "no effect"; they were "selfish appeals". "Try our rivals' too", in the headline, "corrected the situation": buyers insisted on the brand whose maker could court a trial of the rest. [652fd908ec22]

33. **Free given away cheapens; paid-for trial respects.** 2 near-identical food products each offered a full-size package. One gave it free and "failed", even losing trade he had. The other's coupon was good at any store; the maker paid retail for the package. He succeeded. "It is hard to pay for an article which has once been free." "An article good enough for the maker to buy is good enough for the user to buy." See section 9 for the tension with the "Free" advice in the samples chapter. [dad9da2fb2e6]

34. **A requested sample is judged kindly; an unrequested one is not.** Hand an unwanted product to a housewife "and she pays it slight respect". If she asks after reading your story, "She knows your claims ... And she expects to find the qualities you told." Mental impression: show 5 people 5 identical articles and they choose differently; "point out in one some qualities to notice" and all 5 choose it. [dad9da2fb2e6] [0e72ef7fa48e]

35. **A trusted persona can outsell a bare business offer.** 2 side-by-side instalment clothing sellers to poor girls. One made the bare offer. The other put a "motherly, dignified, capable woman" in charge, used her name and picture, had her sign all ads and letters, and wrote "like a friend". The rival "had to quit". [abac911ba2b0] [b5c60126fc0a]

36. **Offer credit through a recommendation, not as a bare offer.** "Offering long-time credit often seems like a reflection." The same backers wrote to good-paying customers: a named customer "tells us that you are one of her good customers ... So we have opened with you a credit account ... Pay nothing in advance." "That was flattering." Mass catalogs "sent out promiscuously did not pay." [abac911ba2b0]

37. **Service-first selling in person.** A brush maker's roughly 2,000 canvassers open with "I was sent here to give you a brush ... take your choice"; the housewife picks others and wants to reciprocate. A coffee seller in about 500 cities leaves half a pound, returns without asking for an order, and credits 5 cents per pound bought toward a kitchen utensil. An electric sewing-machine motor maker offered a 1-week home trial through any dealer with a man to demonstrate: "about nine in ten of the trials led to sales." Cigar makers: "Smoke ten, then keep them or return them." "Practically all merchandise sold by mail is sent subject to return." [dad9da2fb2e6]

### E. Full story and length

38. **Tell a reasonably complete story in every ad.** Learn the pulling claims by tests; "Gradually they accumulate a list of claims important enough to use. All those claims appear in every ad thereafter." "Omit any one and a certain percentage will lose the fact which might convince." When you have a person's attention, "then is the time to accomplish all you can ever hope with him." [8946e9e66b01]

39. **No serials, no one-claim-at-a-time.** "There is no greater folly. Those serials almost never connect." People rarely read successive ads on one line; "In one reading of an advertisement one decides for or against a proposition." [8946e9e66b01]

40. **Write only for new customers.** "People using your product are not going to read your ads." You could tell present users "month after month" that their product "is poison, and they would never know it." "Never waste one line of your space to say something to present users, unless you can say it in your headlines." Accept that the complete story looks monotonous to those who read every ad. Perfected mail-order ads "run year after year without diminishing returns". [8946e9e66b01]

41. **"The more you tell the more you sell."** Hopkins: "it has never failed to prove out so in any test we know." "Note that brief ads are never keyed. Note that every traced ad tells a complete story, though it takes columns to tell." Length follows the product: one sentence may do for chewing gum or Cream of Wheat; "whether long or short, an advertising story should be reasonably complete." A car buyer who leaned to Rolls-Royce read "columns and books" on the Marmon because its rivals' ads were short, and bought it; later he learned facts about a car at nearly 3 times the price that would have sold him. "A man interested enough to buy a car will read a volume about it if the volume is interesting." Switching a woman's breakfast food, toothpaste or soap is hard; try it at her door and you "won't argue for brief advertisements." `[text: universal]` as a fixed rule ("There is a fixed rule on this subject of brevity"). [8946e9e66b01] [243d845edcb1]

42. **Bigger space pays only if filled.** "An ad twice larger brings twice the returns. A four times larger ad brings four times the returns, and usually some in addition." Only "when the larger space is utilized as well as the small". "Set half-page copy in a page space and you double the cost in returns." `[era mechanics]` (print space). [2ac7ed4c4328]

### F. Design, type and pictures

43. **Mail-order form: small type, no waste, no palaver, a coupon.** Mail-order ads are set in small type, "usually ... smaller type than ordinary print", which "proves conclusively that larger type does not pay". "People read all they care to read in 8-point type"; bigger type "is like loud conversation" and "multiplies the cost of your story." No waste space; borders are rarely used. "No palaver ... no boasting, save of superservice ... no attempt at entertainment." The coupon is a cut-out reminder: "A large percentage of people who read an ad and decide to act will forget that decision in five minutes." `[era mechanics]` for type sizes and coupons. [2ac7ed4c4328]

44. **Pictures must out-sell the same space in type.** "From one-third to one-half of an advertising campaign is often staked on the power of the pictures", in art cost and space cost. Do not use pictures "merely because they are interesting. Or to attract attention. Or to decorate an ad." "Use them only when they form a better selling argument than the same amount of space set in type." In mail order, pictures "are salesmen in themselves", sized by importance: a dress being sold may get much space. Pictures in untraced advertising "probably result in whims." `[text: per line]`: "Generalities cannot be applied to art ... Each line must be studied by itself." [e4b23d73d580] [66ff32f28a57]

45. **Where pictures do sell, per the text.** Arrow Collars and most clothing: picturing the product and "men whom others envy, in surroundings which others covet." Correspondence schools (traced advertising): men in high positions or taking upward steps. Beauty articles: beautiful, admired women, with "a great advantage in including a fascinated man". Puffed Grains: pictures of the grains beat figure drawings because they "awake curiosity". "Other pictures form a total loss." [66ff32f28a57]

46. **No eccentric or frivolous pictures.** "People do not patronize a clown." "There are two things about which men should not joke. One is business, one is home." An eccentric picture "takes attention from your subject"; your main appeal lies in the headline, "Over-shadow that and you kill it." "Be normal in everything you do when you are seeking confidence and conviction." [66ff32f28a57]

47. **Art cost, repetition and color are minor questions.** Fine art vs ordinary is "answered both ways": some pay up to $2,000 a drawing because space costs far more; mail-order men use cheap art. "Certainly good art pays as well as mediocre." "The probability is that repetition is an economy": new customers probably do not remember a picture, and if they do, repetition "does not detract". Color does not pay "generally, according to the evidence we have gathered to date", except where it nearly exhibits the product (food dishes, oranges, desserts). "These are minor questions ... Some things you do may cut all your results in two. Other things can be done which multiply those results." [66ff32f28a57] [9310a205f3f3]

### G. Research and strategy

48. **Get full information before writing; one fact can be the keystone.** Hopkins read "medical and otherwise" on coffee for weeks. 1 article "out of a thousand" gave the key: caffeine stimulation comes 2 hours after drinking, so removing caffeine does not remove the kick, and caffeine is tasteless and odorless. For a toothpaste, "volumes of scientific matter dry as dust" held the idea that "helped make millions". "Genius is the art of taking pains." [6959d40e0dc8]

49. **Research consumers and dealers at scale.** Cases: 130 men interviewing all classes of consumers for weeks before a food campaign; letters to 12,000 physicians; questionnaires to "tens of thousands"; a "$25,000-a-year man" going farm to farm for weeks before advertising acetylene gas outfits; another on a tractor; 1,000 men asked what they most wanted in shaving soap; "some thousand of homes" canvassed for pork and beans. Some ad men sell house to house before writing; one spent weeks on 1 article; "It is quite customary to interview hundreds of possible customers." "Guesswork is very expensive." [76127c3b3070] [6959d40e0dc8]

50. **Find who the real rival is.** The pork and beans canvass found only 4 percent used canned beans; 96 percent baked at home. All prior advertising said "Buy my brand", which reached 4 percent. The right appeal was to win people from home-baked beans. It "proved a great success". [6959d40e0dc8]

51. **Know the rivals and the numbers.** Write to every rival for literature and claims; use clipping bureaus; route every consumer and dealer comment to the writer. Learn what a user spends a year ("else we shall not know if users are worth the cost of getting"), total consumption ("else we may overspend"), the percentage of readers the product appeals to, by class (farms vs cities). [6959d40e0dc8] [e9d818096148] [7d36e2443edd]

52. **Strategy questions to settle before a campaign.** "Advertising is much like war, minus the venom." Hopkins lists: name, price, competition, substitution, dealer attitude, distribution. "One oversight may cost the client millions." "Things done in one way may be twice as easy, half as costly, as when done another way." He calls these "samples of the problems", not a complete method. [4afe300cc039] [7d36e2443edd]

53. **Competition questions.** "What are the forces against you? What have they in price or quality or claims to weigh against your appeal? What have you to win trade against them? What have you to hold trade against them when you get it? How strongly are your rivals entrenched?" Fields where a brand created and typifies a habit are "almost impregnable"; they are invaded only "through some convincing advantage, or through very superior salesmanship-in-print." [7d36e2443edd]

54. **Win one before thousands.** For a new shaving soap, nearly every prospect uses a rival and many are "wedded to it". Picture "a man on a Pullman ... using his favorite soap. What could you say to him in person to get him to change to yours? We cannot go after thousands of men until we learn how to win one." A maker with "no distinctions" still needs "a seeming advantage. People don't quit habits without reason." [7d36e2443edd]

55. **Price is strategy.** "A high price creates resistance. It tends to limit one's field." "The greatest profits are made on great volume at small profit" (Campbell's Soups, Palmolive Soap, Karo Syrup, Ford cars). "A price which appeals only to, say 10 percent, multiplies the cost of selling." But small-consumption lines need high margin: "One hardly cares what he pays for a corn remedy because he uses little." On lines "judged largely by price", a higher price "may even be an inducement". `[text: per line]` [4afe300cc039]

### H. What is too costly

56. **Changing habits is very expensive.** Selling shaving soap to Russian peasants would first require changing their beard-wearing habits. A dentifrice advertiser's tests put the cost of converting people to tooth brushing at $20 to $25 each, partly because much of the advertising reached people already converted; "One might not in a lifetime get it back in sales." The maker who learned this devotes "not one line in any ad" to it. A rival who educates on tooth brushing shares the new business with competitors. Money spent to educate people to eat oatmeal gave results "too small to discover". [50fbb6ee4040]

57. **What fails at scale fails small.** "What cannot be done on a large scale profitably can not be done on a small scale." Giving a share of each ad to an impossible object "is only the same folly on a smaller scale." [50fbb6ee4040] [9310a205f3f3]

58. **Category growth needs cooperation; otherwise ride trends.** One orange or raisin grower could not raise consumption ("The cost might be a thousand times his share"), but thousands combined have. No advertiser can afford to educate people on vitamins or germicides; authorities do that in unpaid space. "Great successes have been made by going to people already educated and satisfying their created wants": watch a popular trend and "at the right time offer to satisfy those desires" (yeasts, antiseptics). [50fbb6ee4040]

59. **Low consumption or single sale can kill the economics.** A Dakin-fluid germicide might sell to half the homes, but a small bottle might last years; customers "might cost $1.50 each" and revenue per customer "might not in ten years repay the cost of getting." "Mail order sales on single articles, however popular, rarely cost less than $42.50 each"; dealer sales of like articles probably cost about as much. "Those facts must be considered on any one-sale article." [e9d818096148]

### I. Samples, distribution and dealers

`[era mechanics]` throughout: coupons, stamps, local stores, dealers, jobbers, Sunday papers. Hopkins frames the distribution plans as "the best plans yet discovered for the lines to which they apply. Other lines require different methods." `[text: per line]`

60. **The product should be its own best salesman.** "Not the product alone, but the product plus a mental impression, and atmosphere, which you place around it." Samples, "however expensive ... usually form the cheapest selling method." They apply to "almost every thing"; Hopkins has sampled clothing and phonograph records. [0e72ef7fa48e]

61. **What samples do.** They let you use the word "Free", which "often multiplies readers"; tests show samples may pay for themselves "several times over" through extra readers at no extra space cost. They get action: the reader not ready to buy cuts a coupon. They give you the name and address of an interested prospect to start, inform and follow up. They key the ads. They let you refer people to stocked stores before general distribution. [0e72ef7fa48e] [aa1335efee33]

62. **Do not charge for samples or offer part-price.** Asking 10 cents or a stamp or two "may cost them from 40 cents to $1" extra per reply, retards replies, and bars the word "Free". "You buy one package, we will buy the other" or a coupon for part of the price: "Any keyed returns will clearly prove that such offers do not pay." "Before a prospect is converted, it is approximately as hard to get half price for your article as to get the full price." "Don't ask your prospects to pay for your selling efforts. Three in four will refuse to pay, perhaps nine in ten." Card 0e72ef7fa48e words this as "price at full-size package rather than half-price", which is not what the text says; the text says give it free rather than at part price. [0e72ef7fa48e]

63. **Invest in a warm inquiry.** "It may cost you 50 cents to get the person to the point of writing for a sample. Don't stop at 15 cents additional to make that interest valuable." When a small sample is not a fair test, send an order on the dealer for a full-size package or make the coupon good for one at the store. [0e72ef7fa48e]

64. **Give samples only to people who asked, after reading your story.** "Give them only to people who exhibit that interest by some effort. Give them only to people whom you have told your story." Doorstep samples ("like waifs on the doorsteps") "probably never pay"; many never reach the housewife; "The product is cheapened." Store demonstrations and bulk samples handed to dealers "to be handed out as they will" are the same waste. [0e72ef7fa48e] [aa1335efee33]

65. **Make response easy.** "Asking people to mail the coupon brings minimum returns. Often four times as many will present that coupon for a sample at the store." One line: 70 cents per inquiry by mail vs 18 to 22 cents when presented at a local store. "Most people will pay carfare to get a sample rather than two cents postage." Offered mail, phone or store: 70 percent came by telephone. `[era mechanics]` [aa1335efee33]

66. **Control sample leakage cheaply.** "Only one sample to a home" stops most repeats; those who cheat "are not generally the people who would buy." Checking repeaters on free full-size packages (10 to 50 cents each) cost more than the loss. "Adults only" on the coupon keeps children out. Full-size-package coupons can be bought up in bulk: do not announce the date; use Sunday papers. Have dealers send coupons back promptly so you follow up while interest is fresh. [aa1335efee33]

67. **Judge samples by cost per customer.** Samples "sometimes seem to double advertising cost" and "often cost more than the advertising", yet "rightly used, they almost invariably form the cheapest way to get customers." Arguments against them "are usually biased" (agents who want all money spent in print). "Try some towns with them, some without." [0e72ef7fa48e]

68. **Do not advertise before distribution.** "A venture cannot be profitable if nine in ten of the converts fail to find the goods." Forcing stock by repeated demand "may be enormously expensive"; a national sales force "usually impossible"; dealers distrust promises of advertising. Methods named: sell direct by mail until demand forces dealers; refer sample inquirers to stocked dealers; get dealers to stock under guarantee of sale; consign to jobbers; name stocked dealers in ads. [01a4c889b9f9]

69. **Free-package coupons force distribution fastest (for foods and proprietaries).** Mail dealers a proof of the coupon ad with the list of magazines and their circulation; each coupon "represents a cash sale at full profit" and "No average dealer will let those coupon customers go elsewhere." Some makers got "reasonable national distribution" in 1 week this way (Palmolive Soap, Puffed Grains). "Many advertisers get national distribution without employing a single salesman." [01a4c889b9f9]

70. **Costlier distribution routes.** Free starter packages to every dealer: better than losing created customers, but "very expensive", since "Those free packages must be sold by advertising." Consignment to retailers: "Many dealers resent it. Collections are difficult." Slow methods let "energetic rivals" get ahead. [01a4c889b9f9]

71. **Do not buy dealer effort; buy customers.** Dealers push their own brands; advertised lines carry thin margins and get price-cut. Loading offers ("one case free in ten") mean "advertising must sell ten percent more to bring you the same return", and dealer effort, if any, "merely swing[s] trade from one store to another." Sales by conviction make permanent customers; "People who buy through casual recommendations do not often stick." Window displays may give 1 dealer "a lion's share" without raising total sales; "In many lines such tests will show that costly displays are worthless." "Your object in all advertising is to buy new customers at a price which pays a profit." [88abb52004b4]

### J. Testing and measurement

`[text: universal]` as method. The book's stated aim is to "set down those laws, and to tell you how to prove them for yourself."

72. **Key everything; judge by cost per customer.** "Scientific advertising is impossible without that. So is safe advertising. So is maximum profit." A 5-dollar article: replies cost 85 cents with one ad, $14.20 with another, and 41 cents average over 2 years with a third. On 250,000 replies a year the gap is huge. Unkeyed advertisers pay "from 2 to 35 times what they need cost." "With[out] a key to guide you, your returns are very apt to cost you twice what they need cost", and "some ads on the same product will cost ten times what others cost." [2d66d3646e8c] [3607962859be] [0e72ef7fa48e]

73. **Trust only traced ads and traced advice.** "Never be guided in any way by ads which are untraced." "Take the opinion of nobody, whom know nothing about his returns." Ads that look crowded or verbose but keep running are probably the best of "scores of traced ads". Many ads not keyed now "were keyed at the beginning" and "won on a small scale before they ever ran on large scale." [243d845edcb1] [bf6767351c10] [b15d6119c147]

74. **A test campaign answers questions better than argument.** "Almost any question can be answered, cheaply, quickly and finally, by a test campaign ... not by arguments around a table. Go to the court of last resort, the buyers of your product." A product may fail because a rival is better liked or cheaper, users cost too much to win, people buy and do not repeat, the article lasts too long, or it appeals to a small percentage. "A project you will laugh at may make a great success. A project you are sure of may fall down." [69712b9b3086] [9310a205f3f3]

75. **Small-scale averages predict large scale (Hopkins's claim).** "When we learn what a thousand customers cost, we know almost exactly what a million will cost ... those averages always hold." Measure cost per customer, sale per customer, profit and loss, and "how soon our cost comes back". "So there are today no advertising disasters piloted by men who know." Card caveats note scale can change results; the text does not. [69712b9b3086] [2ed6cf037222] [b15d6119c147]

76. **Tests settle product, method and agency questions.** Product form: a food maker's planned new form drew near-unanimous disapproval in a few-town coupon test; a later form won 91 percent approval from a few thousand women; each test cost about $1,000. Methods: over 50 plans in 5 years for 1 food advertiser; the last cut selling cost 75 percent, "four times more effective" than the prior best, without interrupting the running campaign. Agencies: a challenger takes a few towns and compares results with the main campaign, "like saying to a salesman, 'Go out for a week and prove yourself.'" [69712b9b3086]

77. **Protect proven winners.** Mr. Mead of the Mead Cycle Company "not for $10,000 would he change a single word in his ads", which were the result of years of comparison. Keep a proven tone and proven pictures (principles 47 and 84). [2ed6cf037222] [2ac7ed4c4328]

78. **Cost everything against result, like a storekeeper.** "Even a storekeeper who inserts a twenty-dollar ad knows whether it pays or not." In a big store "every inch used must the next day justify its cost." An advertiser spending up to $700,000 a year did not know if his advertising paid. Hopkins says others spend "five dollars to do what one dollar might do", getting back "30 percent of their cost when they might get 150 percent." Advertising taken "like some secret tonic which others have endorsed" gets credit when business thrives and blames fate when it fails. [8805cbd7f8d7] [f184a46ef6bc] [3607962859be]

79. **Hopkins's forecast.** "The time is fast coming when men who spend money are going to know what they get ... only competent men can survive." "Small expenditures made on a guess will grow to big ones on a certainty." A forecast, not evidence. [f184a46ef6bc]

### K. Individuality and positive tone

80. **Give the advertiser a becoming style, suited to the audience.** "Being eccentric, being abnormal is not distinction to covet. But doing admirable things in a different way gives one a great advantage." Distinctive "perhaps not in appearance, but in manner and in tone": "rugged and honest" where rugged honesty counts; "a good fellow where choice is a matter of favor"; an authority in other lines. [b5c60126fc0a]

81. **Put a person in the ads.** "That's why we have signed ads sometimes, to give them a personal authority. A man is talking ... not a 'soulless corporation.'" "By making a man famous we make his product famous." "When we claim an improvement, naming the man who made it adds effect." [b5c60126fc0a] [abac911ba2b0]

82. **Never change a tone that won.** Each new writer "gets into the spirit adopted by the advertiser. He plays a part as an actor plays it." "Appearing different every time we meet never builds up confidence." Appeals "must seem to come from the heart, and the same heart always, save where a wrong tack forces a complete change." [b5c60126fc0a]

83. **Never attack a rival.** "To attack a rival is never good advertising ... It is not permitted in the best mediums. It is never good policy. The selfish purpose is apparent." `[era mechanics]` for the media rule. [650b052b209a]

84. **Show the bright side; tell people what to do.** Show beauty, health, the face "as it will appear", pretty teeth, well-dressed people, successful men, "what others wish to be". "Tell people what to do, not what to avoid." "Assume that people will do what you ask": "Send now for this sample", not "Why do you neglect this offer?" "Invite them to follow the crowd." Positive vs negative in a keyed test: "the positive ad out pulls the other four to one, if you have our experience." "Before and after taking" ads "are follies of the past. They never had a place save with the afflicted." [650b052b209a]

### L. Letters and follow-up

85. **Letters follow the same laws as ads.** Letters that get acted on or filed "have a headline which attracted your interest. At a glance they offer something that you want." A buyer spending $50,000,000 a year drops "a score of letters" into the basket in 1 minute, sets 1 aside, and files 1 under "Varnish" for later. [5b710f4ca541]

86. **Test letters before mass mailing.** A magazine publisher, before sending 5 million letters, "may try twenty-five letters, each with a thousand prospects", learns the cost, drops unprofitable plans and uses the best. "A general letter is never used until it proves itself best among many actual returns." Where testing is not possible, base letters "on knowledge gained by tests." [5b710f4ca541] [c47b833d687e]

87. **Follow-ups go to half-made converts and decide the sale.** "Some complete a sale, some forfeit the impression gained." "You know what created that interest. Then follow it up along that line, not on some different argument ... Don't undertake another guess." A 2-cent letter gets no more attention than a 1-cent letter; fine stationery no more than poor. "The whole appeal lies in the matter." [3cdc738f5682] [d6fff31fe51f]

88. **Pay for prompt action.** Offer an inducement "or tell what delay may cost". Many successful selling letters put a limit on the offer: "It expires on a certain date." "Strike while the iron is hot." "You can afford to pay for prompt action rather than lose by delay." Case: hundreds of thousands of women bought 6 packages and sent the trademarks for a premium good for 1 week. `[text: universal]` for the principle, `[text: per line]` for the mechanic: "Rarely can one way be applied to two lines. But the principles are universal." [e48273314166] [e18d0d1f9ad3] [3cdc738f5682]

89. **Give a reason to order from you, not from the other catalogs.** Inquirers "might send for three or four similar catalogs." One mail-order firm enclosed a letter and a personal card: "You are a new customer, and we want to make you welcome ... enclose this card ... you get a gift with order, something you can keep." Old customers got a different reason for the gift. It "aroused curiosity", "gave preference to his catalog", and "paid for itself several times over by bringing larger sales per catalog." [af42faf22ef2]

### M. Names

90. **A name that tells a story aids every ad.** The name is displayed prominently, so "it should aid the advertising." Examples: May Breath, Cream of Wheat ("worth a fortune"), Dutch Cleanser, Cuticura, Dynashine, Minute Tapioca, 3-in-One Oil, Holeproof, Alcorub; in Chapter 12 also Shredded Wheat, Puffed Rice, Spearmint Gum, Palmolive Soap. They can be protected and describe the product. [8b2bcdb87528] [4afe300cc039]

91. **Meaningless coined names pay only after long advertising, and most never do.** Examples: Kodak, Karo, Sapolio, Vaseline, Kotex, Lux, Postum. "They can be protected, and long-continued advertising may give them a meaning ... But the great majority of them never attain status ... It is very doubtful that they justify display." Coined names that came to typify a product: Jell-O, Postum, Victrola, Kodak, some admitted to the dictionary. Vaseline "almost monopolized" a new demand "through wisdom at the start". [8b2bcdb87528] [4afe300cc039]

92. **Ingredient or generic names invite substitution.** Syrup of Figs, Coconut Oil Shampoo, Tar Soap, Palmolive Soap "invite substitution" and keep price in the class of like products. Toasted Corn Flakes and Malted Milk (Horlick's), and Royal Baking Powder, created demand that rivals shared; "Some have lost for their originators four-fifths of the trade they developed." [8b2bcdb87528] [4afe300cc039] [e18d0d1f9ad3]

93. **Patent names expire with the patent.** Castoria, Aspirin and Shredded Wheat Biscuit "have become common property." "It often makes a patent an undesirable protection." [8b2bcdb87528]

94. **No frivolous names for serious products.** "In seeking uniqueness one gets something trivial. And that is a fatal handicap in a serious product." [8b2bcdb87528]

95. **When the product must carry a common name, add a person's name.** "The best auxiliary name is a man's name. It is much better than a coined name, for it shows that some man is proud of his creation." Card note: the gendered wording is the text's; the idea is personal accountability. [38681f6fc896]

96. **Cut display that tells no selling story.** "The service of the product, not the name, is the important thing in advertising. A vast amount of space is wasted in displaying names and pictures which tell no selling story." [b8be418498fd]

## 3. Procedures

The book gives principles and cases, not numbered procedures. The sequences below are assembled only from steps the text states, in the order it implies. Where the text does not say how, the step says so.

### 3.1 Research before writing
1. Read the technical and scientific literature on the product and its category, for weeks if needed, looking for the 1 fact that reframes it (caffeine case). [6959d40e0dc8] `[needs brand data: product specs, ingredient and process facts, test results]`
2. Talk to or sell to buyers in person; interview "hundreds of possible customers", or send questionnaires. Learn which arguments land and "the factors which don't appeal". [76127c3b3070] `[needs brand data: customer interviews, reviews, support logs]`
3. Canvass who uses the category and how (pork and beans: 4 percent canned vs 96 percent home-baked). Decide whether the real rival is another brand or a habit. [6959d40e0dc8]
4. Canvass dealers. Collect every rival's literature and claims; clip everything printed on the subject. [6959d40e0dc8] [7d36e2443edd] `[web check: rivals' current ads and claims]`
5. Estimate annual spend per user, total consumption, and the percent of readers the product appeals to, by class. [e9d818096148] `[needs brand data: order frequency, AOV, repeat rate, segment share]`
6. Send doubtful or impressive claims to a lab or chemist; turn true ones into exact figures (425 calories per pint = 6 eggs). Have an authority check every ad in technical lines. [6959d40e0dc8]
7. Answer the 5 competition questions (principle 53) and describe 1 typical rival user; write what you would say to switch that person (Pullman case). [7d36e2443edd]

### 3.2 Write an ad the Hopkins way
1. Fix the typical buyer, a single person likely to want the product. [6ebd6afa8fcc]
2. List every true claim; estimate or test the share of buyers each appeals to. Lead with the widest. Keep cure over prevention and outcome over trouble. Keep a narrow claim out if it could spoil the broad one. [91b7cb943a89] `[needs brand data: keyed results by claim]`
3. Write many headlines (hours each; scores discarded). Each must hail the chosen buyer and show at a glance what they gain. No blind or clever headlines. [c1863a8437f7]
4. Make each claim specific: numbers, test counts, process facts rivals have not told. Drop superlatives. [975c891bc15a]
5. Put every proven claim in the body. Tell a reasonably complete story for the product's weight. Do not serialize. Write for new customers only. [8946e9e66b01]
6. Frame the offer as service: information, sample, trial, approval, paid-later, first package bought by the maker. Do not ask them to pay for your selling effort. [dad9da2fb2e6] [0e72ef7fa48e]
7. Keep the tone positive and in the established personality; show the result, not the problem; no attacks on rivals; no humor about business or home. [650b052b209a] [b5c60126fc0a]
8. Add a way to act now and a reminder of the decision (the coupon's job). [2ac7ed4c4328] [e48273314166]
9. Use a picture only if it out-sells the same space in type. [66ff32f28a57]
10. Run the salesman test on every element (principle 3). Remove what fails unless kept knowingly for pride, with its cost recorded. [6ebd6afa8fcc] [60c2c5bb44cc]
11. Key the ad. [3607962859be]

### 3.3 Headline and appeal testing
1. Run the same body with different headlines, each keyed. [c1863a8437f7]
2. Record the headline beside every keyed return. [c1863a8437f7]
3. Compare on cost per customer or cost per dollar of sale, not reply count. [2d66d3646e8c]
4. Rank appeals. Keep every appeal that pays, even at half the returns of the best. [fe816803de4e]
5. Set the share of ads per appeal by proven pull. Use different ads across overlapping media. [fe816803de4e]
6. When a method "invariably proves best", treat it as fixed for that line and stop retesting it. [2d66d3646e8c]
The text gives no sample size, test length or significance rule. `[needs brand data: traffic and conversion volume per variant]`

### 3.4 Test campaign before scaling
1. Pick 4 or 5 average towns (Hopkins's unit). [69712b9b3086]
2. Use a sample or free-package offer to start users fast. [69712b9b3086]
3. Measure: do users buy after the sample, do they continue, how much do they buy, how long until profit repays selling cost. [69712b9b3086] [e9d818096148]
4. Compute cost per customer, sale per customer, profit or loss, payback time. [69712b9b3086]
5. If cost comes back "before the bills are due", the product can grow without investment. If it takes about 3 months, finance the spread accordingly. [e9d818096148]
6. Scale only after the small test proves safe; keep keying after scale. [b15d6119c147]
7. Keep running tests of new methods in a few towns beside the main campaign. [69712b9b3086]
Hopkins's cost for such a test: $3,000 to $5,000 (1920s), "not all lost". He gives no rule for when to stop a test.

### 3.5 Sample or trial offer
1. Decide if the product can be sampled; Hopkins says almost anything can. [0e72ef7fa48e]
2. Offer it free (no dime, no stamp, no half-price). [0e72ef7fa48e]
3. Require an effort: coupon, phone call or store visit, after the reader has seen the full story. [0e72ef7fa48e] [aa1335efee33]
4. Offer the easiest response routes; local pickup beats mail. [aa1335efee33]
5. Add "one sample to a home" and "adults only" where relevant. [aa1335efee33]
6. If a small sample is not a fair test, use a full-size package through a store coupon. [0e72ef7fa48e]
7. Collect names and follow up while interest is fresh, along the same argument. [0e72ef7fa48e] [3cdc738f5682]
8. Judge by cost per customer; compare towns with and without samples. [0e72ef7fa48e]

### 3.6 Distribution by coupon (foods and proprietaries, 1920s) `[era mechanics]`
1. Start with local advertising, even if magazines suit the article. [01a4c889b9f9]
2. Name stocked dealers in the first ads; offer others inclusion; add names as they stock. [01a4c889b9f9]
3. For national reach, mail every dealer a proof of a free-full-size-package coupon ad plus the list of magazines and circulation, before it runs. [01a4c889b9f9]
4. Route out-of-town inquiries to a local store, not to the mail. [01a4c889b9f9]
5. If only some stores get samples, require a stock purchase (a dozen samples with a dozen packages), then refer all inquiries to stores. [aa1335efee33]

### 3.7 Letters and follow-up
1. Write many letter versions. Give each a headline offering something wanted at a glance. [5b710f4ca541]
2. Send each to a test group (publisher case: 25 letters x 1,000 prospects) before the full send (5 million). [c47b833d687e]
3. Compute cost per result; drop the plan if unprofitable; use the best letter. [c47b833d687e]
4. In follow-ups, continue the argument that created the inquiry. [3cdc738f5682]
5. Add a reason to act now: an inducement, a stated cost of delay, or a real expiry date. [e48273314166]
6. Where buyers compare several sellers, add a personal reason to order from you (the catalog gift card). [af42faf22ef2]

### 3.8 Audit existing spend
1. Is each ad keyed? If not, treat its value as unknown. [3607962859be]
2. Compare it with a proven mail-order-style model; list each departure (big type, empty space, decoration, entertainment, unkeyed picture, brand display) and its cost. [2ac7ed4c4328] [60c2c5bb44cc] [b8be418498fd]
3. For dealer allowances, loading offers and displays, test towns with and without and compare total sales, not store share. [88abb52004b4]
4. Check whether any spend goes to educating a habit or preventing trouble; cut it unless shared across a category. [50fbb6ee4040]
5. Charge each ad line to its product or department and require it to justify itself. [8805cbd7f8d7]

### 3.9 Name choice
1. Classify candidates: story-telling descriptive, meaningless coined, ingredient or generic, patent-bound, frivolous. [8b2bcdb87528]
2. Prefer a name that tells the story or one you can own; avoid generic words rivals can share. [8b2bcdb87528] [4afe300cc039]
3. If the product must use a common name, add a person's name. [38681f6fc896]
4. Check whether the name's rights end with a patent. [8b2bcdb87528] `[web check: trademark availability for the chosen name]`

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

**Overclaims in the text.** Hopkins says small-test averages "always hold", that there are "today no advertising disasters piloted by men who know", and that failures have causes with "nothing to do with the advertising". He shows no data for these. "The more you tell the more you sell ... has never failed ... in any test we know" is also unsupported by numbers. Treat as his claims.

**Internal tensions.**
- Free: the samples chapter says use the word "Free" because it multiplies readers, and Hopkins's agency gave full-size packages free "on numerous lines". The psychology chapter says a free full-size package cheapened a food product and failed, while a maker-paid retail coupon succeeded. The text's own reconciliation is thin: the winning case was still free to the user, framed as the maker buying it. Keep both cases; test the framing. [dad9da2fb2e6] [0e72ef7fa48e]
- Names: Palmolive Soap and Shredded Wheat are praised as story-telling names in Chapter 12. In Chapter 20, Palmolive Soap is listed among ingredient names that "invite substitution", and Shredded Wheat Biscuit among names lost as common property. [4afe300cc039] [8b2bcdb87528]
- Pictures: in mail order, pictures "may form half the cost of selling", yet "none of them uses expensive art work". Consistent if "cost" means space, but the text does not say.
- Rivals: "never attack a rival" vs "Try our rivals' too" is not a conflict: the second invites comparison without criticism.
- Brevity: "fixed rule" on complete stories, yet "One sentence may tell a complete story" for chewing gum. The rule is completeness, not length.

**Text defects.** "Some insist on dressy ads. That is all right to a certain degree, but is quite important" reads like a scan error (likely "not important"). "With a key to guide you, your returns are very apt to cost you twice what they need cost" must mean "without a key". Several words are garbled ("site advantages", "prediction for them").

**Card errors found while reading the full text.**
- e9d818096148 says single-article mail-order sales "rarely cost less than $2.50 each". The text says $42.50.
- 3607962859be says "cost of a dollar doing a five-dollar job". The text says "five dollars to do what one dollar might do".
- 0e72ef7fa48e says "price at full-size package rather than half-price". The text says half-price or part-price trial offers "do not pay" and samples should be free.
- 0e72ef7fa48e says "international or out-of-area circulation". The text says "outside towns".
- dad9da2fb2e6 says the free-vs-paid food package case was aimed at "poorer-class buyers". The text does not say so.
- 652fd908ec22 places the $1,000 Easter hat under the "cheapness" point. The text places it under "people judge largely by price".
- abac911ba2b0's title says "Questionable Persona". The text has no such term.
- Many cards add "For ecommerce:" steps (PDPs, abandoned cart, size guides, checkout bundles, Meta, TikTok, WhatsApp). These are card-writer analogies. The text gives no digital application. Do not cite them as Hopkins's findings.

**Era limits.**
- Media rules: "he can't lie in the best mediums" and "It is not permitted in the best mediums" (attacking rivals) describe 1920s publisher standards. `[web check: current ad-platform and consumer-law rules on comparative claims, "free" offers, health and cure claims, price and profit disclosures, and expiry-date urgency]`
- Coupons, stamps, carfare, dealers, jobbers, Sunday papers, 8-point type, print space and "circulation" are all period mechanics. The text gives no modern equivalent.
- All dollar figures are 1920s. Do not use them as benchmarks.
- "Americans are extravagant" and the gender framing ("Women desire beauty largely because of men", "a man's name") are the text's period generalizations.

**What is missing.**
- No sample sizes, test durations or rules for when a difference is real. Hopkins relies on "thousands" of ads; a small test can mislead. `[needs brand data: volume per test cell]`
- No method for estimating appeal percentages beyond "a canvass might show".
- No guidance on how often to refresh ads beyond "never change" proven ones. He assumes a steady flow of new readers who have not seen the ad.
- No treatment of repeat customers except "sample users repeat ... to some extent" and repeat purchase as a test metric.
- No step-by-step for strategy (name, price, competition): he gives "samples of the problems".
- The single-article $42.50 figure is stated without source or basis.

**Conflicts with common ecommerce advice** (for the Pass 2 merge; the common advice is not from this source).
- Before-and-after and problem-first ads are common in DTC. Hopkins says positive outpulls negative 4 to 1 and before-and-after is a folly, except "with the afflicted". [650b052b209a]
- Creative refresh for fatigue is common. Hopkins says keep proven ads, pictures and tone unchanged for years. [2ed6cf037222] [b5c60126fc0a]
- Entertainment-first and humor-led creative is common. Hopkins says never entertain and never joke about business or home. [6ebd6afa8fcc] [66ff32f28a57]
- First-order percentage discounts and part-price trials are common. Hopkins says part-price trial offers "do not pay" and cheapness is a weak appeal. [0e72ef7fa48e] [652fd908ec22]
- Retention and customer marketing are standard. Hopkins says never spend a line of ad space on present users. His letter and credit cases show he did market to existing customers by letter. [8946e9e66b01]
- Short copy is common advice. Hopkins says brief ads are "never keyed" and more telling sells more. [8946e9e66b01]
- Money-back guarantees are standard. Hopkins says pay-after-trial is "many times more impressive" and common guarantees have "ceased to be impressive". [652fd908ec22]
- Big logo and brand display are common. Hopkins calls display that tells no selling story waste. [b8be418498fd]
- Category creation is a common startup goal. Hopkins says one advertiser should not pay to educate a habit. [50fbb6ee4040]
