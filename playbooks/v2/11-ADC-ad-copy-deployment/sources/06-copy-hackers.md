## 1. What this source is for

It helps an operator build the message before writing any copy: a short messaging strategy, customer research to find pains, motivations and segments, a feature and benefit document, a keyword and competitor audit, and a ranked messaging hierarchy. The output feeds home pages, product pages, landing pages, emails and ad copy. It is strongest on pre-writing research and positioning. It is thin on how to test, how to read results, and anything platform-specific. It was written for software startups, so each rule needs translating to a store.

## 2. Principles

1. **Messages come from customers, not from you.** Condition: every page, tagline and ad, even before naming the company. Every message must be one your ideal visitors want to see. What you or your team like hearing does not matter. Copy that a writer "wordsmiths" from your internal message can excite the team and still fail to convert. The author calls "wordsmithing" an insult and says the reasons you built a product may not match the reasons a customer would choose it. Evidence: author's opinion. [dd20f7934ee1]

2. **Never ask customers to approve your copy.** Condition: applies to reviewing finished copy, not to gathering research. The author says "the moment people are aware of copy, everything changes for them". Evidence: author's opinion, no data. [dd20f7934ee1]

3. **Write a short messaging strategy first; think, then write.** Condition: before any copy or formatting. Formatting (bullets, headlines, buttons) comes after you have messages. A messaging strategy states the principles the copy will follow and sets tone. It gives a reason for each writing choice so writers stop picking words they "like the sound of", which gets worse when busy. It can be 1 paragraph. It is a living document. Evidence: author's practice (Page99Test example in section 7). [20fa6900506f]

4. **Know the real target market; do not assume it.** Condition: before choosing any message. The author's example: a photo-sharing startup may assume its market is early-adopter tech people who upvote its launch post, but research may show the main users are teenage girls, teenage "EMO" boys, new moms and retired women. Messages that work on tech people are unlikely to work on those groups. Ask yourself, "Do I really know who my target customer is?" and be hard on yourself. [6c4ec380b27a]

5. **Pick the segment that visits most and pays you most.** Condition: unless you plan to disrupt a major business (the author names Expedia and the MLS), do not market to a wide range of people. Choose the segment that will visit most often and is most likely to compensate you, with money or with referrals ("referrals/likes" in the next-steps list). "They may not be glamorous and they may not be rich." [6c4ec380b27a] [7a372d0590a0]

6. **The segment shapes the message, the price story and even the offer.** Condition: once the segment is known. Each segment has values and motivations that only a few messages meet, so you cannot pull a random message from "the grab bag". Worked examples (illustrative personas, not benchmarks): [6c4ec380b27a]
   - Young, married stay-at-home moms, no post-secondary education, under $55K household income, $20K credit card debt, high tech-savviness: lead with affordability, steer away from ease (they are at ease with tech). If they use phone apps to keep kids busy in the car, offer a free or low-cost game-style app to win loyalty, perhaps linked front and center on the home page.
   - Middle-aged career-oriented women, low debt, high income, time spent on Skirt.com and Oprah.com: do not talk about affordability. You might adjust pricing and messaging to play into "high prices equal better solutions". Talk about ease only if this segment is not tech-savvy.

7. **A message either targets pain or reflects motivation.** Condition: every core message. The product must address and neutralize or eradicate a critical pain, or pinpoint a key motivation (including values). [fb5fb47770a9]

8. **You cannot create motivation on your site; you can only reflect it, and at best build on it.** Condition: at least the home page, and the primary pages in the user flow. Motivation is the force behind the visit and lives outside the site. Motivations run deep, are the kind of thing only close friends hear, and are often not admitted even to oneself. Reflect them; do not describe them literally. Motivation is created by marketing and PR. [fb5fb47770a9]

9. **Motivation is not a discount, a limited-time offer or your value proposition.** Condition: always. Customers may be price-sensitive, but motivation runs deeper. A value proposition speaks to your unique, desirable solution; it does not speak to the pain the customer feels. Sometimes the value proposition covers motivation, but often you must write separate lines just to reflect it. [fb5fb47770a9] [718d826f9fb7]

10. **Piggyback on motivation others already built.** Condition: when an existing category or big competitor already markets the need. The author's example: a personal finance tool does not need to create motivation, because Intuit, major banks, the TV show Til Debt Do Us Part and the recession (at time of writing) already did. "All you have to do is remind people why they need your solution", for example in a callout below the hero. [fb5fb47770a9] [17b8f4f12a1d]

11. **Back every factual claim.** Condition: any claim of fact. Support can be as simple as a link to the article where the research was first published (her example: Newsweek). [fb5fb47770a9] [19bf368e3817]

12. **Write for 20% to 35% of visitors, not 100%.** Condition: stores and startups that must buy or earn traffic; the author says large companies can afford to be broad "(although still silly)". Real estate agents neutralize a house because they need 1 buyer; you need hundreds, which tempts you to make the site a "beige blob". Trying to please every visitor means saying nothing compelling. Write to your most qualified traffic, "even if that's just 22%". Fairfax Cone taught his writers to write to a single person, not the masses. "If you try to please everyone, you please no one." Evidence: author's argument, illustrative arithmetic (section 4), a 2005 direct mail example, and startup headlines. [52564e4cb23c]

13. **Qualified traffic pays twice.** Condition: after the message fits the segment. Qualified buyers who convert are more likely to stay and to recommend you. Once the message is right for a segment, you can buy more traffic for that segment and expect better ROI than traffic sent to an untargeted page. Evidence: author's claim, no numbers. [52564e4cb23c]

14. **Narrow headlines can widen later.** Condition: a focused start. The author says Reach.ly (hotels only) "can expand that targeted message" as it grows. [52564e4cb23c]

15. **Copy is first about what the user wants, second about how the product meets those wants.** Condition: all copy. Messages must be what customers want to hear and also unique to your product; otherwise you say half-true things or what everyone else says. Unique features are what make people buy yours instead of a competitor's or nothing. [70aaf0b3735c]

16. **A feature's purpose is not its benefit.** Condition: every feature you write about. A feature is a part of the product that does a unique thing (a light switch on a lamp). A benefit is the value the customer gets (you can easily fill a room with light or darken it). Describing what a feature is for is still describing the feature. The benefit says how the buyer will feel and what improves in their life, and it changes by segment. Benefits that tap motivations are powerful. [70aaf0b3735c]

17. **Cut features that have no clear benefit.** Condition: a feature where you must stretch to find a benefit. Do not mention it in copy; the customer does not need to learn about it on your site. [7f5165a57e7a] [70aaf0b3735c]

18. **"How you do what you do" is also a benefit.** Condition: design, values and methods that sit outside any feature. Examples given: using only FSC paper to cut your carbon footprint, a fun cartoon-like look for bookkeeping, donating to 1% for the Planet, using only vegan-certified materials. "Every benefit to using your solution is a benefit worth sharing." Give each its own row. [7f5165a57e7a]

19. **Be specific; do not summarize to fit a space.** Condition: tight word counts (her example: a 6-word space). Choose shorter, tighter words or fewer high-impact words instead of diluting the message. A summary is not noticeable or memorable. "Specifics stick." Apply this in the positioning document too. [d5db0dddac4a]

20. **There is no fixed rule to lead with the benefit.** Condition: choosing the lead (top-priority) message. The author has seen a headline about a new feature beat a headline showing an expected benefit. Some shoppers distrust "soft" benefit language, some jump to comparison charts and catalogues to check features, and some come for one cool feature they heard about; none of them want a benefit lead. Choose by what your customer needs to see when they need to see it. Evidence: author's observation, no data. [d5db0dddac4a]

21. **Pair every feature with a benefit and every benefit with a feature or support point.** Condition: whatever you lead with. Support point example: a snapshot of the interface to back "enjoyable software". [d5db0dddac4a]

22. **Show, don't tell.** Condition: when a visual or the page itself can prove the point without losing clarity. Mint shows clean, easy design through its own web design instead of saying it is design-focused. "Some of the strongest messages do not even appear as text on the page." Each copy chunk should have a visual that either proves the claim (screenshots) or reinforces it (an "it's free" sign). Good writing is good design; copy should guide design. [61d04467e873] [e5294ffc5f69] [20fa6900506f]

23. **Negative framing is not banned; neither side wins by rule.** Condition: choosing positive or negative copy. Most marketing solves pain, which feels negative, yet managers push for positive wording. People are raised to pay most attention to negative phrases ("Don't touch that!" beats "Only touch this"), and people buy to solve a need or scratch an itch. Negative can be powerful when messages must be clear and simplify decisions, but "that doesn't mean negative is always better". Famous winners exist on both sides (Apple "1984" negative; VW "Think Small", 1959, called the top campaign of all time by Ad Age, positive). A hybrid can work (Miller Lite "Tastes great, less filling"). Test to be sure. [19bf368e3817] [2c6f4cda6b12]

24. **Do not paint a negative picture and then attach your brand to it.** Condition: unless your solution is strong enough to lift that weight off completely ("throws it a hundred miles away"). Otherwise the brand gets associated with the bad thing. If it cannot, hint at the negative instead of bringing it and its emotions to the surface. The author says she fully agrees with this argument. [2c6f4cda6b12]

25. **Customer language beats your own wording.** Condition: headlines, subheads, SEO fields, demos, captions, buttons. Keywords people type are "the words of your customers" and "often the exact phrases your customers want to see on your site". She learned this from a search manager whose keywords "sounded like normal language". [936e1b2b613f]

26. **The companies at the top of search for your keywords are your competitors.** Condition: online businesses. Big companies often think they know their competitors and then lose share to a startup (Netflix and Blockbuster). You need to know what competitors say, because prospects have probably already seen those messages. [a3ddc92dd001]

27. **Messaging hierarchy is about where users look, not top-to-bottom order.** Condition: every page layout. The hierarchy ranks messages from most to least important to the visitors you target, starting from the priority column of the positioning document. But you do not dump messages on the page in rank order. Lead with the biggest point for the visitor, then place supporting messages as they are needed ("First comes landing on your site for X reason. Then comes telling them Y. Then comes closing Z deal."). The author does not see "inverted pyramid" and "storytelling" as different enough to separate. [e374cf034894]

28. **Feed information logically so the visitor can decide emotionally.** Condition: every page. Copy reads best when messages are clearly organized, drawing visitors toward the primary call to action ("Learn More", "Buy Now", "Sign Up", "Get Started"), and positioned logically. Decisions are more emotional than logical, but "the only way to help them get emotional is to feed them information in a logical way that prevents them from thinking". [e374cf034894]

29. **The book's "8 small tips" (chapter 8), tips 1 to 6.** Condition: drafting and editing any copy. Evidence: author's opinion plus 2 single cases.
   - Tip 1, overwrite then cut: start with 10x more info than you need. Caples: "Fact-packed messages carry a wallop. Don't be afraid of long copy. If your ad is interesting, people will be hungry for all the copy you can give them. If the ad is dull, short copy won't save it." [753e79e5c3d8]
   - Tip 2, do not give your audience too much credit: people make "100s of 1000s" of decisions a day without thinking and accept things because questioning is exhausting, so you do not need to explain every statement. [10a92744aa71]
   - Tip 3, keep offers simple: 1 concrete condition beats a vague one (Fidelity case, section 7). "Getting succinct and sharp sells." [690303793634]
   - Tip 4, copy works best when readers believe it: a nearly unbelievable line can work, as long-form sales writers know, but never an impossible one, and always back it with clear examples on the page. [6b829c0c6b03]
   - Tip 5, customers want a magic button: people keep believing there are miracle cures, that they deserve them, and that they take little effort and save time; they will buy anything that makes them younger, sexier, more powerful or richer. "Don't take advantage of this." But if the product truly delivers, do not hold back. [6b829c0c6b03]
   - Tip 6, use related news if you can do it "in a non-nasty way" (competitor blunder case, section 7). [d225b0070e78]

30. **Positioning is the most important decision.** Condition: before writing a word. First recognize that you must position the product, then do research: talk to customers, business contacts, anyone who will answer. You can change later, but start right. Examples: Ogilvy & Mather positioned Merrill Lynch as a total financial services centre, not just a stockbroker, and American Express Travelers Cheques for domestic travel, not only foreign. [0e8918a800e5]

31. **Hard writing is a good sign.** Condition: when writing feels easy. Thomas Mann: "A writer is a person for whom writing is more difficult than it is for other people." Easy writing may mean you are not choosing the right word, cutting enough, or positioning for a niche. "Fluff is easy." Note: no card covers this tip. [no card]

## 3. Procedures

### 3.1 Write a messaging strategy [20fa6900506f]
1. Pick your guiding principles. Candidate list from the source: let copy guide design (good writing is good design); some visitors want to read a lot and some do not, and both are customers; support messages with social proof such as testimonials or media reviews; support key concepts with video demos; "just because you say it, doesn't make it true, prove it"; clarity above cleverness; rather than focusing on "consistency" or "matching messages", let the message match the medium at an exact point of need; all visitors can be persuaded to join or upgrade; engage visitors with interesting content, web experiences or mobile/app options.
2. Write the strategy out in 1 sitting. Do not censor yourself. Write as long as you need.
3. Then edit. Do not expect it to be right on the first, second or third try.
4. Use it to judge whether a block of text is working or not.
5. Use it to set copy style, page length and how copy sits with visual assets.
6. Share it with your team and freelancers (for example designers).
7. Update it as tone develops, as you learn customers, and as products change or are added.
Output: 1 paragraph is enough for a startup (example in section 7).

### 3.2 Gather customer intelligence [dd20f7934ee1] [7a372d0590a0] [6c4ec380b27a]
1. Use research you already have (prototype user tests), but aim for what customers "don't even know and couldn't possibly tell you". The benefits the product needs are "woven through every statement they say".
2. Pick 1 or more methods:
   - Email survey to your list (lead gen or pre-launch signups). Ask about them and what interests them in the product. Ask them to do you a "favor" and say "please"; the author says this can raise response rates and asks you to test it.
   - Talk to people in person. Skip the consulting firm focus group. Approach 8 people at a coffee shop; even with $25 gift cards each you save a lot. Conversations stick better than a report.
   - Talk to people online in forums. Listen to what potential customers discuss. Engage only when mutually useful. Do not spam a launch and leave.
   - Buy a market report (Forrester; "seriously expensive").
   - Use a Compete.com PRO subscription and its category lists to flesh out website usage research. [web check: Compete.com status]
   - Survey by phone or in person at a real location.
3. Ask questions that make people reveal themselves: demographics, favorite services and products, motivations.
4. For each candidate market, collect: age group, marital status, number of children, education, annual income, average consumer debt load, favorite websites, favorite pastimes, shopping preference (online vs retail vs print catalogue), tech-savviness. [needs brand data: customer survey, customer database, analytics demographics]
5. After at least 12 people, document everything and analyze it.
6. Do not use the data to validate your assumptions. Look for new information that builds the business, not your ego. Highlight the surprising and the repeated findings.
7. Shortlist segments that meet both conditions: they visit frequently, and they pay you in cash or referrals/likes.
8. Buy PPC ads on Google targeted to each segment and see which segments click through. It costs money; the author calls it money well spent if it finds the single best fit. [verify UI] [web check: which demographic and audience targeting Google Ads still allows]

### 3.3 Find pains [0c27d9b3a340] [fb5fb47770a9]
1. Ask yourself what keeps customers up at night.
2. Run 12 to 15 one-on-one interviews with people from the segment (see numbers table for the claim behind this).
3. Audit competitor sites for the pains they hit on.
4. Use the Google AdWords keyword tool to find phrases your audience searches. [verify UI] [web check: current Google Ads Keyword Planner access]
5. Test PPC ads with different pain points and see which get clicked most.
6. Monitor people in your segment on Twitter and on their blogs for pains they mention.
7. Find out which websites they commonly visit.
8. For each pain, write how your product's benefits address or remove it, and use that in copy.

### 3.4 Find and reflect motivation [fb5fb47770a9] [718d826f9fb7]
1. List the deep motivations of the segment. Source's example list: reclaiming childhood innocence; feeling connected to other people; keeping up with the Joneses; hosting the perfect family Christmas; attracting the person everyone else would want; being the person everyone else would want; never feeling hungry again; finally getting respect from their father or mother; becoming the ultimate lover; guaranteeing their family's financial well-being; protecting loved ones from predators, including disease and bullies; loving what they see in the mirror.
2. Check what visitors say they want against what drives them (Teresa example, section 7).
3. Write a line that reflects the motivation without naming it bluntly.
4. Support it with images, testimonials and case studies (video and print).
5. If the category is already marketed, write a reminder callout below the hero instead of building motivation.
6. Check the copy does these 5 jobs: confirms the solution is what they thought; reminds them they need a solution like yours; keeps them on the page longer and considering the offer; gets them nodding along; moves them to the next part of the experience where you convert them. It will not convert every visitor.
7. For launches, map traffic waves and write for each wave's motivation:
   - Wave 1: beta sign-up brings friends, contacts and family, who join the email list. They want the basics (your mom wants to support you; a jealous ex-coworker wants to pick the idea apart).
   - Wave 2: media covers the beta and sends specific segments (developers from Hacker News, technophiles from TechCrunch), who join the list. They may want more specifics.
   - Wave 3: launch; the email list floods the site, many like it on social networks, niche or foreign press sends mixed traffic.
   - Wave 4: invitations, blog posts and social recommendations bring "Lookie Lous".
   - Waves 3 and 4 want to learn about the solution and the company and to check whether it solves their pain. Once waves 3 and 4 start, copy can reflect more specific motivations.

### 3.5 Revise copy toward a segment [52564e4cb23c] [ab79ca8e0db3]
1. Read existing copy. Ask whether it targets the segments you found or tries to please the majority.
2. At minimum, make headlines more specific.
3. Revise the copy to speak directly to about 35% of visitors (the chapter's next-step wording; the body says 20% to 35%).
4. Watch for pressures that push you broad: deadlines, being too close to the project, fear that investors will not fund something narrow.
5. Once the message fits, buy more traffic for that segment.

### 3.6 Build the product positioning document [7f5165a57e7a] [95690fe6cfee]
1. Make a table with 5 columns: Features | Unique to Us? | Customer Pain Solved | Benefits | Priority.
2. Features: list every single feature. Leave nothing out; a small feature in a bullet list may be what puts you above a competitor for someone. Do not write copy or play with words yet.
3. Unique to Us: check it if only you offer it. If a competitor offers it, list each competitor. If similar, briefly describe how.
4. Customer Pain Solved: from your research, note the pain each feature solves or addresses. With several segments, note which segment.
5. Jot benefits as they occur and group similar features if it helps.
6. Benefits: give every feature at least 1 express benefit. Some have several. Merge cells as needed. Cut features where you have to stretch to find a benefit.
7. Add rows for benefits not tied to a feature ("how you do what you do"), with a note saying so.
8. Priority: rank each feature and benefit row. Any system works (colors, numbers such as 1 = high). The author uses high, medium, low. Not every feature is a gem, and not every benefit compels your segment.
9. Be specific in the benefits column.
10. Make 1 document per product ("each flavor").
11. Reuse it for every page, email and in-product message, and for the messaging hierarchy, value proposition and points of differentiation. The author says it is critical, may be "lengthy, possibly ugly", and is a listing job, not wordsmithing.
[needs brand data: full feature list, competitor feature list, customer pain research]

### 3.7 Keyword research for copy language [936e1b2b613f]
1. Open the Google AdWords keyword tool. Enter at least 1 primary keyword or phrase that fits the product. [verify UI] [web check: tool is now Google Ads Keyword Planner; access rules]
2. Review global and local monthly searches to find more keywords.
3. If a much higher-volume phrase fits, consider a landing page that matches it (Bingo Card Creator example, section 7). Judge fit; the source frames this as that founder's call.
4. Write the keywords down per page.
5. Use them in: H1 headlines and H2 subheads; URLs, meta titles and meta descriptions; demos; captions under screenshots; the second line of multi-line buttons.

### 3.8 Competitor content audit, home page only [a3ddc92dd001] [17b8f4f12a1d]
1. Search each top keyword phrase. Note the top 3 results on the results page, organic and PPC, for each phrase. Those are your competitors.
2. Audit the home pages of at most 10 competitors.
3. Record 5 things per site: (1) value proposition or headline; (2) top messages they are trying to communicate; (3) primary calls to action: wording, visual design, position on the page; (4) special reasons to buy, such as guarantees and assurances that might make a visitor take out a credit card; (5) other standout ideas.
4. Work in a slide deck, 2 slides per site: slide 1 a screen capture of the home page for your records; slide 2 the top messages you see on it.
5. Synthesize in 3 tables:
   - Value proposition table, 3 columns: competitor | value proposition on home page (some have none) | benefits highlighted, in short words such as "security", "ease", "affordability".
   - Top messages table: short message per row | 1 column per competitor with tick marks | total column counting sites that use it.
   - Primary call to action table: same layout, 1 CTA per row, tick marks per competitor, total column.
6. Read totals: common messages are either white noise from overuse (use sparingly or not at all) or a baseline visitors expect to see reflected. The audit cannot tell which.
7. Use it to learn what visitors expect, what may soon be white noise, glaring gaps in messaging, and what tone or style others use or avoid.
8. Use the CTA table to decide above-the-fold CTAs (example: if every competitor has a big demo link, decide on purpose whether you offer one front and center).
9. Do not judge effectiveness yet. Assess and test messages later.
10. Optional: recruit users of a chosen demographic on UserTesting.com to review competitor pages; about $29 per user in 2010, with videos of each user. [web check: current UserTesting pricing]
[needs brand data: keyword list per page; competitor list]

### 3.9 Messaging hierarchy [e374cf034894]
1. Start from the Priority column.
2. Rank messages from most to least important to the target visitor.
3. Decide per page what the visitor needs first (why they landed), then what supports it, then the close.
4. Place key messages where people look, near eye-catching elements.
5. Write the hierarchy down if your team is less confident or if writing sessions are spread out. A small team with a handful of messages may hold it in their heads. The longer the gap between sessions (for example returning weeks later to write new PPC landing pages), the more you need it written.
6. Test: pick a high-traffic page. Are the messages you want seen (strong social proof, a stellar benefit) near buttons, icons or images? If not, reorganize and test. [needs brand data: page traffic and conversion]
7. If a page has a stock photo of a person near a button, test making the person look toward the button.

### 3.10 Review positive and negative framing on key pages [2c6f4cda6b12] [19bf368e3817]
1. Scan key pages for negative words: don't, never, avoid, banish, refuse, get rid of, but.
2. If negative is intentional, keep it.
3. If negative is unnecessary and positive might do better, revise only if the page's conversion rate is low or its bounce or exit rate is high. [needs brand data: page conversion, bounce and exit rates]
4. If negative messages are working, leave them ("If it ain't broke, don't fix it").
5. Test both framings when choosing.

### 3.11 Low-budget market tests (Tim Ferriss) [45fdf8131aa8]
1. To pick a name, buy PPC ads with different candidate titles; use the winning ad's title.
2. To pick visual creative, place printed options among real products where shoppers browse (Ferriss put covers on other books in a bookstore) and see which people gravitate to.
Note: the card's ecommerce translation (test product or collection names in ads, expose hero creative variants to traffic) is the card's own extension, not in the source.

### 3.12 Drafting and editing [753e79e5c3d8]
1. Start with 10x more information than you need.
2. Write it all (Caples: if you need 1,000 words, write 2,000).
3. Then trim hard. The author treats editing as just as important as writing: "Write throughout the night... But then spend the day editing."

## 5. Diagnostics and checks

- **Bland headline check.** How: read the headline and ask if any company in the category could use it. Good: it names the segment or its specific want (designers, students, hotels, "the real estate obsessed"). Bad: generic lines like "Connect with people near you" for a campus-only service, or a women-focused finance product that never says "women". Next: make it more specific; rewrite for 20% to 35% of visitors. The more neutral and bland the copy, the more likely no segment is targeted. [ab79ca8e0db3] [52564e4cb23c]
- **Wordsmithing check.** How: trace each message to research (customer words, keywords, pains, motivations). Good: every message comes from customers. Bad: the team loves it but it came from why you built the product. Next: go back to research. [dd20f7934ee1]
- **Feature vs benefit check.** How: for each bullet ask "what does the buyer feel or gain?" Good: an outcome for a named buyer. Bad: a restated spec or a description of the feature's purpose. Next: rewrite per segment, or cut the feature if no benefit exists. [70aaf0b3735c]
- **Support check.** How: look at each feature and benefit on the page. Good: every feature has a benefit, every benefit has a feature or support point (screenshot, sign, demo). Bad: bare claims. Next: add the pairing or a visual proof. [d5db0dddac4a] [61d04467e873]
- **Claim check.** How: list factual claims. Good: each has a source link. Bad: unsourced stats. Next: add the source or cut. [fb5fb47770a9]
- **Motivation check.** How: does the home page and each primary flow page reflect why the visitor came? Good: a line that mirrors a deep motivation, backed by images and testimonials. Bad: only a discount, a limited-time offer or the value proposition. Next: write a motivation line; if the category is already marketed, add a reminder callout below the hero. [fb5fb47770a9]
- **Offer clarity check.** How: read the offer and ask 3 questions: what exactly is offered or reduced, what is the qualifying amount or condition, which product or account it applies to. Good: 1 plain statement with all 3. Bad: "reduced fees for keeping a certain amount". Next: rewrite as 1 concrete line. [690303793634]
- **Negative image check.** How: does the copy build a vivid negative scene and then place the brand in it? Good: the solution fully removes the weight, or the negative is only hinted. Bad: brand sits beside a heavy negative image it cannot erase. Next: soften to a hint. [2c6f4cda6b12]
- **Negative framing performance check.** How: page conversion, bounce and exit rates. Good: negative copy converting; leave it. Bad: low conversion or high bounce/exit with unnecessary negativity. Next: test a positive version. The source gives no numeric thresholds. [2c6f4cda6b12] [needs brand data: page metrics and a baseline]
- **Hierarchy and layout check.** How: on a high-traffic page, find the messages you most want seen. Good: they sit near buttons, icons or images. Bad: buried away from eye-catching elements. Next: reorganize and test; test gaze direction of any person photo near a button. [e374cf034894]
- **Believability check.** How: flag lines that sound almost too good. Good: nearly unbelievable but possible, with clear examples on the same page. Bad: impossible to believe, or bold with no support. Next: add examples or tone down. [6b829c0c6b03]
- **Over-explanation check.** How: look for lines that justify ordinary statements. Good: ordinary claims stated plainly. Bad: every line defended. Next: cut. Bold claims still need support. [10a92744aa71]
- **Research validation check.** How: review survey notes. Good: surprising and repeated findings are highlighted. Bad: notes only confirm what you already believed. Next: re-read for new information. [7a372d0590a0]

## 7. Examples and case results

All are examples, not promises. Several are author opinions about sites as they looked in 2011.

- **Page99Test messaging strategy (1 paragraph)**: copy is short, friendly and to the point; assumes visitors want to read and rate book excerpts, not web copy; words kept to a minimum and, when used, clear, powerful and memorable; where a screenshot, press logo, button, video demo or icon can be used, it replaces copy without losing clarity. [20fa6900506f]
- **Teresa (JennyCraig.com)**: 39, 65 pounds overweight, says she wants weight-loss info. Real motivations: prove herself against the skinny neighbor, attract an ideal partner, earn her cranky mother's respect, love what she sees in the dressing room mirror. Do not write "Become the Size Zero You See in Magazines". Write "Embrace the Woman in the Mirror", with a photo of 2 friends leaving a dressing room smiling, testimonials from real women who love shopping now, and video and print case studies. [fb5fb47770a9] [web check: current ad platform policies on weight loss and body image before using this pattern in ads]
- **Personal finance callouts below the hero**: "Americans who use software to manage their money sleep better at night." [fb5fb47770a9]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about

1. Jumping into formatting (bullets, headlines, buttons) before you have messages. [20fa6900506f]
2. Choosing words because you "like the sound of" them. [20fa6900506f]
3. Paying a writer to wordsmith your internal message; loving copy that then fails. [dd20f7934ee1]
4. Asking customers to approve copy. [dd20f7934ee1]
5. Hiring a consulting firm for a focus group; focus groups invite groupthink, where 1 dominant person sways the rest. [dd20f7934ee1]
6. Spamming forums with a launch and leaving. [dd20f7934ee1]
7. Using research to validate assumptions instead of finding new information. [7a372d0590a0] [0c27d9b3a340]
8. Assuming your market (for example tech early adopters) without research. [6c4ec380b27a]
9. Writing the motivation bluntly ("Become the Size Zero..."). [fb5fb47770a9]
10. Treating a discount, a limited-time offer or the value proposition as motivation. [fb5fb47770a9]
11. Making unsupported claims. [fb5fb47770a9]
12. Neutralizing the site into a "beige blob" to please everyone. [52564e4cb23c]
13. Generic headlines that could belong to any company; losing focus under deadlines, closeness to the project, or investor worries. [ab79ca8e0db3]
14. Leaving small features out of the feature list. [7f5165a57e7a]
15. Toying with words while building the positioning document. [7f5165a57e7a]
16. Calling a feature's purpose its benefit ("those people quickly lose friends"). [70aaf0b3735c]

18. Forgetting benefits of how you do what you do (values, design). [7f5165a57e7a]
19. Summarizing to fit space, which strips specifics. [d5db0dddac4a]
20. Always leading with the benefit by rule. [d5db0dddac4a]
21. Banning negative words by reflex; or creating a negative image and attaching your brand to it. [19bf368e3817] [2c6f4cda6b12]
22. Rewriting negative copy that already works. [2c6f4cda6b12]
23. Thinking keyword research only helps SEO. [936e1b2b613f]
24. Thinking you already know your competitors; big companies lose share this way. [a3ddc92dd001]
25. Reading competitor message frequency as proof the message works. [a3ddc92dd001]
26. Laying out messages strictly top to bottom in rank order instead of where users look. [e374cf034894]
27. Not writing the hierarchy down, then drifting when you return weeks later. [e374cf034894]

## 9. Caveats, conflicts and what is missing

- **Written for 2011 software startups, not stores.** Examples are SaaS, apps and web services. Launch waves assume beta sign-ups and tech press. The method transfers; the examples need translating. Several cards add ecommerce translations the source did not give: shipping thresholds, bundle discounts and subscription terms [690303793634]; "cheapest / fastest / specialist / bundle" positions [0e8918a800e5]; testing product and collection names in ads [45fdf8131aa8]; 2-field checkout and instant demo ideas [e5294ffc5f69]; "table stakes" labels [95690fe6cfee]. Treat these as the card writer's extensions.
- **Card claims not in the text.** Card [17b8f4f12a1d] lists "phrases that appear in reviews" as a language source; the book never mentions reviews. Card [2c6f4cda6b12] says negative framings "often capture attention more sharply"; the book says negative "can be very powerful" but "doesn't mean negative is always better". Card [ab79ca8e0db3] adds that broad hooks may suit demand capture ads; the book does not say this. Card [19bf368e3817] adds shopper-type matching for framing; the book links shopper types only to benefit vs feature leads.
- **Self-tension the author admits.** Tip 4 (make copy believable, back bold lines with examples) and tip 5 (customers want a magic button and will believe you have it) pull in different directions; she calls tip 5 "seemingly contrary". Tip 2 (you do not need to explain every statement) sits against "just because you say it, doesn't make it true, prove it". The workable reading, which the cards also take: bold claims need proof on the page, ordinary claims do not need a defense.
- **Own-rule break.** She says to back every claim, yet her finance callouts include unsourced stats (30% of Americans, $1,400 on coffee; money as the #1 divorce cause).
- **Numbers are illustrations.** The 2% big-company conversion rate, the 10% and 5% segment math, the 100x keyword gap and the "extra 5%" gaze upside are unsourced or hypothetical. The 80% pain-point figure is a citation of 1 book. The Fidelity and news-campaign results are single cases with no baseline, sample or time control.
- **Thin on how to test.** She says "test it" often (favor/please, framing, layout, gaze) but gives no sample sizes, durations, success metrics or thresholds. "Low" conversion and "high" bounce/exit have no numbers. [needs brand data: baseline conversion, bounce and exit rates per page]
- **Segment PPC tests are dated.** Targeting Google ads "to each segment" by demographics and interests has changed a lot since 2011. [web check: current Google Ads audience and demographic targeting options] On broad-targeting ad platforms, segment tests now run mainly through different creative and copy rather than targeting settings.
- **Health and body-image copy.** The Teresa example and the motivation list ("never feeling hungry again", "becoming the ultimate lover", "loving what they see in the mirror") touch areas where ad platforms restrict copy. [web check: current Meta and Google policies on weight loss, body image and personal attributes]
- **"Message match" wording.** Her strategy principle says to favor "the message match[ing] the medium at an exact point of need" over "consistency" or "matching messages". This can read as against the usual ad-to-landing-page message match advice. She does not expand on it, so do not treat it as a rule against keeping ad and page promises aligned.
- **Competitor research use.** She uses competitor pages to learn expectations, white noise and gaps. She does not tell you to copy competitor wording. ecomOS rules also treat competitor ads as signals only.
- **Scope label.** The library header files this under 10-MCR (paid social creative), while the Drive folder is 11-ADC (copywriting after the 24 Sep 2026 split). Content is web copy and messaging strategy; its closest use in ecomOS is pre-writing research for ad copy, PDPs and landing pages.
- **Missing:** how to run or code interviews, sample survey questions, how to weigh surprising vs repeated findings, how to write a value proposition (deferred to later books), formatting (Book 2), long-form sales letters (Book 5), any mobile or ad-platform specifics, and any measurement plan. The book's diagrams and completed audit slides are lost in extraction.
