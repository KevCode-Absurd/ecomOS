# Building High Performance Teams: How-To Tips from Wharton Fellow and Author

credibility: Mario Musa, co-author (with Derek Newberry and Madeline Boyer) of "Committed Teams: Three Steps to Inspiring Passion and Performance." Evidence base: three years running Wharton's Executive Development Program (EDP), where about 60 executives per cohort form teams and compete for two weeks in a realistic business simulation. This is qualitative field observation of executive simulation teams, not a controlled study and not ecommerce-specific data.

## 1. What this source is for
Gives a simple, repeatable framework for how any team (including a store's ops, merchandising or creative team) should get organized and stay organized: agree goals/roles/norms explicitly, check in on them regularly, and close the gap between what the team says it will do and what it actually does. Also gives guidance on when to use a team at all versus assigning work to one person, and on building psychological safety so real problems surface.

## 2. Principles
1. High-performing teams commit explicitly to three foundations before doing task work: goals, roles, and norms. The source says this must be an explicit, "really good conversation," not assumed. This is the first step of the "3x3" framework (3 foundations x 3 steps). [d35411c91008]
2. Teams naturally drift from their agreed goals/roles/norms over time because people sit on multiple teams and projects with outside commitments; drift is called "natural" and "almost inevitable." Condition: applies to any team with divided attention, which the source treats as the normal case. [d35411c91008]
3. Teams must periodically check in: revisit the original commitments and ask "what do we want to do, how do we want to work together, how do we share information and make decisions, are we still committed?" [d35411c91008][352063f0fa58]
4. The most effective way to close the "saying-doing gap" (between stated intent and actual behavior) is small, specific steps, not big overhauls. Condition: applies once a gap is diagnosed at check-in. Evidence: Musa's stated finding from the EDP observations, not a measured comparison. [0396231dd3a9]
5. Closing a gap works better when the team also engineers the surrounding environment to support the new behavior, and adopts "realistic optimism" — thinking ahead about what could block the plan — rather than pure optimism. [0396231dd3a9]
6. The 3x3 loop (Commit -> Check-in -> Close) is iterative and continuous, not a one-time onboarding exercise. [d35411c91008]
7. Goals, roles and norms should be kept few and simple. Musa: teams "typically get overwhelmed" because they try to do too much or set goals that are too big. Condition: applies at goal-setting time and is also the reason drift-detection stays workable later. [352063f0fa58][712acd79637a]
8. Better check-in conversations come from three guidelines: (a) name and discuss style differences (extroversion/introversion, comfort with conflict); (b) build trust mainly through one-on-one dialogue, since group dynamics get complex; (c) keep each conversation's agenda narrow. [712acd79637a]
9. Not all work belongs to a team. Work needing specialized expertise, highly creative work, or work under real time pressure is usually better done by one individual, because "individuals tend to be more efficient than groups" in those cases. Team structure is for problems needing multiple perspectives or multiple kinds of expertise. [9f7764e0e51b]
10. Three recurring team failure modes, per the source's observation of ~100 teams: (a) the "great person theory" — over-relying on one dominant-personality or strong-vision individual, called "almost always a big mistake"; (b) focusing on the plan and not on how to execute and organize people around it; (c) skipping the roles conversation, so a good goals discussion never turns into "who does what," which means effort isn't maximized. [12ad1d771f2c]
11. Psychological safety — people feeling safe to share what they actually think — is, in Musa's words, the single thing he would focus on if he could pick only one. Default human behavior is withholding: "well over half the time people are not sharing what they are thinking and feeling," per research he cites without a named study. Built through trust, one-on-one conversations, listening, and empathy. [c94641818f5e]
12. Speed of team process should match task type, borrowing Kahneman's fast/slow framing: go fast and skip heavy process when speed matters; slow down and be deliberate about team formation and conversation when the decision needs multiple perspectives. [ab649028be47]
13. There is no fixed ratio of check-in time to working time. Musa gives the "consultant answer": it depends on the team's appetite for process. What matters is that check-ins happen at all, with judgment on how much time to spend. [d35411c91008][ab649028be47]

## 3. Procedures
The source gives a framework and guidelines, not a fixed operational checklist. The closest thing to a procedure is the 3x3 loop itself:

1. Commit: hold an explicit, dedicated conversation covering three things together: goals (what we want to achieve), roles (who does what), and norms (how we share information and make decisions).
2. Check-in (recurring, cadence not specified): revisit the same three foundations. Ask directly whether the team is still committed to them and whether the original working style still holds.
3. Close: name the saying-doing gap found in step 2. Pick one small, specific change (not a broad reform). Adjust the surrounding environment so that change is easier to make. Apply "realistic optimism": think ahead about what will block the change and plan around it.
4. Return to step 2. Repeat indefinitely; the source explicitly frames this as continuous, not a one-time setup.

For running check-in conversations specifically, apply these sub-steps (from principle 8): discuss communication styles as a team; hold one-on-ones in addition to group meetings to build trust; and cap the agenda to a few topics.

The source gives no step-by-step method for the Commit conversation itself, no cadence formula for Check-in, and no worked example of writing goals/roles/norms documents. It is a conversational framework; how to structure the conversation is left to the reader's judgment.

## 5. Diagnostics and checks
- To detect drift: at check-in, compare current goals/roles/norms understanding against what was originally agreed. Ask: are we still committed? Does our working style still hold? [d35411c91008]
- To diagnose a saying-doing gap: compare what the team said it would do against what it is actually doing day to day. [0396231dd3a9]
- To decide team vs. individual for a task: check whether the task needs specialized expertise, is highly creative, or is time-critical (assign to one person) versus whether it needs multiple perspectives or multiple kinds of expertise (assign to a team). [9f7764e0e51b]
- To decide fast vs. slow process: check whether speed is the priority (go fast, skip process) or whether the decision needs multiple perspectives pulled in (go slow, be deliberate). [ab649028be47]
- To check for psychological safety problems: notice if meetings produce agreement or silence but real problems (in an ecommerce context: stockouts, weak creative, margin issues) only surface later. Musa's default assumption is that most people are withholding unless the environment says otherwise. [c94641818f5e]
- To check for the three common failure modes: look for (1) one dominant person driving everything, (2) a team that has a plan but no discussion of who executes what, (3) a goals discussion that ended with "let's go do it" and no explicit role assignment. [12ad1d771f2c]

## 8. Mistakes the source warns about
1. The "great person theory": relying too much on one person with a strong vision or dominant personality who seems to be able to "do it all." Called "almost always a big mistake." [12ad1d771f2c]
2. Focusing on the plan itself and not on how to execute it or organize people around it. [12ad1d771f2c]
3. Skipping the roles conversation: agreeing on goals, saying "let's go do it," and never assigning who does what — this means the team's effort is not maximized. [12ad1d771f2c]
4. Assigning tasks to a group that should have gone to an individual (specialized-expertise, highly creative, or time-critical work), which wastes the efficiency an individual would have had. [9f7764e0e51b]
5. Assuming silence in meetings means agreement, when the default is that people withhold their real views more than half the time. [c94641818f5e]
6. Letting goals or agenda topics get too big or too numerous, which overwhelms the team. [352063f0fa58][712acd79637a]
7. Treating team-building as a one-time onboarding event rather than a continuous loop that must be repeated. [d35411c91008]
