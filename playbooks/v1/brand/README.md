## Define evidence-led brand positioning
Workflow ID: `brand.position-brand`

**Deliverable:** Positioning brief and proof requirements

### Proposed decision sequence
1. Identify the customer circumstance and credible product advantage
2. Separate supported benefits from speculative psychology
3. Write a specific positioning statement with reasons to believe

#### Position a local business against the giant's size weakness, then make operations match the promise
Card: `6355cdae1a6b79393b2e98b4` · Source: Positioning_ The Battle for Your Mind_ The Battle for Your Mind (1).docx · Section: 5

United Jersey became the 'fast-moving bank' by exploiting big New York banks' slow service and backing the ad promise with seven concrete operational commitments.

Applies when: Positioning a smaller store or local brand against a large national competitor whose weakness is slowness or bureaucracy.

- Study the competition, not yourself, to find the position, as Tylenol did by studying aspirin's problems rather than its own product.
- Target the competitors who actually occupy the prospect's mind, not smaller rivals nobody thinks about — the source chose the big New York banks because 'positioning is always a question of dealing with what's in the mind.'
- Name the leader's real weakness: large size means slow reaction time, similar to the Avis 'shorter line' claim.
- Before advertising the promise, change operations to deliver it. United Jersey made seven commitments: decentralized decisions (a lending team can approve a $10 million loan, meeting daily), cross-training staff so questions aren't referred elsewhere, statewide ATM commitment, six-times-daily lock box pickups, FACT terminals for fast merchant approvals, responsiveness on rates and products, and a Princeton headquarters central to all customers.
- Only then run the promise-based advertising. TV spots contrasted United Jersey bankers with the fictional 'Lethargic National Bank,' and print used headlines like 'Haste makes money' and 'Banks shouldn't hold up people.'
- Reinforce internally with 'Time Is Money' desk signs for officers.
- Track awareness and attitude, not just sales. The source reports unaided awareness nearly tripled in just over a year and one officer noted line officers were visibly striving to live up to the promise.
- **Condition:** The reported earnings increase of $30 million (26%) is a specific case result, not a general expectation.
- **Condition:** The source's essence criterion is that a good position transcends every aspect of the company — if operations can't match the ad, the strategy fails.

#### Decide whether a tagline earns a place in the store's messaging
Card: `612c157ef1c5cb0236f6d707` · Source: Designing Brand Identity.docx · Section: 1

Taglines are shorthand for the brand promise; keep one only if it is short, differentiated, memorable and consistent with actual delivery.

Applies when: When writing or reviewing a store's tagline, homepage hero line, packaging promise or recurring campaign line.

- Check the essentials: short, differentiated from competitors, unique, captures brand essence and positioning, easy to say and remember, no negative connotations, displayed in a small font, protectable and trademarkable, and evokes an emotional response.
- Classify the tagline type to ensure fit: imperative (commands action, usually starts with a verb — Nike 'Just do it', TOMS 'One for One'); descriptive (states the service, product or promise — BMW 'The ultimate driving machine'); superlative (positions as best in class, e.g. Budweiser 'King of beers'); provocative (thought-provoking, often a question — eBay 'Where else?'); or specific (reveals the business category).
- Confirm the tagline is a brand promise you can actually deliver — the source cites Target's 'Expect More, Pay Less' as a promise to consumers.
- Plan for long use and frequent, consistent repetition; the source says the best taglines have a long life and transcend changes in the marketplace and lifestyle.
- Expect taglines to have shorter life spans than logos historically, but do not use that as license to change them casually — repetition is what builds meaning.
- **Condition:** Tagline examples are illustrative, not evidence that a similar line will lift sales.
- **Condition:** Protectability matters; verify trademark status if the line is central to your packaging or campaigns.

#### Map positioning on an extreme emotional XY grid
Card: `cdddb1268ba489e48a1f390e` · Source: This Is Marketing_ You Can’t Be Seen Until You Learn to Se (1).docx · Section: 0

Use unconventional emotional axes (not price vs features) to find a defensible positioning space.

Applies when: Choosing how to differentiate your store or product line in a crowded category.

- Pick two extreme emotional attributes your category actually competes on — for example speed vs security, or status vs convenience.
- Plot competitors on the two axes to see where the crowded middle sits; the middle is usually where undifferentiated brands die.
- Choose a corner position that is extreme on at least one axis rather than a balanced compromise.
- Define the brand promise so it clearly attracts the customers who want that extreme and repels the ones who don't.
- Use the chosen position to guide merchandising, PDP messaging, and which product attributes you lead with — not just ads.
- Revisit the grid when you add categories; an extreme position can carry across lines or may need its own grid.
- **Condition:** Source uses these as strategic frames, not quantified scores; the axes are chosen judgment calls, not measured data.
- **Condition:** An extreme position intentionally repels some buyers; that exclusion is the point, not a flaw.

#### Keep the brand speaking with one voice across channels
Card: `304f64fb3d93907eac8d56dc` · Source: Designing Brand Identity.docx · Section: 1

'Stay on message' means the same distinctive voice and unified message across web, social, sales conversation and leadership communication, centered on the customer.

Applies when: When assigning copy across store pages, emails, packaging inserts and social posts, or fixing inconsistency after multiple teams and freelancers have written content.

- Inventory the language assets the source lists, so nothing is left to improvisation: nomenclature (formal and informal company name, taglines, descriptors, product/process/service/division names), brand essence, mission and vision statements, value propositions, key messages, guiding principles, customer pledges, vocabulary, history, boilerplate and elevator speech.
- Standardize voice and tone rules: headline style, punctuation, capitalization, emphasis, and the standard for accuracy, clarity and consistency.
- Cover information mechanics too: calls to action, phone numbers, URLs, email signatures, voicemail, abbreviations, titles, addresses and directions.
- Apply the same discipline across touchpoints the source enumerates: websites and blogs, news releases, FAQs, press kits, brochures, shareholder communications, call-center and sales scripts, presentations, announcements, blast emails, ad campaigns, direct mail, product directions, signage and apps.
- Write so the message is vital, straightforward, eloquent and substantive, and accessible to all customers; cut through hype and clutter.
- Accept that unified messaging requires buy-in at all levels and long-term commitment — repetition is what makes a message memorable.
- **Condition:** Consistency requires ongoing governance; one style guide alone does not enforce it.
- **Condition:** The source notes a busy reader may spend only minutes, so prioritize clarity and brevity over completeness in any single message.

#### Strip Technical Jargon from Product and Marketing Copy
Card: `3cac34e1c33d609532448226` · Source: Building a Brand – Redesigning a Business Start to Finish.docx · Section: 0

Write a brand voice that is human, confident and clear even in a technical product category

Applies when: Writing product descriptions, specs pages, emails or ads for a technically complex product where copy has drifted into insider language

- Set the target balance explicitly. The source's stated goal was professional but not corporate, friendly but not fake, and it describes this as a difficult balance to strike — write the two poles down as a copy review test.
- Assume your buyer is not a specialist. Even though the keyboard world is full of technical jargon, the source makes a conscious effort to use clear, simple language anyone can understand, on the reasoning that newcomers and enthusiasts both need to buy.
- Draft core messages collaboratively in a shared document and refine them over multiple passes. Mode used a Notion document to write and refine core messages so word choices felt authentic rather than imposed.
- Ban marketing buzzwords and favor honest, straightforward communication. Keep the emotional promise concrete — the source's themes were 'making technology feel more human and inviting' and 'creating joy in everyday experiences', expressed plainly rather than as abstract brandspeak.
- Produce messaging samples attached to specific placements (website, marketing) rather than a general tone document, and keep a separate set of thought-starter 'brand expressions' showing the voice applied across physical and digital surfaces for future launches and events.
- **Condition:** Voice work was done by an internal team with an outside facilitator; a solo operator should expect more drafting rounds to reach the same clarity.
- **Condition:** Simplifying does not mean removing necessary specs — the source keeps deep technical information available for research-heavy buyers and reserves simple language for messaging.

#### Use semiotics and vernacular to signal tribe belonging
Card: `b1ae1eab635d788a376bacb9` · Source: This Is Marketing_ You Can’t Be Seen Until You Learn to Se (1).docx · Section: 0

Deliberate symbols, category vernacular, and visual cues attract the right tribe and filter out the wrong people.

Applies when: Designing PDP imagery, packaging, tone of voice, or ad creative that should signal who the product is for.

- Identify the trusted visual and verbal patterns your target tribe already recognizes in the category (source example draws on cues like driving gloves with holes or car wheel flares).
- Embed those recognizable signifiers in your product photography, copy, and design rather than inventing unfamiliar signals.
- Use intentional signals to attract insiders and repel outsiders — the source cites spam with deliberate bad grammar as a filter that selects only the gullible.
- Match tone and vernacular to the tribe's own language so the store feels like 'people like us' rather than an outsider.
- Audit existing assets for signals that attract the wrong audience or dilute the tribe signal.
- Keep symbols coherent across PDP, email, packaging, and ads so the signal compounds.
- **Condition:** Semiotic choices are interpretive and category-specific; what signals insider status in one tribe reads as noise or offense in another.
- **Condition:** Over-filtering can shrink your addressable audience — the source treats this as a feature, but confirm demand exists.

#### Consistency is a brand promise; authenticity is for amateurs
Card: `e1a3cf352d92198af6b44a65` · Source: Marketing Godfather_ How To Build An Audience That Buys (Best Hour You’ll Spend Today!) _ Seth Godin.docx · Section: 0

Deliver the promised version of your brand on every touchpoint regardless of how you feel, because customers bought the promise, not your mood.

Applies when: Customer service standards, shipping and fulfillment consistency, and repeat-touchpoint design such as subscriptions, email and support responses.

- Adopt the source's rule: 'authenticity is for amateurs, consistency is for professionals' — customers don't want your authentic bad day, they want the version of you that you promised them.
- Apply the hotel illustration: a doorman having an authentically bad day is not what a guest wants; a messy room because the housekeeper was authentic is not an acceptable service outcome.
- For subscriptions and replenishment: the performer's example is that if you're sick of playing your hit, don't tour — or if you tour, you give the audience what they signed up for instead of switching to classical music unannounced.
- Set explicit brand promises (delivery time, packaging, response time, product quality) and hold them even when it's inconvenient, because that is what a brand is.
- Keep the source's boundary: don't do work you're not proud of or work that burns you out — consistency is not the same as enduring a bad business.
- **Condition:** The source addresses pushback directly: we have been non-authentic since age two (driving 20mph with cars ahead), so this is not a demand to fake your whole identity — it is a service standard.
- **Condition:** This is a strong norm stated by the source; there are legitimate brand positions built on raw authenticity, and the choice is situational.

#### Apply the cigarette fMRI finding to replenishment and cue design
Card: `b50e436ee0a24664b37659b5` · Source: BUYOLOGY MARTIN LINDSTROM (1).docx · Section: 3

Logo-free brand-associated imagery triggered more craving activity than explicit product or logo imagery among smokers.

Applies when: Replenishment triggers, retention email design, or subscription reactivation campaigns for habitual or consumable products.

- The study showed that logo-free cues associated with a cigarette brand — a red Ferrari, cowboys, a sunset — produced more activity in reward/craving regions than logos or pack images did. Lindstrom reports the effect was consistent for both Marlboro and Camel smokers and lasted under five seconds of exposure.
- Translate to your category: identify sensory or visual cues your regular buyers already associate with using your product (the mug, the scent, the setting, the routine).
- Use those cue-only assets in replenishment emails and push notifications ('time to restock', 'your morning') rather than pasting a product logo or price banner on every message.
- Keep cues consistent over time so they accumulate association — the source describes years of tobacco marketing building the link between race imagery and the cigarette brands before the association took hold.
- For repeated-purchase conversion, test cue-only creative against logo-plus-product creative on reorder rate, not on initial open rate.
- **Condition:** The craving-region fMRI findings are specific to nicotine-dependent smokers viewing tobacco cues; craving is not the same mechanism as ordinary purchase intent.
- **Condition:** Cross-category association building takes years of consistent spend; a single campaign will not produce the same effect.
- **Condition:** Lindstrom's finding is presented as a research result within his own lab, not an industry benchmark.
